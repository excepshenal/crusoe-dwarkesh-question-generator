## One-Paragraph Bio

Richard Lee Rhodes (born July 4, 1937, in Kansas City) is an American journalist, historian, and author of more than two dozen books, best known for *The Making of the Atomic Bomb* (1986), which won the Pulitzer Prize, the National Book Award, and the National Book Critics Circle Award. Orphaned in effect by his mother's suicide thirteen months after his birth and an abusive stepmother, he was raised partly at a home for boys in Missouri before earning a scholarship to Yale (B.A. cum laude, 1959). He worked as a magazine writer and book editor (Hallmark Cards, *Newsweek*, contributing editor at *Harper's*, *Playboy*, and *Rolling Stone*) before building a career as a freelance narrative nonfiction writer. Rhodes is best known for a four-volume history of the nuclear age, but his range spans biography, criminology, the Holocaust, energy, and four novels. He is a longtime advocate of both nuclear disarmament and the expansion of nuclear power for combating climate change, and an affiliate of Stanford's Center for International Security and Cooperation (CISAC).

## Major Works and Contributions

### The Nuclear Quartet

**The Making of the Atomic Bomb** (Simon & Schuster, 1986; roughly 900 pages). This is Rhodes's defining work. It narrates the story of nuclear weapons from the early-twentieth-century discovery of the atomic nucleus and nuclear fission, through the Manhattan Project, to the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, set against the rise of fascism and antisemitism in Europe. A signature scene is **Leo Szilard's chain-reaction epiphany**: on September 12, 1933, waiting at a London stoplight on Southampton Row near the British Museum, Szilard conceived how a nuclear chain reaction might be made to work — an idea Rhodes ties to H.G. Wells's notion of an "Open Conspiracy." The book renders biographical portraits of the physicists (Niels Bohr, Enrico Fermi, J. Robert Oppenheimer, General Leslie Groves, and others) and explains complex physics in lay terms. Rhodes spent roughly five years researching, drawing on declassified documents (including FBI files), Japanese survivor accounts, scientist-statesman correspondence, and oral histories from some twenty-one Manhattan Project participants. A central intellectual thread is Rhodes's adoption of **Bohr's idea of "complementarity"**: the bomb is simultaneously a weapon of mass destruction and a peace-forcing instrument that, by making national secrecy and traditional warfare obsolete, pushes the world toward an "open" international order. The book won the **1987 National Book Award for Nonfiction**, the **1987 National Book Critics Circle Award**, and the **1988 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction**. Nobel laureate I.I. Rabi called it "an epic worthy of Milton."

**Dark Sun: The Making of the Hydrogen Bomb** (Simon & Schuster, 1995; part of the Sloan Technology Series; ~731 pages). A finalist for the 1996 Pulitzer Prize. The book pairs the technical story of the thermonuclear weapon with the story of Soviet atomic espionage. Rhodes argues the H-bomb was driven heavily by domestic politics and nuclear hawks — and in his epilogue treats it as "unnecessary." He details the **Teller-Ulam design**: Stanislaw Ulam's late-1950 insight about using compression from the fission primary, and Edward Teller's addition of radiation compression of the fusion fuel (the breakthrough collaboration is dated to March 1951). Rhodes is notably critical of Teller, arguing that Teller — "the self-styled father of the H-bomb" — actually *retarded* American progress by insisting on a megaton-scale "Super." On espionage, he documents **Klaus Fuchs** (who passed implosion details and a full description of the Fat Man plutonium bomb; confessed January 1950, sentenced to 14 years), **Julius and Ethel Rosenberg** (executed June 19, 1953), and **Theodore Hall** (recruited at Los Alamos at age 18). The book covers the first U.S. thermonuclear test, "Ivy Mike" (November 1, 1952, ~10.4 megatons), and the Soviet program under Igor Kurchatov, including Andrei Sakharov's "Layer Cake" design and the first Soviet test of August 29, 1949.

**Arsenals of Folly: The Making of the Nuclear Arms Race** (Knopf, 2007). The third volume argues that the four-decade superpower arms race was wasteful, irrational, and dangerous — arsenals expanded "beyond all logic." It documents how Reagan's early-1980s buildup deepened Soviet fears (Yuri Andropov believed Reagan might be preparing a first strike), and how NATO's autumn 1983 "Able Archer" exercise nearly triggered a Soviet defensive launch. Its narrative climax is the **1986 Reykjavik summit**, where Reagan and Gorbachev came close to agreeing to abolish nuclear weapons before the deal collapsed over the Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars"); Rhodes assigns a derailing role to hardliner Richard Perle.

**The Twilight of the Bombs: Recent Challenges, New Dangers, and the Prospects for a World Without Nuclear Weapons** (Knopf, 2010). The final volume covers roughly 1990–2010: the securing of nuclear materials after the Soviet collapse, South Africa's voluntary disarmament, the Indian and Pakistani tests, North Korea, and the threat of nuclear terrorism. A pointed chapter argues that Israel's 1981 bombing of Iraq's Osirak reactor *accelerated* rather than halted Saddam Hussein's program. Rhodes closes by arguing that abolishing nuclear weapons is "both necessary and feasible," drawing an analogy to public-health disease-eradication campaigns. (He also wrote a shorter work, *Nuclear Renewal*, in 1993.)

### Beyond Nuclear History

Rhodes's non-nuclear output is wide-ranging:

- **Why They Kill: The Discoveries of a Maverick Criminologist** (Knopf, 1999). A study of criminologist **Lonnie Athens**, who proposed **"violentization"** — a four-stage process (brutalization, belligerency, violent performances, virulency) by which people become violent.
- **Masters of Death: The SS-Einsatzgruppen and the Invention of the Holocaust** (Knopf, 2002). On the mobile SS killing squads that murdered more than 1.5 million people between 1941 and 1943 — "the Holocaust by bullets."
- **John James Audubon: The Making of an American** (Knopf, 2004); Rhodes also edited *The Audubon Reader* (2006).
- **Hedy's Folly: The Life and Breakthrough Inventions of Hedy Lamarr** (Doubleday, 2011), on her co-invention with George Antheil of a frequency-hopping spread-spectrum system (US Patent 2,292,387, 1942).
- **Hell and Good Company: The Spanish Civil War and the World It Made** (Simon & Schuster, 2015).
- **Energy: A Human History** (Simon & Schuster, 2018), a survey of energy transitions from wood to coal to oil to electricity to nuclear.
- **Scientist: E. O. Wilson: A Life in Nature** (Doubleday, 2021).

He has also published four novels and a memoir cluster including *A Hole in the World: An American Boyhood* (1990).

## The Substance of His Ideas

**The inevitability of the bomb.** Rhodes argues that once nuclear fission was understood, weaponization was essentially inevitable; scientists could not have secretly agreed to suppress it. World War II *accelerated* the American program (driven by fear that Germany had started first) but did not create the underlying trajectory.

**Bohr's complementarity and the "open world."** Rhodes's most distinctive thesis applies Bohr's quantum concept to geopolitics: the bomb is at once a weapon of annihilation and a forcing function for transparency and the end of major war. He recounts Bohr's wartime appeals to Roosevelt and Churchill to share nuclear knowledge (including with the USSR) to prevent a secret arms race — appeals Churchill rejected.

**Hiroshima and Nagasaki.** Rhodes leans toward the traditionalist position — that the bombings materially contributed to Japanese surrender — emphasizing the Japanese leadership's extraordinary resistance to capitulation. He reframes the atomic bombs as primarily fire-and-blast weapons rather than radiation weapons, pushing back against exaggerated radiation mythology, and contextualizes their destruction against the conventional firebombing of Tokyo. His emphasis places him closer to the traditionalist camp than to Gar Alperovitz's "atomic diplomacy" revisionism.

**Disarmament.** Rhodes is a committed advocate of deep reductions and eventual abolition. He praised the 1946 Acheson-Lilienthal Plan; argued that "the only morally justifiable level of world nuclear arsenals must be fewer than 100 15-kiloton weapons," grounding the argument in nuclear-winter science (even a regional India-Pakistan exchange would cause global cooling and mass starvation). A structural claim: the arms race has been driven substantially by domestic politics — the "weak on defense" charge wielded against Democratic presidents.

**Nuclear power.** A former skeptic turned advocate, motivated by climate change, Rhodes argues nuclear power is "easily the most promising single energy source" for the twenty-first century. He cites that nuclear power "prevented some 1.84 million deaths" from air pollution between 1971 and 2009 (Kharecha and Hansen), and high capacity factors (~92%). He attributes "radiation phobia" to the historical accident that nuclear power and nuclear weapons "were all born together," and argues proliferation from civilian power is a political choice, not a technological inevitability.

## Method, Influences, and Reception

Rhodes describes himself as a writer of "verity" rather than "nonfiction." He is a journalist by training, not a credentialed academic historian; held visiting fellowships at MIT and Harvard's history of science department. His work belongs to the literary-reportage tradition of John Hersey's *Hiroshima* (1946) and Robert Jungk's *Brighter Than a Thousand Suns* (1958). For *Dark Sun* he interviewed insiders including Edward Teller, J. Carson Mark, and Luis Alvarez; Hans Bethe gave that book a positive review. *Energy: A Human History* drew the sharpest pushback, with reviewers arguing its closing chapters read like advocacy for the nuclear industry.

## Notable Biographical Facts

Rhodes's childhood was severe (mother's suicide; abusive stepmother; placed at the Andrew Drumm Institute, a farm-home for boys in Independence, Missouri). He attended Yale on a full scholarship (1955–1959). Early career: *Newsweek*, Radio Free Europe, book-editing at Hallmark Cards (1962–1970). Honors include Guggenheim, Ford, and Sloan fellowships and a MacArthur grant under its Program on Peace and International Cooperation. He became an affiliate of Stanford's CISAC around 2004.
