## Broad research

### One-paragraph bio

Daniel Howard Yergin (born February 6, 1947, in Los Angeles) is an American economic historian, energy analyst, and author who has become arguably the most widely cited public interpreter of the global energy system. The son of a Hollywood Reporter editor and a sculptor, he was raised in Beverly Hills, earned a B.A. from Yale (1968, where he co-founded the campus magazine The New Journal) and a Ph.D. in international history from Trinity College, Cambridge (1970s), which he attended as a Marshall Scholar. He lectured at Harvard Business School and the Harvard Kennedy School into the mid-1980s and co-ran the Harvard Business School Energy Project. In 1982 he co-founded Cambridge Energy Research Associates (CERA) with Joseph (Jamey) Rosenfield; CERA was acquired by IHS Inc. in 2004, became part of IHS Markit in 2016, and folded into S&P Global with the 2022 merger, where Yergin is vice chairman. He chairs CERAWeek, the large annual energy conference held in Houston. His 1991 history of oil, *The Prize*, won the 1992 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction. He has served on the U.S. Secretary of Energy Advisory Board across multiple administrations, sits on the National Petroleum Council, and is a Brookings trustee. He is married to Georgetown Russia scholar Angela Stent.

### Major works

**Shattered Peace: The Origins of the Cold War and the National Security State (1977).** Yergin's first book, drawn partly from his Cambridge dissertation. It is a revisionist-leaning diplomatic history arguing that early Cold War U.S. policy hardened around what he called the "Riga axioms" (a deeply suspicious, ideological reading of Soviet intentions) at the expense of the more accommodationist "Yalta axioms," and that this shift institutionalized a permanent "national security state." This establishes his lifelong method: long-arc narrative history built on archival and interview material.

**Energy Future: Report of the Energy Project at the Harvard Business School (1979),** co-edited with Robert Stobaugh. An early policy intervention arguing, against the conventional wisdom of the late-1970s shortage panic, that conservation and energy efficiency were the cheapest and most available "source" of energy, and that solar/renewables deserved serious attention. This pro-efficiency strand recurs across his later work.

**The Prize: The Epic Quest for Oil, Money, and Power (1991).** His signature work, ~900 pages, a Pulitzer winner and the basis of an eight-part PBS/BBC documentary. Central argument: oil is not merely a commodity but the decisive strategic resource of the twentieth century, woven through its wars, empires, and economies. The narrative runs from Edwin Drake's 1859 Pennsylvania well and the rise and 1911 antitrust breakup of John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, through Winston Churchill's pre-WWI decision to convert the Royal Navy from coal to oil (the origin of Yergin's favorite energy-security maxim), the role of oil in both World Wars (Hitler's drive for Caucasus oil, the Japanese strike south after the 1941 U.S. embargo), the postwar Seven Sisters, the 1960 founding of OPEC, the 1973 embargo and 1979 shock, and the 1980s price collapse. Its thesis that oil access shapes geopolitics is the foundation everything else he writes rests on.

**The Commanding Heights: The Battle for the World Economy (1998),** co-authored with Joseph Stanislaw (also a PBS series). A history of the twentieth-century contest between state control and markets — the title is Lenin's phrase for the strategic sectors a state must hold. It traces the swing from Keynesian and socialist planning toward the market liberalization, privatization, and deregulation of the 1980s–90s (Thatcher, Reagan, the post-Soviet transitions, emerging-market reform). It reveals Yergin's broadly market-oriented disposition.

**The Quest: Energy, Security, and the Remaking of the Modern World (2011).** A sequel to *The Prize* extending the story past the Gulf War into the twenty-first century. It covers the post-Soviet scramble for Caspian oil, the rise of China and India as demand centers, the peak-oil debate (which he rejects — see below), the emergence of natural gas and the early shale story, and a substantial history of climate change as a political idea — tracing how it moved from a fringe scientific concern (he notes early-twentieth-century worry was as much about a coming ice age) to a central organizing issue, alongside the "rebirth of renewables" in wind, solar, and biofuels. He argues efficiency and eventual grid parity for renewables will reshape the system, but gradually.

**The New Map: Energy, Climate, and the Clash of Nations (2020, rev. 2021).** His most recent book, organizing the contemporary world around energy geopolitics. Its central claims: (1) the U.S. shale/fracking revolution turned the United States into the world's largest oil and gas producer and an "energy superpower," upending decades of assumed scarcity and dependence; (2) Russia under Putin uses gas exports (and pipelines like Nord Stream) as an instrument of leverage over Europe while pivoting east toward China; (3) China is converging with Russia against U.S. primacy and projecting power through the Belt and Road and especially the South China Sea (the "nine-dash line"), the world's most critical trade artery; (4) the Middle East remains fractured by Iran, jihadism, and sectarian conflict; and (5) climate concern is driving an energy transition whose pace, cost, and geopolitics are far more contested than its advocates assume.

### The substance of his ideas, with reasoning and counterarguments

**Energy security and the diversification doctrine.** Yergin's most durable framework descends from Churchill's 1913 line, which he quotes repeatedly: "Safety and certainty in oil lie in variety, and variety alone." In his Foreign Affairs essays ("Energy Security in the 1990s," 1988; "Ensuring Energy Security," 2006) and in his analysis of the 1973 crisis, he argues energy security rests on a handful of principles: diversification of supply sources and types; resilience and spare capacity (buffers like the Strategic Petroleum Reserve); transparency/information to prevent panic-buying; international cooperation to avoid competitive scrambles; and flexible markets rather than price controls. His reasoning is rooted in his own history of 1973: he stresses the embargo itself removed only about 9% of total world supply (roughly 14% of internationally traded oil), yet prices roughly quadrupled (from about $1.80 to $11.65 a barrel) because spare capacity had vanished — U.S. spare capacity disappeared after Texas lifted production limits in 1971 — and because panic and hoarding, not the physical shortfall, drove the shock. The policy lesson he draws is that markets plus buffers plus information manage scarcity better than allocation and controls. Critics on the left argue this framing underweights how market mechanisms concentrate power in incumbent producers and can leave consumers exposed; energy-justice critics argue "security" defined this way privileges supply continuity over distributional and environmental harm.

**The rejection of peak oil.** Yergin is one of the most prominent skeptics of geologist M. King Hubbert's "peak oil" thesis. In his 2011 Wall Street Journal essay "There Will Be Oil" (adapted from *The Quest*) he argues that Hubbert-style forecasts repeatedly fail because they treat reserves as a fixed geological stock and ignore economics and technology. His reasoning: higher prices and innovation continually convert "resources" into recoverable "reserves." He cites that in 2007–2009, for every barrel of oil produced worldwide, about 1.6 barrels of new reserves were added, and points to the world having supposedly "run out" of oil several times historically only to be rescued by new technology (deepwater Gulf of Mexico, 3D seismic, horizontal drilling, and ultimately shale). His preferred image is not a peak but an "undulating plateau" — production rising for decades, leveling off perhaps around mid-century, then declining gradually. The strongest counterargument, voiced at the time by peak-oil analysts such as those at The Oil Drum and by economists in the tradition of Herman Daly: stated reserves (especially OPEC's) are unverifiable "guesstimates" often inflated for political/quota reasons; conventional crude production was on a plateau for years despite record prices and new technology, suggesting flow rates, not ultimate volumes, are the binding constraint; technology and field-extension gains cannot continue indefinitely; and Yergin's optimism brackets out environmental cost (Daly faulted him for barely mentioning the climate and spill externalities of ever-more-marginal extraction). The shale boom largely vindicated Yergin on volumes through the 2010s, but the debate over whether he confuses recoverable resource with sustainable flow rate persists.

**The energy transition as "energy addition."** Yergin's most cited recent argument, developed in *The New Map* and sharpened in the 2025 Foreign Affairs essay "The Troubled Energy Transition: How to Find a Pragmatic Path Forward" (with Peter Orszag and Atul Arya), is that the shift away from fossil fuels will not be linear, fast, or cheap. His core empirical claim is historical: previous energy transitions took roughly a century and were really "energy additions" — coal did not eliminate wood, oil did not eliminate coal; each new source layered on top of the old (he notes whale oil is about the only fuel that actually disappeared). He argues the same is happening now: renewables are growing rapidly but on top of, not in place of, fossil fuels, so fossil-fuel share of primary energy has barely moved. He stacks several "realities" against rapid transition: the sheer scale of decarbonizing a roughly $115 trillion global economy in 25 years; the return of energy security as a priority after Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine; the mineral intensity of clean technology (e.g., an EV uses on the order of 2.5x the copper of a conventional car) colliding with Chinese dominance of mineral processing and rare earths; permitting timelines (he cites figures on the order of decades to open a new U.S. mine); and surging electricity demand from AI and data centers, which he argues will sustain natural gas demand. His pragmatic prescription: pursue affordability and reliability alongside emissions goals, speed permitting, integrate North American resources, and diversify critical-mineral supply chains away from China. The strongest counterargument — pressed in a Bloomberg Opinion critique and elsewhere — is that this "pragmatism" recycles incumbent talking points to argue, in effect, that the transition is not happening and cannot happen soon; critics contend that solar, batteries, and EVs are now on steep cost-decline and exponential-adoption curves that historical analogies to slow coal/oil transitions fail to capture, that "energy addition" describes the past rather than the policy-and-cost-driven present, and that framing the transition as merely "troubled" risks becoming self-fulfilling by dampening urgency.

**Tensions within his own views.** Yergin holds positions that sit in some tension. He is genuinely pro-efficiency and was early to take renewables seriously (*Energy Future*, *The Quest*), yet is consistently cast — and sometimes casts himself — as a skeptic of transition speed, which lets critics file him with the incumbent oil-and-gas interests that pay for CERAWeek and CERA's consulting. He emphasizes markets and the futility of forecasting fixed limits (his anti-peak-oil case), yet his transition argument leans heavily on the inertia of physical and political systems — i.e., on limits to how fast change can occur. He celebrates the shale revolution as an energy-security triumph and a geopolitical asset for the United States, while also documenting (in *The New Map*) how cheap, abundant hydrocarbons complicate decarbonization. He frames climate change as one of the defining issues of the age, yet his practical conclusions almost always counsel slower, more hydrocarbon-tolerant paths.

### Debates he engages and where he stands

- **Peak oil:** firmly skeptical; "undulating plateau," resource-as-economic-not-geological, technology and price as the decisive variables.
- **Energy security:** the diversification/resilience/information school; markets-plus-buffers over controls; he treats the 1973 and 2022 shocks as proof that security must remain a first-order concern even amid decarbonization.
- **Pace of the energy transition:** the central debate of his recent career — he is the leading articulator of the "slower, costlier, more complicated, and additive than advertised" position, against advocates of rapid, disruptive transition.
- **U.S.-China and great-power rivalry:** he sees a sharpening, energy-inflected great-power competition, with Russia pivoting toward China and energy/minerals as terrain of contest.

### Influences

Yergin's method — sweeping narrative history grounded in primary documents and elite interviews, aimed at a general audience — descends from his training as a Cambridge diplomatic historian. His framing of energy as the engine of modern political economy, and his fondness for the Churchill/Royal Navy episode as origin myth, are recurring touchstones. His market-versus-state interest (*Commanding Heights*) reflects the late-twentieth-century liberalization debates he chronicled. His co-authors and collaborators (Robert Stobaugh, Joseph Stanislaw, Thane Gustafson on Russia, and energy-policy figures such as Ernest Moniz on later studies) indicate the academic and policy networks he works within.
