## Broad research

### One-paragraph biography

David Emil Reich (born July 14, 1974, Washington, D.C.) is a geneticist who is among the central figures in the field of ancient DNA, the study of genomes recovered from the remains of long-dead people. He is a professor in the Department of Genetics at Harvard Medical School (joined 2003), holds a joint appointment in Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences department of human evolutionary biology, is an associate member of the Broad Institute, and became a Howard Hughes Medical Institute investigator. He grew up in Chevy Chase, Maryland; his mother is the novelist Tova Reich and his father, Walter Reich, was the founding director of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum and teaches at George Washington University — a family history that Reich has cited as making him acutely conscious of how genetic claims about human groups can be abused. He concentrated in social studies (initially) at Harvard College (class of 1996), shifted toward the sciences, and earned his doctorate at the University of Oxford under the population geneticist David Goldstein, with a thesis on reconstructing human evolutionary history through genetics. His laboratory has industrialized ancient-DNA extraction and analysis: by the early 2020s it had generated genome-wide data from more ancient individuals than all other laboratories combined (more than 16,000 ancient humans reported by 2022, against roughly five sequenced ancient genomes worldwide in 2010). He is the author of the 2018 trade book *Who We Are and How We Got Here: Ancient DNA and the New Science of the Human Past*. *Nature* named him one of its "10 people who mattered" in science in 2015 for transforming ancient DNA "from a niche pursuit to an industrial process."

### Major contributions, with specifics

**Reconstructing Indian population history (2009).** With Kumarasamy Thangaraj, Nick Patterson, Alkes Price, and Lalji Singh, Reich published a *Nature* paper (vol. 461, pp. 489–494, Sept. 24, 2009) analyzing 25 Indian groups. They argued that most present-day mainland Indians descend from a mixture of two highly divergent ancestral populations: "Ancestral North Indians" (ANI), genetically close to Middle Easterners, Central Asians, and Europeans, and "Ancestral South Indians" (ASI), as distinct from ANI as ANI is from East Asians. ANI ancestry was estimated at roughly 39–71% across groups, higher in traditionally upper-caste groups and Indo-European speakers. The indigenous Andaman Islanders were identified as the closest available proxy for unmixed ASI-related ancestry. A methodological innovation was estimating admixture proportions without needing pure ancestral reference samples. A 2013 follow-up dated the major ANI–ASI mixture to roughly 1,900–4,200 years ago, implying earlier unmixed groups.

**Archaic admixture: Neanderthals and Denisovans (2010).** Reich contributed to the draft Neanderthal genome (Green et al., *Science*, 2010), which concluded that roughly 1–4% (often cited as 2–5%) of the genomes of non-Africans derives from interbreeding with Neanderthals, with the proportion similar across non-African populations. Later work refined the mating to roughly 54,000–49,000 years ago. He then led "Genetic history of an archaic hominin group from Denisova Cave in Siberia" (*Nature* 468, pp. 1053–1060, Dec. 2010), in which DNA from a finger bone identified a previously unknown archaic lineage — the Denisovans — sister to Neanderthals, contributing roughly 4–6% of the ancestry of present-day Melanesians (and, in later work, Aboriginal Australians and some island Southeast Asians). The Denisovans were, unusually, defined first by genetics rather than by diagnostic fossils.

**Three-ancestral-population model of Europeans (2014).** In Lazaridis, Patterson, Reich et al., "Ancient human genomes suggest three ancestral populations for present-day Europeans" (*Nature* 513, pp. 409–413, 2014), sequencing of a ~7,000-year-old German farmer and ~8,000-year-old hunter-gatherers from Luxembourg and Sweden led to a model in which most Europeans descend from West European Hunter-Gatherers (WHG), Early European Farmers (EEF, largely Near Eastern), and Ancient North Eurasians (ANE), a Siberian-related lineage. The paper proposed that the farmers carried ~44% ancestry from a "Basal Eurasian" population splitting before other non-African lineages diverged.

**Steppe migration and Indo-European languages (2015).** In Haak, Lazaridis, Patterson, Reich et al., "Massive migration from the steppe was a source for Indo-European languages in Europe" (*Nature* 522, pp. 207–211, 2015), the team showed that Late Neolithic Corded Ware people from Germany traced roughly 75% of their ancestry to Yamnaya steppe pastoralists, documenting a large migration into central Europe ~4,500 years ago. This was offered as genetic support for a steppe origin of at least some European Indo-European languages, paralleling work from Eske Willerslev's group published the same year.

**Native American peopling (2012).** "Reconstructing Native American population history" (*Nature*, July 11, 2012) used 364,470 SNPs across 52 Native American and 17 Siberian groups to argue for at least three streams of Asian gene flow into the Americas: a "First American" stream ancestral to most, a second contributing nearly half the ancestry of Eskimo-Aleut speakers, and a third contributing roughly one-tenth of Na-Dene (Chipewyan) ancestry.

**Indo-Anatolian / Southern Arc and the homeland question (2022–2025).** A long collaboration with the archaeologist David Anthony (begun via a 2010 meeting between Anthony and Patterson) culminated in the 2022 "Southern Arc" trilogy and the 2025 paper Lazaridis, Patterson, Anthony, Reich et al., "The genetic origin of the Indo-Europeans" (*Nature* 639, pp. 132–142, published Feb. 5, 2025), analyzing ancient DNA from 435 individuals. It identifies a "Caucasus–Lower Volga" (CLV) population that contributed about four-fifths of Yamnaya ancestry and at least one-tenth of the ancestry of Bronze Age central Anatolians (where Hittite, an Anatolian language, was spoken). The authors argue for a shared "Indo-Anatolian" homeland in the North Caucasus–lower Volga region and a proto-Indo-Anatolian split around 4300–3500 BCE, with the Yamnaya expansion beginning from a small community north of the Black Sea.

**Methodology and tools.** Reich's program rests heavily on allele-frequency statistics co-developed with Nick Patterson: the f-statistics framework, including the "three-population test" (f3) and "four-population test" (f4 / D-statistic / ABBA-BABA), used to detect and quantify admixture, plus qpAdm and related admixture-graph machinery, distributed as ADMIXTOOLS and later ADMIXTOOLS 2 (an R reimplementation). A signature feature is inferring unsampled "ghost" populations from statistical signatures before any matching ancient genome is found — the Ancient North Eurasians were posited this way and later matched to the ~24,000-year-old Mal'ta boy from near Lake Baikal (Raghavan et al., 2013).

### The substance of his ideas, the reasoning, and the tensions

**"Everyone is mixed; the tree is a net."** Reich's central empirical claim is that essentially all human populations are products of repeated mixture of earlier populations that were themselves mixtures. The reasoning is methodological: ancient DNA repeatedly shows discontinuities at single locations across time — the people living somewhere 4,000 years ago are often not the direct ancestors of those living there 2,000 years later — and admixture statistics detect ancestry from sources that cannot be reduced to a single branching tree. He illustrates this with Britain, which he describes as having seen multiple major migrations in the last ~10,000 years, several involving near-total population turnover. The implication he draws is that "purity" of any ethnic group is a myth and that present-day populations are recent and contingent constructs.

**Migration over in-place evolution.** Where earlier archaeology often explained cultural change as ideas diffusing among stable populations ("pots, not people"), Reich argues that ancient DNA shows large-scale movement of people, sometimes with dramatic replacement — e.g., the steppe-derived turnover associated with Corded Ware and Bell Beaker cultures. He pairs this with a finding of male-biased expansion: the Yamnaya-associated spread carried a narrow set of Y-chromosome lineages, and he has cited estimates that a large fraction of men in some regions descend from a small number of Bronze Age male lineages, which he reads as evidence of socially stratified, polygynous male power rather than uniform population blending.

**Genetic drift over adaptation as the driver of differences.** Reich tends to emphasize that most differences between human populations reflect neutral drift and the accidents of which groups expanded, not natural selection optimizing groups for their environments. He has said he sees little evidence that genetic adaptation, rather than culture and contingency (e.g., Roman military organization, agricultural technology), explains why some groups demographically overwhelmed others. This is a deliberate counter to adaptationist or hierarchical readings of population success.

**Race, "populations," and average genetic differences.** Reich's most contested position, set out in a March 2018 *New York Times* op-ed ("How Genetics Is Changing Our Understanding of 'Race'") and in *Who We Are*, holds that "race" as commonly used is a social construct that does not map cleanly onto biology, *and yet* there are real average genetic differences in ancestry that partly correlate with today's racial categories. His reasoning: because so many traits are influenced by genetic variants that differ in frequency between long-separated populations, it is statistically expected that there are *some* average genetic differences between populations in traits including behavior and cognition — and he argues that flatly denying this possibility in advance is scientifically untenable and, in his framing, leaves a vacuum that genuine racists exploit. He simultaneously insists that any such differences are unlikely to match common stereotypes, that within-group variation vastly exceeds between-group variation, and that genetic difference can never justify unequal treatment. The internal tension critics highlight is between these two halves: he wants both to dismantle "race" as biologically incoherent and to insist that average biological differences between race-correlated populations are real and must be studied.

**Self-acknowledged instability.** Reich has been unusually public about uncertainties in his own models. He has flagged "weird signals" — for instance, that mitochondrial and Y-chromosome data can suggest Neanderthals and modern humans are closer to each other than to Denisovans while whole-genome data suggest otherwise — and has remarked, half in jest, that under some framings non-Africans could be described as "Neanderthals" with a large fraction of modern-human admixture. He has described his own steppe/migration models as "teetering" as new data arrive, calling the situation exciting and destabilizing. This candor is itself a stance: he treats the field as fast-moving and provisional.

### Debates he engages, and where he stands

**Steppe vs. Anatolian hypothesis for Indo-European origins.** The long-running dispute pits the "steppe hypothesis" (Proto-Indo-European homeland in the Pontic–Caspian steppe ~4000 BCE, associated with Marija Gimbutas and David Anthony) against the "Anatolian hypothesis" (homeland in Anatolia ~8000 BCE, spread with farming, associated with Colin Renfrew). Reich's genetic work has been read as a major boost to the steppe model, and his 2025 synthesis proposes a North Caucasus–lower Volga "Indo-Anatolian" homeland that he argues reconciles the Anatolian-branch puzzle (Anatolian languages such as Hittite show weak steppe genetic signal) with steppe origins for the rest of the family. He is careful to say the data do not simply "kill" rival views; other geneticists, such as Carles Lalueza-Fox (Pompeu Fabra University), have publicly cautioned that the Anatolian hypothesis is not eliminated.

**Replacement vs. continuity in archaeology.** Reich stands on the migration/replacement side of the long anthropological debate, arguing that ancient DNA has settled some cases (e.g., steppe ancestry sweeping into Bronze Age Europe) that archaeology alone left ambiguous. Critics in archaeology have warned against equating "pots with people" too readily and against simplistic narratives of conquest and replacement.

**Race/genetics controversy.** His 2018 op-ed and book drew a sharp response: an open letter, "How Not To Talk About Race And Genetics," signed by 67 (often cited as 68) scientists, social scientists, legal scholars, and public-health researchers, published by BuzzFeed after the *New York Times* declined it. The signatories' core arguments, voiced in their own terms: Reich conflates loosely defined "race" with the technical notion of "population"; the long entanglement of genetics with racism conceals real scientific weaknesses in the project of locating group differences in cognition or behavior; and skill at analyzing DNA does not confer authority over the cultural and political meanings of human groups, so such claims should not be advanced without deep collaboration with social scientists. The writer Angela Saini (*Superior: The Return of Race Science*) argued that Reich's framing lends credibility to race-realist arguments. From a different direction, conservative commentary (e.g., in *National Review*) read Reich as conceding that population differences are real while objecting to any policy use of such claims. Reich responded in a follow-up that laid out principles holding that genetic differences, whatever they prove to be, cannot license discrimination, and that pre-emptively denying the possibility of average differences is itself harmful and "patronizing." Where he stands: he maintains that the questions are legitimate scientific ones while disclaiming any hierarchical or policy conclusions.

**Ethics of ancient DNA.** Reich has engaged the debate over how ancient-DNA research treats human remains and descendant/Indigenous communities. He co-organized a 2020 virtual meeting that produced a 2021 *Nature* set of ethical guidelines emphasizing treating samples as once-living people, minimizing destruction of bone and teeth, securing permissions, and engaging local and descendant communities. Critics in this debate have argued that the field, including high-throughput labs, has at times moved too fast relative to community consent.

### Collaborators and influences

His most important long-term collaborator is **Nick (Nicholas) Patterson**, a mathematician and former cryptographer (British GCHQ) who joined the work around 2002 and co-developed the statistical engine (f-statistics, ADMIXTOOLS) underlying much of Reich's output. Core lab figures include **Nadin Rohland** (long-time lab/wet-lab director) and **Swapan/"Shop" Mallick** (bioinformatics). Archaeologist **David Anthony** (Hartwick College, emeritus), author of *The Horse, the Wheel, and Language*, is his key archaeological partner on Indo-European questions, and **Iosif Lazaridis** is a leading first author on the European and Indo-European papers. He has collaborated with Indian geneticists **Lalji Singh** and **Kumarasamy Thangaraj** (CCMB, Hyderabad) and, in archaic-genome work, with the broader circle around **Svante Pääbo** (whose Leipzig group produced the Neanderthal and Denisova sequences). His doctoral advisor **David Goldstein** shaped his early population-genetics training. He also worked closely with statistical geneticists such as **Alkes Price** and population-genetic mapping work with **Simon Myers**.

### Notable biographical specifics

Reich initially leaned toward sociology/social studies before turning to physics and then genetics; he came to ancient DNA partly from a background in medical genetics (gene-mapping for disease) after arriving at Harvard in 2003. Three of his four grandparents were Jewish refugees from Europe, and the family was close to the journalist I.F. Stone; his father's leadership of the Holocaust Museum and the historical abuse of Indo-European "Aryan" narratives by Nazi ideology are themes Reich repeatedly invokes when explaining his caution about how findings are framed. His honors include being named one of *Nature*'s 10 (2015), the Dan David Prize (2017), the NAS Award in Molecular Biology (2019), the Wiley Prize in Biomedical Sciences (2019), the Darwin–Wallace Medal of the Linnean Society (2019), the Massry Prize (2021), and recognition tied to the Antiquity Prize (2022). His lab's industrial scale — capturing roughly 1.2 million genome-wide SNP positions per ancient sample at high throughput — is itself a defining feature of his career, having shifted ancient DNA from a study of rare individual genomes to population-scale datasets.
