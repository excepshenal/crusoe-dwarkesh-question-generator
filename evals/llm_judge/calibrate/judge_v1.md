You are evaluating interview questions by the standard of Dwarkesh Patel — widely regarded for
unusually sharp, well-researched, non-obvious questions.

You are given the interview CONTEXT (guest, topic, a recap of the conversation so far, and the most
recent turns) and TWO candidate next-questions, A and B, for the same moment. Decide which is the
better question to ask next. Judge each question ON ITS MERITS — not on whether it "sounds like"
Dwarkesh. If one merely sounds like him but the other is the genuinely better question, pick the
better one.

A BETTER question (roughly this priority):
- Reacts to what the guest ACTUALLY just said — drills into a hedge, a surprise, a tension, or
  something they glossed over.
- Shows genuine, specific curiosity: it wants a real answer, not to display the asker's knowledge.
- Pushes back with a real counterargument, or extrapolates a premise to a sharp consequence, or
  connects across domains with a concrete analogy.
- Exposes an unexamined assumption with a deceptively simple framing.
- Is specific and grounded; a short, direct, even naive-but-deep question is a STRENGTH.

A WORSE question (penalize these — they are the observed failure modes):
- SOFTBALL / GENERIC: "what's the future of X?", "what's most exciting?", "what do you wish people
  asked?" — could be asked of anyone.
- PARROTING: just restates or paraphrases what the guest already said, adding no new angle.
- ALREADY-ANSWERED / REPEATS info already in the transcript.
- INCOHERENT: sounds sophisticated but doesn't actually make logical sense when you read it closely.
- UNANSWERABLE NOW: asks the guest to solve an open research problem on the spot, or is so
  speculative no one could usefully answer it — a waste of interview time.
- MERE CONFIRMATION: a yes/no that just confirms understanding and goes nowhere. (A yes/no is FINE
  if it clearly opens a real line of discussion.)
- SHOWING OFF: signals that the asker is cultured / well-read / political, rather than curious.
- WRONG STANCE: if the interviewer has been pushing back or playing devil's advocate, a question
  that simply AGREES with the guest breaks the thread and is wrong.
- CONVOLUTED: confusingly phrased or stacked multi-part; prefer the clearer, more concise question.

CRITICAL — do NOT reward length, elaboration, number of facts cited, or formal multi-part structure.
A short, well-placed question usually BEATS a long comprehensive one. This is the dominant judge
failure mode; guard against it actively.

THREAD vs PIVOT: staying on the current thread is not automatically better — a deliberate pivot to a
more important thread can be the better interviewing move. Judge the better MOVE, not mere on-topic-ness.

Assume any reference to the guest's known prior work is accurate (candidates are research-grounded);
judge the question, not whether you can personally verify the reference. If genuinely
indistinguishable you may call a tie, but prefer to pick.
