## Broad research

### One-paragraph bio

Byrne Hobart is an American finance-and-technology writer, equity analyst, and investor best known for *The Diff*, a paid newsletter covering "inflections in finance and tech" that he has grown to more than 50,000 subscribers. Born in St. Louis (his mother a nurse, his father in group-health-insurance sales), he attended the Jesuit St. Louis University High School and won a full-ride scholarship to Arizona State University, but dropped out at 18 and moved to New York City in 2006 to pursue a career as a hedge-fund analyst. He worked first in online marketing — including building SEO strategy for Yahoo's news, sports, and finance verticals — before joining the equity hedge fund SAC Capital (now Point72) around 2012, where he covered internet and consumer-internet companies (Shutterfly, Vistaprint, Ancestry.com, WebMD, Google, Amazon). After being laid off in a 2014 team restructuring he briefly worked at 21.co (the Bitcoin company led by Balaji Srinivasan), then returned to East-Coast analyst roles and began writing publicly in 2018, first on Medium and then on Substack. He turned on *The Diff*'s paywall in February 2020 as COVID-19 spread, and within roughly five months newsletter income exceeded all his other earnings. He is a CFA charterholder, a partner at the frontier-technology investment firm Anomaly (with Patrick Blum and Tobias Huber), runs an AngelList syndicate, also writes the explainer publication *Capital Gains*, and is co-author, with Tobias Huber, of *Boom: Bubbles and the End of Stagnation* (Stripe Press, October 2024). He reports writing roughly 500,000 words per year.

### Major works and themes

**The Diff (2018–present).** Hobart's central project is a weekday long-form newsletter analyzing "technologies, companies, and trends that are making the future high-variance," concentrating on finance and tech because those domains move fast enough to reveal where the broader economy is going. He has framed his explicit ambition as becoming "the favorite primary source that the nerds about the 2020s are all reading" — i.e., writing the first draft of financial history for future historians. The newsletter is named after the Unix `diff` utility, reflecting his technical orientation. Format: each issue pairs an essay with several curated news items annotated for long-term significance; free subscribers get a subset, paid subscribers get the full schedule. The newsletter began with roughly 1,200 readers and was endorsed early by Patrick Collison (Stripe), Patrick McKenzie, and Leo Polovets, and it is frequently compared to Matt Levine's *Money Stuff*, with the distinction that Hobart leans on cross-disciplinary mental models and financial analogies rather than legal-financial commentary. Notable individual essays/issues include "Stripe and Solid-State Economics" (May 2021), "The Factorio Mindset" (February 2022), "Understanding Jane Street" (August 2022), "Hand-Crafted Artisanal Liquidity Provision" (August 2022), and "Money, Credit, Trust, and FTX" (November 2022).

**Boom: Bubbles and the End of Stagnation (Stripe Press, 2024), with Tobias Huber.** This is Hobart's flagship statement of his worldview, nominated for the Manhattan Institute's Hayek Book Prize and praised by Tyler Cowen. The book takes an inductive approach: it examines a set of historical breakthroughs — the Manhattan Project, the Apollo program, the growth of semiconductor manufacturing (Moore's Law), the mid-century golden age of corporate R&D, the U.S. shale/fracking revolution, and Bitcoin — and argues that each ran on "bubble dynamics": FOMO-driven enthusiasm, messianic leadership operating with reduced accountability, and a degree of seemingly irrational commitment that nonetheless produced durable value. The thesis: bubbles are not purely destructive; certain "productive" bubbles supply the activation energy and coordination needed for transformative innovation, by concentrating capital and talent into a "cluster in time" and by letting participants suspend ordinary rational evaluation long enough to commit to long-horizon projects. The book situates this against the "Great Stagnation" diagnosis (productivity growth stalling since around 1971, declining scientific output per dollar, sequel-driven culture) and pairs it with claims about risk-aversion's social and demographic roots — an aging population, fewer children, and the rising influence of risk-averse decision-makers in institutions.

**Capital Gains** is a companion publication explaining the finance concepts a general reader's education tends to skip. Hobart has also published shorter pieces in outlets such as a16z's *Future* ("Well-Behaved Bubbles Often Make History"). He co-hosts the podcast *The Riff* with Erik Torenberg.

### The substance of the ideas, with reasoning, tensions, and opposing arguments

**On bubbles and stagnation (his signature position).** Hobart's core claim is that the modern West is, if anything, *under-bubbled* — that the cultural and institutional machinery for generating definite, ambitious, coordinated bets on the future has atrophied. The reasoning runs: real breakthroughs require many actors to make complementary, irreversible commitments before the payoff is provable; under normal market discipline, each actor waits for the others, and nothing happens. A bubble breaks this stalemate by creating a self-referential belief system in which participants stop trying to persuade skeptics and instead act as if the future they want is already inevitable. Two mechanisms recur in his account: (1) *parallel/complementary progress* — technologies that need each other to be useful (cars and gas stations; faster chips and software written to exploit them) get built simultaneously because everyone assumes everyone else will follow through; in the semiconductor case, chipmakers pushed past current demand trusting that software developers would write programs to consume the extra speed, which then justified the next round of investment; and (2) *temporal clustering* — pulling future demand and talent into the present so a critical mass forms at once. He explicitly invokes Peter Thiel's "definite optimism" (from *Zero to One*) as the cultural ingredient: a clear, concrete plan for a different future, as opposed to indefinite optimism (things will improve, but we can't say how). The COVID response, in the book's telling, is a *negative* example — the U.S. lacked bubble dynamics, manufacturers hesitated to scale test/vaccine capacity and were then stranded once shortages eased.

*Strongest counterarguments and named opponents.* The obvious opposing position, which Hobart engages, is the conventional economist's view that bubbles destroy capital and misallocate resources — the dot-com and 2008 housing bubbles as canonical waste. Hobart's rebuttal is that even "failed" bubbles can leave durable infrastructure and a trained cohort (fiber laid in the late 1990s; engineers and a software ecosystem). A second, more pointed critique appears in reviews: a *determinism/agency* tension — if progress depends on demographic and possibly biological drivers of risk-appetite (the book floats declining birth rates and even declining testosterone, possibly tied to endocrine disruptors, as factors), it is unclear what individuals or policymakers can actually do, which sits awkwardly with the book's prescriptive, optimistic framing. Reviewers also note a paradox the book itself raises but does not fully resolve: societal risk-aversion has *increased* even as material safety nets and baseline safety have improved, which complicates any simple story about why ambition declined. The stagnation framing he adopts is itself contested ground — it descends from Tyler Cowen's *The Great Stagnation*, and the counter-camp argues measured stagnation is partly a measurement artifact (free digital goods, quality improvements) rather than a real slowdown.

**On market efficiency and where edge comes from.** Hobart treats markets as broadly but imperfectly efficient and is unusually candid that *alpha sums to zero before fees, taxes, and transaction costs* — for one investor to beat the market, another must lose. From this he draws several positions. He argues hedge-fund outperformance has eroded since roughly 2000 as more capital chased the same inefficiencies, and he has floated that the industry may be near "peak pods" — the maximum number of multi-manager portfolio managers the market can profitably support. He describes the modern "pod shop" as a machine for isolating idiosyncratic stock-picking skill: managers get risk budgets while factor and industry exposures are hedged out, which he characterizes as "a method of measuring investment skill masquerading as an asset class." He traces the lineage to Alfred Winslow Jones's original long/short model. A recurring epistemic discipline he attributes to the best analysts: constantly asking "why do I deserve to be right about this?" — i.e., what is the specific reason the rest of the market is wrong.

*A productive tension in his own views:* he simultaneously believes markets are hard to beat and that good analysis (reading 10-Ks and prospectuses that even professionals skip, doing primary-source channel checks) yields real edge. He resolves this partly by claiming the single largest durable advantage belongs not to professionals but to *retail/long-horizon* investors — they avoid short-term capital-gains taxes, face no monthly-performance or leverage pressure, and can hold through drawdowns that force levered funds to exit prematurely. He is also skeptical that venture-capital skill is measurable at all: power-law returns mean a single huge winner can dominate, making luck and skill nearly indistinguishable, and VCs lack the clean marginal-value-of-research calculus that trading firms have.

**On hidden macro bets inside "neutral" strategies.** Hobart argues that supposedly diversified strategies carry concealed directional wagers: risk parity and the 60/40 portfolio are implicitly long low inflation, which is why they broke down in 2022; venture capital is effectively a bet on a flat yield curve and low rates, which sustain high valuations. The 30-year fixed mortgage is, in his words, "intrinsically toxic" because it embeds a huge interest-rate option for homeowners — refinancing waves force hedgers to trade Treasuries, raising Treasury volatility, and because Treasuries are the global benchmark cost of capital, he argues "the price of literally everything in the world is downstream" of American mortgage microstructure.

**On who should hold which risks.** He argues young households saving for retirement are the socially optimal holders of equity risk (long horizons make volatility cheap and let them dollar-cost average), whereas endowments and sovereign wealth funds should arguably hold *less* equity because sudden drawdowns force painful budget cuts; founders, by contrast, should hold concentrated equity for alignment. He is also a skeptic of reported private-equity outperformance: he contends that once you adjust for leverage and compare to similarly small, statistically cheap public companies, PE returns largely track public markets — except suspiciously during downturns, when illiquid marks aren't written down, which he reads as smoothing/mark-to-market discretion rather than genuine risk-adjusted alpha.

**On AI and labor.** As early as 2020 he argued that labor-saving AI could *increase* labor demand rather than reduce it, an instance of his general resistance to first-order "automation destroys jobs" intuitions.

### Debates he engages and where he stands

- **The stagnation debate:** he is firmly in the "real stagnation, fixable through ambition and definite optimism" camp, allied intellectually with Thiel and Cowen, against those who see slowdown as a measurement illusion.
- **Are bubbles bad?** He is the leading contemporary advocate that *some* bubbles are net-positive and even necessary, against the mainstream finance/economics view of bubbles as waste.
- **Active management / EMH:** he is a sophisticated near-believer in efficiency who nonetheless thinks edge is real but scarce and shrinking, and that the durable edge has migrated to patient long-horizon capital.
- **Public vs. private markets:** he is a skeptic of the reported risk-adjusted superiority of private equity and of the measurability of VC skill.

### Influences

The most visible influence is Peter Thiel, whose definite/indefinite-optimism framework structures *Boom*. Tyler Cowen's *The Great Stagnation* supplies the diagnostic backdrop. He frequently cites Dan Wang's essay "Definite Optimism as Human Capital" (a restatement and extension of the Thiel idea — Wang, not Hobart, is its author) as a touchstone for treating optimism and concrete visions of the future as a form of capital. His co-author Tobias Huber (a philosopher with a doctorate from ETH Zurich, also a partner at Anomaly) is the intellectual collaborator on the bubble thesis. He is part of a broader "progress studies" / tech-finance writing milieu that includes Patrick McKenzie (patio11) and Patrick Collison.
