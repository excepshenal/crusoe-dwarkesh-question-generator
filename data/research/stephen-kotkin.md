## Broad research

### One-paragraph biography

Stephen Mark Kotkin (born February 17, 1959, in Englewood, New Jersey) is an American historian of Russia, the Soviet Union, and modern geopolitics. The son of a factory worker of Belarusian-Jewish descent (his father's family came from Vitebsk), he earned a BA in English from the University of Rochester (1981), then an MA (1983) and PhD (1988) in history from the University of California, Berkeley, where he trained under the labor and Russian historian Reginald E. Zelnik and the historian of Russian thought Martin Malia. He joined Princeton University in 1989 and taught there for 33 years, becoming the John P. Birkelund '52 Professor in History and International Affairs, directing the Russian and Eurasian Studies Program (1995–2008), and co-directing the Certificate in History and the Practice of Diplomacy (2015–2022); he took emeritus status in 2022. He is now the Kleinheinz Senior Fellow at the Hoover Institution and a senior fellow at Stanford's Freeman Spogli Institute for International Studies. He has held Guggenheim, ACLS, and NEH fellowships. His signature project is a planned three-volume biography of Joseph Stalin; he writes and speaks frequently for general audiences, especially in *Foreign Affairs*, on Russian power and the contemporary world order.

### Major books, with specifics

**Magnetic Mountain: Stalinism as a Civilization (University of California Press, 1995; ~640 pp.).** Kotkin's first major book grew out of his Berkeley dissertation and fieldwork in Magnitogorsk, the steel city the Bolsheviks began building on the bare Urals steppe in 1929. Rather than treating Stalinism as either coercion-from-above or a betrayal of revolutionary ideals, he reframes it as a coherent *civilization*: "a quintessential Enlightenment utopia, an attempt, via the instrumentality of the state, to impose a rational ordering on society" while transcending the class divisions of the capitalist industrial era. Methodologically the book is best known for two contributions. First, drawing explicitly and "eclectically" on Michel Foucault, Kotkin applied a microphysics-of-power lens — chapters under the heading "Living Socialism: The Little Tactics of the Habitat" examine living space, daily life, and identity. Second, his concept of "speaking Bolshevik" argues that ordinary workers learned to perform and deploy the regime's official language strategically — not necessarily out of belief, but because adopting it yielded benefits (housing, food, salaries, advancement). Participation in this "identification game" explains the relative quiescence and complicity of Soviet society without reducing people to either true believers or terrorized victims.

**Armageddon Averted: The Soviet Collapse, 1970–2000 (Oxford University Press, 2001; revised 2008).** A compact synthesis arguing that the USSR's end was not foreordained by economic failure, central planning's inefficiency, or Western (especially Reagan-era) military pressure — and that, remarkably, it happened with very little bloodshed. Kotkin's central thesis is that Mikhail Gorbachev and his generation were *true believers* in socialism whose pursuit of a "romantic dream" of socialist renewal inadvertently dismantled the system they meant to save. Gorbachev, "flabbergasted" that reform was producing liquidation, would not cross the lines that might actually have transformed the economy — private property and market allocation — because doing so would have meant abandoning socialism itself. Kotkin emphasizes contingency and restraint: those positioned to use mass violence to preserve the Union were too old, too divided, or too indecisive, so a "deadly system" came to a "meek dissolution." The "Armageddon" of the title is the nuclear-armed superpower's potential for catastrophic violent breakup, which did not occur.

**Uncivil Society: 1989 and the Implosion of the Communist Establishment (with Jan T. Gross, 2009).** Argues against the romantic "civil society triumphs" narrative of 1989, holding that Communist regimes in Eastern Europe collapsed primarily because of the bankruptcy and loss of nerve of their own ruling establishments rather than because of organized dissident movements — a companion thesis to *Armageddon Averted*.

**Stalin, Volume I: Paradoxes of Power, 1878–1928 (Penguin Press, 2014; ~976 pp.; Pulitzer Prize finalist, 2015).** The first installment of the biography. Its governing claim is that Stalin must be understood not as a clinically deranged or merely psychologically damaged individual but as a product *and* maker of historical structures — autocracy, the late imperial Russian state, geopolitics, the First World War, and above all Bolshevik ideology and Leninist organizational method. Kotkin directly reverses Trotsky's famous claim that "the apparatus made Stalin": instead, "Stalin created the apparatus, and it was a colossal feat." He insists Stalin was a committed Marxist-Leninist whose worldview was real and consequential, "realistic to the point of cynicism and ideological to a fault." On structure-versus-agency he holds both ends: large structural forces explain the field of action, but individual decisions and contingencies determined outcomes. The volume's most contested specific claim is that Lenin's so-called "Testament" (which called for Stalin's removal as General Secretary) was not genuinely Lenin's but was effectively authored by his wife, Nadezhda Krupskaya — Kotkin points to procedural irregularities, Lenin's post-stroke speech impairment around the alleged dictation dates, and how conveniently the text tracked the April 1923 party debates.

**Stalin, Volume II: Waiting for Hitler, 1929–1941 (Penguin Press, 2017; ~1,184 pp.).** Covers Stalin's consolidation of power through the eve of the German invasion. Major theses: (1) On collectivization and the 1931–33 famine, Kotkin argues Stalin's grain seizures were the direct cause of mass starvation in Ukraine, the Lower Volga, and Kazakhstan, and that Stalin and his circle knew about the famine and did nothing to mitigate it — but he rejects the claim that Stalin was deliberately trying to exterminate peasants or Ukrainians as such; in his formulation, "there was no 'Ukrainian' famine; the famine was Soviet." (2) On the Great Terror, he attributes the violence to the combination of Bolshevik/Communist modes of thinking (a regime structurally surrounded by enemies in a hostile capitalist world) with Stalin's own suspicious, "demonic" cast of mind; he stresses that terror operated from below as much as imposed from above, enabled by bureaucratic incentives and Stalin's information monopoly, and that Stalin was calculating and strategic rather than irrational. (3) On the 1939 Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact, he reads it as Stalin's geopolitical coup — buying time, technology, and restored great-power standing. (4) On June 1941, he portrays Stalin as shrewd but "too crafty for his own good," misjudging Hitler by assuming Hitler would not risk a two-front war.

**Stalin, Volume III (forthcoming, 2026).** The concluding volume, carrying the story through World War II and after; widely anticipated, projected for July 2026.

Other works include *Steeltown, USSR* (1991), *Rediscovering Russia in Asia* (1995, ed.), *The Cultural Gradient* (2003, ed.), *Korea at the Center* (2005, ed.), *Manchurian Railways and the Opening of China* (2010, ed.), and *Historical Legacies of Communism* (2014, co-ed. with Mark Beissinger).

### The substance of his ideas, with reasoning and counterarguments

**On Stalin: ideology and structure over psychopathology.** Kotkin's foundational move is to displace the psychological-monster reading of Stalin (the abused boy who became a paranoiac) with a political and ideological one. His reasoning: Stalin's "paranoia" tracks the structural predicament of a Communist regime that genuinely believed itself encircled by capitalist enemies and penetrated by spies, so behavior that looks pathological is legible as ideology-plus-strategy. He argues you cannot understand Stalin's decisions outside the tragic arc of imperial Russia and the Bolshevik project. *Counterarguments and named opponents:* The Lenin Testament-as-forgery thesis drew the sharpest fire. Ronald Grigor Suny argued it "relies on conjecture rather than evidence," and Mark Edele called it an "empirically shaky thesis," noting that otherwise mainly neo-Stalinist writers embrace it. More broadly, critics in the *Stalin* roundtables questioned whether Kotkin's emphasis on Stalin's sincere ideological belief understates careerism and contingency, and whether the "great man / colossal feat" framing risks overstating Stalin's singular agency.

**On the famine and the genocide question.** Kotkin's position — Stalin caused and knew of the famine but did not intend ethnic extermination — places him against the Holodomor-as-genocide interpretation most prominently associated with Anne Applebaum (*Red Famine*). The opposing argument holds that the specific targeting of Ukraine (closing republic borders, requisitioning seed grain, the national dimension of repression) constitutes intent to destroy Ukrainians as a group. Kotkin counters that the famine struck multiple grain regions (Kazakhstan's pastoral catastrophe was proportionally enormous) and that the driver was ruthless extraction and indifference, not a plan of national annihilation. Sheila Fitzpatrick has summarized his stance neutrally; the debate remains a genuine and politically charged scholarly tension, sharpened by the Russia-Ukraine war.

**On the Soviet collapse.** Here Kotkin stakes out a clear position against two rival explanations. Against the "Reagan won the Cold War / arms-race-bankrupted-the-USSR" school, he argues military spending did not cause collapse. Against the pure economic-determinist school (central planning was doomed, so collapse was inevitable on schedule), he argues the system limped along and could have continued, and that what killed it was the *reformist idealism* of believers, not the failure itself. The internal tension some critics note: by foregrounding Gorbachev's romantic agency and "dumb luck," Kotkin arguably leans on contingency for the collapse even as he leans on deep structure ("perpetual geopolitics") for Russian behavior generally — a productive tension between his structuralism and his emphasis on individual decision that runs through all his work.

**On Russia/Ukraine and geopolitics: "perpetual geopolitics."** Kotkin's master concept is that Russia repeatedly "imagines itself to be a providential country" with great-power aspirations it lacks the capacity to fulfill, because "the West is just immensely stronger, larger, richer, better technologically." Faced with this ambition-capability gap, Russian regimes resort to coercion, repression, and territorial expansion to "manage the gap with the West." He frames this as a recurring strategic *choice* across czarist, Soviet, and Putin-era Russia — explicitly not as cultural inevitability or "Russians are eternally aggressive." On the 2022 invasion, he rejects the NATO-expansion-as-cause argument associated with John Mearsheimer (and others in the realist "the West provoked Russia" camp). His reasoning: the pattern of an "autocratic, repressive, anti-Western" Russia predates NATO and even NATO's existence; Russia voluntarily signed agreements like the 1997 NATO-Russia Founding Act affirming states' freedom to choose alliances; therefore Ukraine's NATO aspirations violated no Western commitment. He characterizes the invasion as "an act of anger, resentment, and desperation" rooted in Russia's refusal to accept the post-1991 settlement. *Named opponents:* Mearsheimer and the offensive-realist school, who argue great-power security logic and NATO enlargement explain Russian behavior; Kotkin's counter is that this misattributes a domestically generated, regime-type-driven pattern to Western action.

**On Russia's future and Western policy.** In "The Five Futures of Russia" (*Foreign Affairs*, April 2024) he sketches scenarios — including a "France" path (upheaval followed eventually by a more liberal, rule-of-law order), retrenchment/retreat, becoming a junior partner or vassal of China, a "North Korea" path of dysfunctional dependency, and chaos/disintegration (loose nuclear and biological materials, criminal impunity). His policy reasoning is that the United States cannot engineer which future arrives but should prepare for all of them, sustain strength and alliances, and avoid both wishful regime-change fantasies and capitulation. In "The Cold War Never Ended" (*Foreign Affairs*, May/June 2024) he argues the structural Western-vs-authoritarian contest persists and that the framework of containment and patient strength remains relevant. On American competitiveness he has urged expanded STEM and trade/community-college education, deregulation to enable housing construction, and forms of national service — connecting domestic vitality to geopolitical durability.

**On communism's human cost.** Kotkin has written that Communist systems caused at least 65 million deaths between 1917 and 2017, emphasizing that famine and starvation from coercive "social engineering" killed even more than deliberate executions did — a claim consistent with his famine analysis (lethal indifference and extraction rather than only intentional killing).

### Debates he engages and where he stands

- **Totalitarian school vs. revisionists:** Kotkin transcends the old binary. With *Magnetic Mountain* he was read as a post-revisionist who took both ideology and everyday social practice seriously, rejecting both the pure top-down totalitarian model and the revisionist downplaying of Stalin's centrality.
- **Stalin's agency:** Against Trotsky's "apparatus made the man," he insists Stalin built the apparatus.
- **Genocide intent in the famine:** Against Applebaum; "Soviet, not Ukrainian" famine.
- **Causes of Soviet collapse:** Against both Reagan-triumphalism and strict economic determinism; for reformist idealism plus contingency.
- **Causes of the Ukraine war:** Against NATO-provocation realism (Mearsheimer); for Russia's internally generated "perpetual geopolitics."

### Influences

His Berkeley advisors Reginald Zelnik (labor/social history) and Martin Malia (Russian intellectual history; author of *The Soviet Tragedy*, which read Soviet history through the ideological logic of socialism) clearly shaped his blend of social history and ideology-centered analysis. Michel Foucault's analytics of power and discourse underpin the method of *Magnetic Mountain*. His access to and praise for the Hoover Institution's archival collections informs his later archival-biographical work.

### Notable biographical specifics

- Long Princeton tenure (1989–2022), directing Russian and Eurasian Studies (1995–2008).
- Now anchored at Stanford/Hoover; writes prolifically for *Foreign Affairs* and comments widely on contemporary Russia.
- Married to Soyoung Lee, an art historian and museum curator; two children.
- A dedicated critical monograph, *Stalin. Waiting for the Truth!*, was published challenging factual claims in Volume II — evidence of the contentious reception his Stalin work attracts.
