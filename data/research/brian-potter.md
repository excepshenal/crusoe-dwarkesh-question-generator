## Broad research

### One-paragraph bio

Brian Potter is a structural engineer turned writer and analyst who studies the technology and economics of how buildings, infrastructure, and physical goods get made. He holds a bachelor's degree in civil engineering from Georgia Tech and a master's in systems engineering from the University of Central Florida, and spent roughly fifteen years working as a structural engineer in a variety of industry roles — consulting engineer, subcontractor, and design-builder. Most consequentially for his later work, he managed an engineering team at Katerra, the SoftBank-backed construction startup that raised over $2 billion to industrialize homebuilding and collapsed into bankruptcy in 2021. As Katerra was failing, Potter began writing the newsletter *Construction Physics* (first post September 2020) as a way to work through why the construction industry had resisted the kind of efficiency revolution Katerra had promised. The newsletter — by his own count around 186 essays and roughly 600,000 words over about four and a half years — grew into one of the most-read independent publications on the built environment. He is now Senior Infrastructure Fellow at the Institute for Progress (IFP), which supports the newsletter, and the author of the book *The Origins of Efficiency* (published September 23, 2025). His overarching method is to research narrow technical subjects exhaustively and report what the evidence actually shows, and his recurring conclusion — captured in his own summary "things are almost always more complicated than they seem" — is that apparent inevitabilities in how we build are usually contingent on history, geography, and economics rather than logical necessity.

### Major works and themes, with specifics

**Construction Physics (newsletter, 2020–present).** Originally focused on diagnosing why construction productivity has stagnated, it broadened into infrastructure, manufacturing, materials, and industrial history. Notable recurring series and essays include the *Katerra* post-mortems, the *manufactured/mobile home* histories, *Operation Breakthrough* (the 1970s HUD industrialized-housing program), Toyota's prefab homes, and the learning-curve essays. The publication is supported by IFP.

**"Another Day in Katerradise" (Katerra post-mortem).** Potter's insider account of Katerra, where founder Michael Marks (a former Flextronics CEO) sought to apply electronics-manufacturing principles to buildings — prefabricating panelized light-wood structures with windows, plumbing stacks, and electrical boxes pre-installed in the factory. The company raised over $2 billion, employed roughly 8,000 people at peak across nine countries, and ran factories in Phoenix and Spokane. Potter's diagnosis: Katerra "scaled up massively prior to having a product people wanted to buy" — a basic lack of product-market fit. It pursued dozens of products at once (garden apartments, CLT panels, custom software) while repeatedly pivoting its core building system (CLT → light wood → cold-formed steel and back), each pivot resetting the factory economics. Acquired contractors and design firms kept operating as before; cross-division collaboration was nearly impossible; bureaucracy was so heavy that a change saving $1 million on an active project took months of internal negotiation. The proximate trigger for bankruptcy was the collapse of fellow SoftBank-backed Greensill Capital after a recapitalization that cut Katerra's valuation ~90%. His deeper conclusion: the engineering team would design factory-produced buildings and the costs would keep "coming back too high" — Katerra's foundational thesis, that construction would be far cheaper if done in factories, was either wrong or "woefully incomplete."

**"The Elusive Cost Savings of the Prefabricated Home."** A core empirical claim: despite repeated industrialization attempts since the 1920s, prefab/modular typically yields only ~5–20% cost savings versus site-built, far below the 80%+ reductions seen in auto manufacturing. A 1998 HUD/NAHB study found factory-built homes cost ~15% less than equivalent site-built homes. Modern modular executives concede the draw is schedule, not cost ("It's the reduction in schedule"). The big exception is HUD-code *manufactured* (mobile) homes: single-wides cost ~35–47% less and double-wides ~60–64% less than conventional homes — but Potter argues this comes from minimizing material use, cheap finishes, low margins, and delivering the whole house as one or two complete modules (minimizing interfaces and on-site work), not from generic "factory magic." As manufactured homes get more conventional (CrossMods), the advantage erodes toward typical prefab margins.

**"Trends in US Construction Productivity" (and the September 2025 Aspen Institute paper "Labor Productivity in Construction").** Potter assembles the evidence that US construction productivity has been flat or declining for decades. Headline figures: construction labor productivity fell ~0.59%/year from 1964–2004 while non-farm industries grew ~1.77%/year; by official statistics "a construction worker in 2020 produced less than one in 1970," even as overall economic productivity roughly tripled since 1964. He stress-tests this across four granularities — sector-wide (spending ÷ hours), sub-sector (units or sq ft per worker), project-level (RS Means and Craftsman estimating guides, 1950s–2023), and task-level (installation rates) — using BLS, BEA value-add data, KLEMS, and Construction Industry Institute benchmarking. Crucially, he takes the skeptics seriously: output-mix shifts, quality improvements (bigger, better-insulated, more code-compliant homes), input-cost deflators that mask real gains, and the rise of renovation (now 40–45% of residential spending vs. 20–25% in 1970) all distort the numbers. His honest verdict: even after these adjustments, "most measures of construction productivity show at best very low levels of growth," and the consistency across methods suggests the stagnation is real, not a measurement artifact. International context: across ~45 countries, most wealthy nations improved 1970–1995 then stagnated after 1995; the US is not uniquely bad (its ~0.2%/year post-1995 slightly exceeds peers), with only Ireland and Belgium sustaining 1%+ annual improvement.

**"Where Are My Damn Learning Curves?" (and "Construction Costs Rarely Fall").** Potter's signature theoretical contribution applies the learning/experience curve to construction and finds it largely absent. Learning curves describe a constant cost reduction per cumulative doubling of output (e.g., solar PV ~44% learning rate). His arithmetic: the US had ~45 million housing units in 1950 and has built 100 million+ since — about 1.66 doublings; even a mild 90% learning rate would imply ~17% cost decline, an 85% rate ~25%, "but we don't see anything like that at all." His mechanisms for why: (1) every project is a near-new process — new team, owner, layout, and site each time — so within-project learning is "constantly reset to zero"; (2) little fixed-cost leverage — doubling a building's size just yields twice the building, since cost is mostly labor and materials; (3) the basic technology of how a building goes together is mature, so few transformative breakthroughs remain. Standardization and continuous improvement need a stable, controlled environment — exactly what construction lacks.

**"What Makes Housing So Expensive?" and the NBER/SSRN working paper "Building Costs and House Prices" (with Chad Syverson, June 2025).** Potter separates the *structure* cost from the *land* cost. For most US housing the physical structure dominates, but in the dense metros where new housing matters most, land can exceed ~70% of home cost — and that land price is driven not by people valuing space but by regulatory and zoning limits on how many units can go on a parcel. Rezoning to allow more units sharply raises land value. The Syverson collaboration places his construction-cost work into formal economics.

**The Origins of Efficiency (book, Sept 23, 2025).** The synthesis of his thinking. Thesis: efficiency is not one breakthrough but "a small number of specific things you can do to make a process more efficient," derived from studying dozens of production processes — cigarette making, package delivery, steel, tomato harvesting. Efficiency comes from relentless iteration, tight feedback loops, scale (each doubling of volume reliably lowers cost), and the *selective* mechanization of labor-intensive tasks — and critically requires making the same thing again and again. The 10-chapter book explains efficiency strategies, their limits and interactions, and closes on why construction specifically resists improvement. He explicitly critiques the assumption that factories automatically cut costs, citing the failures of Lustron (1940s), Operation Breakthrough, Toyota Home, and Katerra.

**IFP infrastructure and energy reports.** Beyond housing, Potter writes on the broader "why America struggles to build" agenda: "Why Does Nuclear Power Plant Construction Cost So Much?" (May 2023), the "Compute in America" / "How to Build an AI Data Center" series (June 2024) on AI infrastructure, transmission-line cost allocation and "How to Save America's Transmission System" (2024), shale-revolution technology history (2023), environmental review/permitting reform ("How to Stop Environmental Review from Harming the Environment," 2022; "Moving Past Environmental Proceduralism," 2024), indoor air quality (2023), fusion (July 2024), and "Eliminate Redundant Subway Cross-Passages" (2026) on cutting transit construction cost without compromising safety.

### Substance of his ideas — reasoning, counterarguments, and internal tensions

**Why industrialized construction keeps failing (his central position).** Potter's reasoning runs: factory manufacturing lowers cost through scale, standardization, and learning curves, but construction systematically denies all three. Geography forces every project to a different site with different soil, climate, seismic/wind loads, codes, and permitting jurisdictions; transporting volumetric modules is bulky and expensive; factory overhead is a fixed cost that needs high, steady volume to amortize, which the lumpy, custom demand for buildings rarely supplies; and modular building multiplies the *interfaces* (panel-to-panel joints, MEP connections) that have to be re-coordinated on site, eating the factory savings. The exception that proves the rule is the HUD manufactured home, which works precisely because it ships as one or two complete units to a deregulated national standard with cheap finishes and thin margins.

**The strongest counterargument (which he voices).** The industrialization optimists — the thesis Katerra, Lustron, Operation Breakthrough, and Toyota Home all bet on — argue that construction is simply a manufacturing problem that hasn't yet been solved by a sufficiently capitalized, disciplined operator: if you standardize the product, automate the factory, and reach volume, the auto-style 80% cost curve should appear. A related skeptic position on the productivity data argues the "stagnation" is largely a measurement illusion: output mix shifted toward labor-intensive homebuilding (the Allen analysis of 1968–1978 attributed most of the measured decline to mix, not real efficiency loss), quality rose (today's square foot embodies more output), renovation inflates labor hours, and input-cost deflators hide genuine gains. Potter does not dismiss these — he grants each is real and partly true — but concludes that after correcting for them the picture still shows near-zero growth, and that the cross-method consistency makes the stagnation hard to wave away.

**Tensions in his own views.** (1) He is at once a deflationary skeptic of construction's measured productivity *and* a careful debunker of naive productivity pessimism — he insists both that things are worse than boosters claim and better than the rawest BLS numbers imply. (2) His learning-curve framework says scale and repetition drive cost down everywhere, yet his core finding is that construction is the domain where those forces structurally fail to apply — so the optimistic mechanism and the pessimistic conclusion sit in deliberate tension. (3) He worked at Katerra and believed (or hoped) in industrialization, then became its most rigorous in-house critic; his stance is less "prefab is doomed" than "prefab's cost savings are real but small, and the conditions for big savings are rare." (4) On housing, he locates the dominant cost in *regulation-inflated land* in dense metros while elsewhere insisting *structure* cost dominates — a position that resolves only by geography, which can read as having it both ways depending on the city.

**Where he stands in the field's major debates.**
- *Construction productivity stagnation:* real, decades-long, and broadly shared across wealthy nations after 1995 — not a US-specific pathology and not purely a measurement artifact, but also not as catastrophic as the headline "a 1970 worker out-produced a 2020 worker" suggests.
- *Industrialization of building:* skeptical of the "factories will revolutionize housing" thesis; the savings are modest (5–20%) except for the narrow manufactured-home case; schedule, not cost, is prefab's real advantage.
- *Housing affordability:* in expensive metros the binding constraint is land-use/zoning regulation, not construction technology; cheaper construction methods cannot fix affordability where land (driven by allowed density) is 70%+ of cost.
- *Infrastructure cost (nuclear, transit, transmission):* America's building problem is heavily institutional — permitting, environmental proceduralism, lost learning from one-off projects, and design churn — rather than a lack of engineering capability.

### Influences

His analytical toolkit draws on the economics of learning/experience curves (the progress-curve literature), and his housing-cost work is co-authored with University of Chicago economist Chad Syverson (the NBER/SSRN paper "Building Costs and House Prices," 2025); he also cites Goolsbee and Syverson (2025) on the ~50% post-1960s productivity decline and the Allen output-mix analysis. His historical case studies (Lustron, Operation Breakthrough, Sears Catalog Homes, Toyota Home) function as a personal canon of industrialization attempts. Institutionally he is shaped by the "abundance"/progress-studies milieu around the Institute for Progress and the broader build-more, permitting-reform policy community.
