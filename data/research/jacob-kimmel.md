## Broad research

# Jacob Kimmel — Reference Dossier

Jacob C. Kimmel is a computational and stem-cell biologist who co-founded NewLimit, a biotechnology company developing epigenetic-reprogramming medicines to treat aging and age-related disease, where he serves as co-founder and CEO/President. He earned his PhD (2015–2018) in the Department of Biochemistry & Biophysics at UC San Francisco, working within the NSF Center for Cellular Construction and advised jointly by cell biologist Wallace Marshall and stem-cell biologist Andrew Brack; his thesis, "Inferring stem cell state from cell behavior," combined timelapse imaging, single-cell genomics, and machine learning to read cell state from dynamics. He then moved to Calico Life Sciences in South San Francisco, rising from Data Scientist (2018–2020) to Computational Fellow (2020–2021) to Principal Investigator in R&D (2021–2022), where he led a program on "repurposing developmental programs to restore youthful gene expression in old cells." He left to co-found NewLimit in 2021–2022 with Coinbase co-founder Brian Armstrong and bioengineer/investor Blake Byers. By Google Scholar he has roughly 739 citations and an h-index of 9, with a body of work concentrated on single-cell aging atlases, stem-cell dynamics, and methods/screens for partial cellular reprogramming. He also maintains personal essays at jck.bio and writes on the NewLimit blog, and lists interests including machine learning, carbon capture, hiking, and music.

## The core scientific thesis: aging is "plastic at the cellular level"

Kimmel's central organizing claim, repeated across his essays and NewLimit's public materials, is that aging is not a one-way accumulation of irreversible damage but is at least partly *plastic* — that the cellular phenotypes of old age can be pushed back toward youthful states by changing gene-regulatory (epigenetic) programs rather than by repairing physical damage. The reasoning rests on a now-famous existence proof: Shinya Yamanaka's 2006 demonstration that four transcription factors (Sox2, Oct4, Klf4, Myc; "SOKM" or OSKM/"Yamanaka factors") can convert a fully differentiated adult cell into an induced pluripotent stem cell, erasing not only cell identity but also many molecular hallmarks of age. If forced expression of a handful of regulators can reset a cell's developmental and apparent biological age, Kimmel argues, then aging phenotypes are downstream of a reconfigurable regulatory state, and the engineering problem is to capture the rejuvenation while avoiding the dangerous loss of identity.

The mechanistic premise — that age is substantially encoded in the epigenome and is therefore in principle reversible — aligns closely with the "information theory of aging" articulated most prominently by David Sinclair (Nature Aging, 2023; and the 2023 Cell paper "Loss of epigenetic information as a cause of mammalian aging"), which holds that aging is driven by progressive loss/noise of youthful epigenetic information and that reprogramming retrieves it. Kimmel's own work is more empirically cautious than Sinclair's strong causal framing: his contribution is less a theory of *why* aging happens than a measurement-driven program for *whether and how* reprogramming-style interventions can be made to work safely. This is itself a tension in the field — the strongest counter-position, voiced by critics of Sinclair's 2023 work (a 2024 Cell correspondence titled "The information theory of aging has not been tested," and a widely circulated LessWrong critique of the Cell paper), is that the headline experiments may reflect cell killing and inflammatory damage (e.g., genotoxic effects of the I-PpoI system) rather than a clean epigenetic-information loss, and that the damage/"hardware" view of aging has not been displaced. Kimmel's stance threads this needle: he treats reprogramming's rejuvenating effects as an empirical fact worth engineering around, without committing to the contested claim that epigenetic-information loss is *the* cause of aging.

## Foundational work: cell identity, not tissue, governs how cells age

Kimmel's most-cited paper, "Murine single-cell RNA-seq reveals cell-identity- and tissue-specific trajectories of aging" (Genome Research, 2019; ~200 citations; with Penland, Rubinstein, Hendrickson, D.R. Kelley and colleagues at Calico), is a single-cell atlas of aging built by profiling individual cells from kidney, lung, and spleen of young and old C57Bl/6 mice. The central finding is that aging is highly cell-type specific: "most of the transcriptional changes are specific to one or just a few cell types," with only a small shared core, and that "cell type explains most of the variation in both aging trajectories and magnitudes, while tissue environment is a minority influence." Methodologically the paper is notable for treating annotation and quantification as machine-learning problems: he trained a deep neural network on the expert-annotated *Tabula Muris* corpus and used it to classify cell types in his own aging data, and he used optimal-transport (Wasserstein) distances to quantify the *magnitude* of aging per cell type, finding multi-fold differences across cell types. The conceptual payoff for his later therapeutic program is direct: if cells of different identities age along different trajectories and to different degrees, then rejuvenation interventions must be cell-type-aware, and a single universal intervention is unlikely.

## Stem-cell dynamics: reading state from behavior

A second strand of Kimmel's work treats cell *state* as something inferable from dynamics rather than only from a static snapshot. In "Inferring cell state by quantitative motility analysis reveals a dynamic state system and broken detailed balance" (PLoS Computational Biology, 2018; with A.Y. Chang, Brack, Marshall) he used quantitative motility/timelapse analysis to show that stem cells occupy a dynamic state system that violates detailed balance — i.e., the system is out of thermodynamic equilibrium, with directional cycling between states rather than reversible back-and-forth. In the related "Differentiation reveals the plasticity of age-related change in murine muscle progenitors" / "Aging induces aberrant state transition kinetics in murine muscle stem cells" (Development / Cell Reports, ~2020–2021; with Hwang, Scaramozza, Marshall, Brack), he applied single-cell RNA-seq plus RNA-velocity and dynamical-systems analysis to muscle and mesenchymal stem cells before and after a differentiation challenge. The key result is that aged muscle stem cells follow broadly the same differentiation *trajectory* as young cells but stall near a commitment "decision point" — RNA-velocity divergence analysis identified an unstable "repulsor"/energy-barrier state separating stem and committed fates, and old cells get stuck there. Crucially, differentiation *unmasked* latent age-related defects that were invisible in the resting state, reinforcing the theme that aging phenotypes are conditional and trajectory-dependent — and, in his framing, plastic.

## The signature result: rejuvenation can be decoupled from identity loss

Kimmel's most important conceptual contribution to reprogramming therapeutics is the claim that *rejuvenation and loss of cell identity are separable axes*. In "Diverse partial reprogramming strategies restore youthful gene expression and transiently suppress cell identity" (Cell Systems, 2022; ~90 citations; with A.E. Roux, Zhang, Paw and colleagues), the group used single-cell genomics to map cells across reprogramming trajectories and screened combinations of the Yamanaka factors. Two findings anchor his later strategy. First, "the transcriptional effects of partial reprogramming scaled with the number of unique factors delivered," and "effects from combinations of three factors were highly similar to the full Yamanaka factor set," implying no single factor is strictly required. Second, and more consequential, he measured an "aging signature" and a "cell-identity" program separately and found that "almost all combinations significantly reduced the expression of the aging signature," yet "the degree of rejuvenation and identity suppression were not significantly correlated, suggesting these effects can be decoupled." This decoupling directly attacks the assumption that you must dedifferentiate a cell (risking identity loss and tumors) to make it young.

The same paper supplies his safety-relevant caveat: single-cell resolution revealed a small subpopulation activating the pluripotency factor *Nanog*, prompting his warning that "even transient activation of pluripotency programs poses a neoplastic risk," and that bulk measurements miss this rare-but-dangerous state. It also gestures at an escape route from pluripotency entirely — inspired by amphibian limb regeneration, transient expression of the mesodermal factor *Msx1* (outside the pluripotency program) improved myogenic gene expression, suggesting rejuvenation via non-pluripotency programs.

## Scaling reprogramming into an engineering discipline (Partial-seq and the NewLimit thesis)

The NewLimit program operationalizes the "decoupling" insight at scale. In "Partial reprogramming reverses features of aging in human T lymphocytes" (2024), Kimmel and colleagues built **Partial-seq**, a pooled single-cell screen that measures the gene-expression effect of partial reprogramming across thousands of transcription-factor sets simultaneously — screening **over 4,000 TF sets** in aged human T cells. Headline findings: "many TFs beyond pluripotency factors restored youthful gene expression and cell states, without abolishing T cell identity"; "TF combinations were more likely than individual factors to reverse cell age"; and, importantly, "many of these effects persisted after TF activation ceased," i.e., the rejuvenation can be durable rather than requiring continuous expression. T cells were chosen as a "keystone" immune cell type whose decline drives age-related immune dysfunction.

The platform logic is laid out in his essays "Rejuvenation By Reprogramming," "Designing reprogramming therapies," and NewLimit's "Developing reprogramming therapies." The argument: full pluripotent reprogramming "erases the identity and function of adult cells and is not a plausible therapy"; the therapeutic window is narrow because "beneficial and dangerous doses of these pluripotent reprogramming interventions are often only 2-fold different"; and the search space is astronomically large — with ~50 candidate factors there are "over 10,000,000 possible combinations of six or fewer factors," meaning "there are far more possible reprogramming strategies than we can ever test experimentally." His proposed solution is to combine "single cell genomics, pooled perturbation screening, and machine learning" so that models trained on prior screens predict the outcomes of untested factor sets — turning reprogramming design "from an artistic endeavor into an engineering discipline." This is an explicit bet on data-and-compute-driven biology: build a measurement flywheel, learn a predictive model of the perturbation→phenotype map, and navigate the combinatorial space rather than brute-forcing it.

NewLimit's commercial trajectory reflects this thesis. The company reported a prototype medicine that reverses cell age in aged human liver cells, with a first liver-focused program aimed at faster healing after injury, protection against dietary damage, and accelerated alcohol recovery; it raised a $435M round led by Founders Fund (closed reported June 2026), with Thrive Capital, Greenoaks, Quiet Capital, and returning investors including Kleiner Perkins, Nat Friedman/Daniel Gross, Valor, and Eli Lilly Ventures, and stated an intent to begin a first clinical trial in 2027.

## Major debates and where Kimmel sits

**Is reprogramming-based rejuvenation safe enough to be a medicine?** The dominant criticism of the whole field — articulated in reviews such as Paine et al. (Aging Cell, 2024) and longevity-research commentary — is teratoma/cancer risk: Yamanaka factors are pro-oncogenic (especially Myc and Klf4), and mis-timed or over-dosed reprogramming in vivo produces teratomas. Kimmel does not dispute this; he reframes it as the central engineering constraint and offers three answers grounded in his data: (1) use *partial*, transient reprogramming and stop short of pluripotency; (2) exploit the rejuvenation/identity decoupling so you can choose factor sets that rejuvenate without driving cells toward a pluripotent (tumor-prone) state; and (3) search beyond the Yamanaka factors entirely (the Partial-seq result that non-pluripotency TFs rejuvenate, and the *Msx1* result). The unresolved tension: his own single-cell work shows rare *Nanog*-activating cells even under "partial" conditions, so the safety argument depends on rare-cell control that bulk assays cannot guarantee — a point his methods are designed to surface but that remains a live risk for any in-vivo therapy.

**Epigenetic "software" vs. damage "hardware" as the cause of aging.** The pioneers Kimmel builds on — Yamanaka, and Juan Carlos Izpisúa Belmonte (Salk, 2016 in-vivo cyclic reprogramming) — established that reprogramming reverses age markers; Sinclair pushed the strong claim that *epigenetic information loss is the cause* of aging. Critics (the 2024 Cell "has not been tested" correspondence; the LessWrong critique of Sinclair's 2023 paper) argue the causal experiments may instead reflect cell killing and inflammation, and that genomic/molecular damage remains a leading driver. Kimmel's position is pragmatic rather than doctrinal: his published claims are about *demonstrable plasticity of phenotype* (old cells can be made to express youthful programs and recover function), which holds regardless of whether epigenetic noise is the ultimate cause. This lets him benefit from the reprogramming evidence base without staking the company on the contested causal theory — but it leaves open the deeper question his platform implicitly assumes: if some aging is true molecular damage ("hardware"), reprogramming the "software" may rejuvenate expression while leaving the damage in place, capping how durable the benefit can be. His T-cell finding that effects "persisted after TF activation ceased" is his best empirical counter to that worry.

**Can ontogeny be reversed, and how far?** In public talks (e.g., Lifespan.io, "Reversing Cell Age, Suppressing Cell Identity, Can Ontogeny Be Reversed?") he frames the open scientific question as whether developmental aging can be run backward in a controlled way, and whether the rejuvenation observed in vitro translates to function in vivo and at organismal scale. The skeptical counter from gerontology is that cell-level rejuvenation signatures (and epigenetic-clock reversals) are not the same as restored tissue function or extended healthspan, and that many interventions improve markers without proven functional or lifespan benefit. Kimmel's emphasis on *functional* readouts (differentiation competence in muscle progenitors; immune-relevant T-cell states) and on durable, identity-preserving rejuvenation is his attempt to answer this, but the organismal translation remains unproven and is the explicit purpose of NewLimit's planned 2027 trial.

## Influences and interlocutors

Direct scientific lineage: PhD advisors **Wallace Marshall** (quantitative cell biology, the "cell as computer/dynamical system" framing visible in his motility and RNA-velocity work) and **Andrew Brack** (muscle stem-cell aging). At Calico he collaborated closely with computational biologist **David R. Kelley** (deep learning for genomics) and with **A.E. Roux** on the partial-reprogramming work; a 2017 internship at **IBM Research** under **Simone Bianco** shaped his deep-learning-for-imaging methods. Intellectual antecedents he builds on or argues against include **Shinya Yamanaka** (the reprogramming existence proof), **Juan Carlos Izpisúa Belmonte** (in-vivo partial reprogramming), and **David Sinclair** (information theory of aging) on one side, and the field's safety-and-causation skeptics (teratoma-risk reviewers; the "not been tested" critics) on the other. His co-founders **Brian Armstrong** and **Blake Byers** are interlocutors on the company-building and capital side rather than the science.
