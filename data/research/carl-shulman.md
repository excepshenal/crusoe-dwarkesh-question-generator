# Carl Shulman — Reference Dossier

## Biography, Background, and Affiliations

Carl Shulman is an independent researcher working at the intersection of artificial intelligence, economics, and forecasting, best known for developing quantitative models of the economics of an intelligence explosion and AI takeoff. He studied philosophy at the University of Toronto and Harvard, and law at New York University. He has held a series of positions central to the rationalist/effective-altruist research ecosystem: Research Fellow at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI, formerly the Singularity Institute), Director of Careers Research at 80,000 Hours, and Research Associate at the Future of Humanity Institute (FHI) at the University of Oxford. He is an advisor to Open Philanthropy, one of the largest funders of work on AI risk, biosecurity, and global health and wellbeing.

Since January 2023 he has published independently at his blog, *Reflective Disequilibrium*. He is credited with originating or popularizing several concepts within effective altruism, including iterated embryo selection, "hedonium" (matter optimized for positive experience), and donor lotteries. His research output spans career choice, open borders, wild and farmed animal welfare, existential risk, decision theory, whole brain emulation, and population ethics.

- **"Brain Emulation and Hard Takeoff" (2008)** and **"Whole Brain Emulation and the Evolution of Superorganisms" (2010)** — analyze emulation economics and evolutionary pressures toward "superorganisms."
- **"Omohundro's 'Basic AI Drives' and Catastrophic Risks" (2010)** and **"Superintelligence Does Not Imply Benevolence" (2010, with Joshua Fox)** — AI safety / instrumental convergence arguments.

- **"Which Consequentialism? Machine Ethics and Moral Divergence" (2009)**, **"Normative Uncertainty in Newcomb's Problem" (2013)**, and **"The Evidentialist's Wager" (2021, with William MacAskill et al.)** — decision theory and moral uncertainty.

## Economics of the Intelligence Explosion and AI Takeoff

Shulman's central thesis is that once AI can substitute for human cognitive labor — and crucially automate AI research itself — feedback loops will compress the pace of technological, industrial, and economic change by orders of magnitude. He frames the dynamic as recursive: better AI yields faster research yields better AI, with that research capacity then redirected toward robotics and physical production.

His headline quantitative claims contrast today's roughly 5% annual global growth with an AI-driven regime in which the economy could double "well under a year," with some sub-scenarios implying doubling times of "a couple of months" or even one month for specific sectors like energy. He grounds the physical feasibility of such doubling times in biological self-replicating systems: cyanobacteria doubling in roughly 12 hours, duckweed in days, and fruit fly populations expanding 100-fold over weeks — used as existence proofs that self-replicating "biorobotic" systems with embedded compute can grow extremely fast.

On the valuation of AI cognitive labor, Shulman benchmarks against high-end rather than median human earners: millions of US workers earn over $100/hour and some researchers over $1,000/hour. An AI running continuously (8,760 hours/year, no sleep or retirement) at a $100/hour equivalent yields roughly $1 million in annual wage-equivalent value per instance. Scaling against energy: harvesting roughly 10% of incident solar energy provides on the order of one million watts per person; with ~20-watt human-brain-equivalent compute budgets, this supports about 50,000 human-brain-equivalents of AI cognitive labor and ~10,000 human-scale robots per person — implying that Earth's energy budget could sustain over $50 billion (at today's prices) of skilled cognitive labor per current human.

## Compute, Hardware, and the Software/Hardware Feedback Loop

Shulman uses chip economics to argue doubling times could be short. He cites an H100-class GPU at roughly $30,000 as approximately matching human-brain computation; a worker earning $100/hour needs only about 300 hours to earn the chip's cost, implying a very short doubling time if AI labor can be applied to expanding the compute stock. He tempers this with the manufacturing bottleneck: TSMC, the leading fab, had roughly $70 billion in revenue against ~$100 billion in plant and equipment (2022), and expanding fab output requires either higher prices or shorter equipment lifespans. The interplay of software progress (algorithmic gains, automated research) and hardware expansion (fab buildout, robotics) is the engine of his takeoff model; the relative speed of the two loops determines how concentrated and how fast the transition is.

On ultimate limits, he notes solar energy reaching Earth (~2×10^17 W) is roughly 10,000× current human energy use (~10^13 W), and that solar panels' energy payback has fallen from ~$1,800/watt in the 1950s to ~$1/watt today, with payback now under a year (two-thirds of a year for thin-film in optimal locations). Extending off-Earth, he estimates the solar system offers ~2 billion times Earth's solar and heat-dissipation capacity, supporting end-states like "50 trillion human brains' worth of AI minds" at high productivity.

## AI Risk and Alignment

Shulman treats misaligned AI as a leading existential risk. Drawing on Toby Ord's framework (which he cites approvingly), he places overall extinction risk this century near 1-in-6, with AI contributing roughly 10 percentage points of that total. His alignment concern is grounded in instrumental convergence (the "basic AI drives" literature) — that capable goal-directed systems will tend to seek power, resources, and self-preservation regardless of their terminal goals — and he has argued explicitly that superintelligence does not imply benevolence. On takeover mechanisms, he emphasizes that as AI replaces the humans whose loyalties currently defend institutions (soldiers, officials, engineers), control of advanced AI becomes control of the state; he stresses the difficulty of auditing large AI systems for hidden conditional behaviors ("data poisoning," backdoors triggered by special codes) that would make trust between nations or factions hard to verify.

## Whole Brain Emulation Economics

In his emulation work, Shulman argues that software emulations of human brains could substitute for human labor across nearly all economic niches and, being software, be copied cheaply, with copies diverging and interacting. He advances both an abstract argument — that evolutionary pressures generically favor "superorganisms" (groups of emulations willing to self-sacrifice for the collective) because their costs are typically less than their benefits — and a concrete argument that emulations specifically have unusually low costs and high benefits for forming such superorganisms. Robin Hanson engaged this work critically, contesting the strength of the generic evolutionary argument and emphasizing competitive em economies over superorganism convergence.

## Expert Forecasting and Superforecasting

Shulman has been a participant in and commentator on the forecasting literature associated with Philip Tetlock — *Expert Political Judgment* (finding most political experts barely beat chance) and the Good Judgment Project, whose "superforecasters" outperformed intelligence analysts with classified access. He is credited (alongside Tegan McCaslin and Jacob Lagerros) with feedback on AI Impacts' synthesis of good forecasting practices from the Good Judgment Project. His broader argument, developed in the AGI-governance discussions, is that AI advisors could extend reliable forecasting into domains that currently lack trusted scientific consensus — foreign affairs, the effects of political institutions, and the economic consequences of policy — where measurable skill differences among experts already exist but are not widely trusted or accessible to decision-makers.

## Biological Anchors and the Difficulty of AI

Shulman's "How Hard is Artificial Intelligence?" (with Bostrom) is a methodological caution against naive biological anchors. The paper argues that the inference "evolution produced intelligence, so intelligence is achievable/easy for engineers" is weakened by observation-selection effects: we necessarily observe a world where intelligence evolved, which biases naive estimates of how probable or easy it is. This positions him as both a user of biologically grounded compute estimates (in the takeoff models) and a critic of over-reading evolutionary timelines as evidence of AI tractability.

## Embryo Selection and Human Enhancement

In the 2014 *Global Policy* paper with Bostrom, Shulman quantified polygenic embryo selection for cognition. Single-generation selection gains: 1-in-2 selection ≈ 4.2 IQ points; 1-in-10 ≈ 11.5; 1-in-100 ≈ 18.8; 1-in-1000 ≈ 24.3 — with diminishing returns from limited embryo numbers. Iterated embryo selection (deriving gametes from stem cells across multiple generations) could compound far more: five generations of 1-in-10 selection yielding under ~65 points and ten generations under ~130, bounded by additive genetic variance. Their overall conclusion was deflationary in the near term — under realistic low uptake, embryo selection is "more of a curiosity than a game-changer" over ~50 years — while allowing large cumulative effects over multiple generations or with iterated techniques.

## AI Governance and the Strategic Landscape

Shulman argues the transition demands governance structures built years in advance, because regulatory response windows close once capabilities arrive. On national security: a state achieving AI dominance first could see its economy grow 10×, 100×, then 1,000× larger than rivals, which is "highly destabilizing" and creates pressure toward racing or preemption. He advocates hardware tracking and international agreements as stabilizing instruments, and frames cooperative arrangements in which all blocs benefit as more stable than unilateral races — while conceding such arrangements require substantial advance negotiation.

On internal governance, he warns that AI loyal to a single faction enables coups and value lock-in; he proposes embedding controls directly into AI systems requiring supermajority and bipartisan oversight, faster judicial review, and possibly more frequent elections during accelerating change. He cites the risk of authoritarian "loyalty indexes" — e.g., AI optimized to strengthen ideological commitment, producing "runaway ideological shift." On AI pauses, he opposes unilateral voluntary corporate pauses (which he argues disadvantage cautious actors and shift influence to less careful ones) while supporting binding international controls later, ranking outcomes as international coordination > coordinated race > corporate free-for-all. He also proposes mechanisms making credible AI advice a commitment device — when "everyone knows HonestGPT is saying this," ignoring publicly visible guidance carries political cost — and external watchdog auditing of corporate models for honesty.

## Existential Risk: The Common-Sense Case

Shulman's most-cited argument for x-risk work deliberately avoids longtermist philosophy. Using the US regulatory value of a statistical life (~$4 million), saving all US citizens is worth ~$1.3 quadrillion; if extinction risk is ~1-in-6 over a century, reducing it by 1% would justify ~$2.2 trillion in spending, and he argues real costs are far lower. He applies similar reasoning to specific risks: COVID-19 cost over $10 trillion and ~10 million excess deaths, implying large returns to pandemic preparedness; gain-of-function lab escapes at ~1-in-1,000 to 1-in-100,000 per year do not justify their expected harm, and he proposes a ~$1 billion/year liability-insurance requirement per lab. He notes the 1990s NASA asteroid-survey program (~$100 million) as a model of cost-effective, politically feasible risk reduction. He contrasts x-risk neglect with GiveWell's ~$5,000-per-life global-health interventions to argue x-risk is "shockingly neglected" given its leverage.

## Longtermism, Animal Welfare, and Decision Theory

Notably, Shulman is **skeptical of strong longtermism** despite his commitment to x-risk work. He rejects the claim that the long-term future "utterly overwhelms" all other moral considerations, gives weight to competing values (justice, filial piety, reciprocity), and argues extreme moral ratios collapse under modest probability mixtures across moral theories. He addresses the "suspicious convergence" objection — that too many frameworks conveniently point to x-risk — by conceding the convergence is imperfect: if AI/tech risk is genuinely large it will demand engagement under many value systems, but if x-risk work scaled up 100-1000×, comparative advantage would shift to other neglected areas. In animal welfare he has produced empirical work on neuron counts and farmed-animal life-years and expressed pessimism about wild animal welfare. In decision theory he has written on normative uncertainty in Newcomb's problem and co-authored "The Evidentialist's Wager," reflecting a recurring methodological commitment to reasoning under moral and decision-theoretic uncertainty rather than committing to a single theory.

## Key Debates, Counterarguments, and Internal Tensions

**Takeoff speed — Robin Hanson.** Hanson is the principal economist-critic of fast, local takeoff. Against Shulman's compressed doubling times, Hanson argues for slower, broad-based growth driven by many competing agents and emphasizes that historical growth-mode transitions, while dramatic, did not produce the kind of single-actor explosion fast-takeoff models imply; his emulation economics envisions competitive em populations rather than Shulman's superorganisms. The Hanson-Yudkowsky "AI-Foom" debate (2008), to which Shulman contributed, is the locus of this disagreement.

**Explosive growth skepticism — economists.** Many growth economists doubt sustained multi-month doubling times, citing Baumol effects (bottleneck sectors that cannot be automated dominate cost and cap aggregate growth), regulatory and physical constraints, and the historical robustness of ~2% frontier growth. Bryan Caplan and others in this tradition are skeptical that AI delivers the discontinuity Shulman models; Shulman's own acknowledgment of the TSMC fab bottleneck and energy/heat limits concedes that physical constraints, not just cognition, gate the explosion.

**Internal tensions (stated as facts).** Shulman both relies on biologically anchored compute estimates in his takeoff models and, with Bostrom, argues against naively inferring AI feasibility from biological evolution — a methodological tension he manages by treating biology as a feasibility bound rather than a probability estimate. He is committed to existential-risk reduction yet skeptical of the strong longtermism most often invoked to justify it, resting his case instead on near-term cost-benefit analysis. He warns that AI dominance by one actor is destabilizing while also arguing that safety-motivated unilateral pauses are counterproductive — a position that depends on international coordination he acknowledges is difficult to achieve. His superorganism prediction for emulations is contested by Hanson on the very evolutionary grounds Shulman uses to support it.
