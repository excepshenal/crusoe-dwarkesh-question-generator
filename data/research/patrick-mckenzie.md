# Patrick McKenzie (patio11) — Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from primary sources: his own blogs (kalzumeus.com, bitsaboutmoney.com), his essays, and adjacent primary materials. Patrick McKenzie writes online under the handle "patio11."

## Bio and Career Arc

Patrick McKenzie is an American software engineer and writer best known for his essays on the software business, careers, and financial infrastructure. He grew up in the United States and moved to Japan immediately after college, where he lived for years and worked for Japanese companies for roughly six years while simultaneously building a software business on nights and weekends. His long residence in Japan recurs in his writing as a vantage point on institutions, bureaucracy, and cross-cultural systems.

His first product, **Bingo Card Creator** (launched 2006), generated custom printable bingo cards for elementary-school teachers. It was a deliberately unglamorous, small "lifestyle" business that he used as a public laboratory for marketing, SEO, conversion optimization, and A/B testing. Revenue from it eventually let him quit his salaried day job in 2010; he sold the business in 2015. In parallel he built **Appointment Reminder** (circa 2010–2016), a SaaS product built on Twilio's API that sent appointment-reminder calls, texts, and emails for professional-services businesses; it supported a small team before he sold it in 2016. He also ran a consulting and education practice teaching B2B SaaS marketing, lifecycle email, and conversion testing, and was CEO of **Starfighter**, a venture that built games to identify talented programmers (it did not succeed as envisioned). He authored A/Bingo, an A/B testing library for Rails whose approach was later adapted at Khan Academy.

McKenzie joined **Stripe** around 2016–2017, where he spent roughly six years on growth and on **Stripe Atlas** (the product that incorporates startups); he has stated Atlas became responsible for "tens of percent" of all incorporations in Delaware. He moved to an advisory/senior-advisor role at Stripe in early 2023.

In 2021 he launched **Bits about Money** (bam / bitsaboutmoney.com), a free, roughly-monthly newsletter of explainers about financial infrastructure. He also hosts the **Complex Systems** podcast, which examines the technical and human factors underlying infrastructure. During the pandemic he co-founded and served as CEO of **VaccinateCA** (see below). He is also an angel investor and advisor to multiple companies.

## Major Works and Essays

**"Don't Call Yourself a Programmer, And Other Career Advice" (kalzumeus.com, 2011).** His most-cited career essay. Central arguments: engineers are typically "cost centers," not profit centers, and you should attach yourself to work that demonstrably increases revenue or reduces cost ("Nobody ever outsources Profit Centers"). Describe yourself by business accomplishments, not by the label "programmer," which makes you "an anomalously high-cost peon." Communication is your most important professional skill; he claims his own technical ability is "slightly below average" but that writing and speaking well carried his career. Most jobs are never advertised — information travels "at approximately the speed of beer" through networks. He argues people vastly overestimate competitor skill (many senior engineers fail FizzBuzz), that programming-language choice barely matters to hiring, that startup equity is usually roughly worth what you sacrificed in salary, and that appearance/grooming are cheap professional hacks. The essay closes with the claim that career will not dominate life happiness — family, faith, and hobbies usually matter more.

**"Salary Negotiation: Make More Money, Be More Valued" (kalzumeus.com, 2012).** His other canonical career essay. Core tactical rules: **never give a number first** (deflect with lines like "I'm more concerned with whether we're a mutual fit"); only negotiate after reaching "Yes-If" (agreement in principle to hire you), not during interviews; negotiate on multiple axes (vacation, equity, title, start date), not just base salary; do not appear needy, because a weak BATNA hands the employer leverage. He argues a $5,000 raise compounds to roughly $100,000 over a decade through raises and 401(k) effects, and that "negotiating never makes worthwhile offers worse" — the floor outcome is the original offer. The kalzumeus "greatest hits" page credits the essay with millions of dollars per year in aggregate engineer pay improvements.

**Bingo Card Creator / software-business writing (2006–2015).** Published annual "income reports" and essays such as "Running a Software Business on 5 Hours a Week," "Engineering Your Way to Marketing Success," and "How to Compete with Open Source." These established him in the bootstrapper/indie-SaaS community. His A/B testing method: form a "theory of mind" of the visitor, generate hypotheses, design experiments to invalidate them, fix obvious copy errors before fine-tuning, and test the entire funnel. He cites a famous "two-character" A/B test on Bingo Card Creator that was worth tens of thousands of dollars.

**"Falsehoods Programmers Believe About Names" (2010)** catalogued mistaken assumptions in name handling and spawned an entire "falsehoods programmers believe about X" genre.

**Bits about Money essays (2021–present)** are deliberately explainer-style: he has stated the newsletter "very intentionally almost never discusses anything happening uniquely in a particular week," preferring to explain why financial systems fit together. Notable entries include essays on interchange and card networks, "The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero," KYC/AML, "Banking in very uncertain times" (the March 2023 banking turmoil), "The alchemy of deposits," deposit-insurance maximization, debanking, and "Seeing like a bank."

## VaccinateCA

In January 2021 McKenzie tweeted that someone should build a civic-tech project so that residents would not each have to phone every doctor to find a vaccine. The effort coalesced immediately: a Discord server launched on January 14–15, 2021, drew roughly 70 volunteers overnight, and the team made its first 125 calls on Day 2, finding 24 sites with available vaccine. On January 21 (Day 8) the volunteers nominated McKenzie as CEO and incorporated as **Call the Shots, Inc.** The model was deliberately low-tech: volunteers called pharmacies and clinics on personal phones, logged availability in Airtable, and pushed it to a simple static website updated every few minutes from JSON. By Day 18 it supported 10 languages; it later contracted professional call centers while keeping volunteer scouts.

Key facts McKenzie drew out: California reported only about 27% of delivered shots administered by mid-January 2021 (ranked roughly 49th nationally); one caller could complete about 100 calls a day; California needed coverage of about 6,000 pharmacies; the Federal Retail Pharmacy Program eventually handled around half of US doses; and the state's own MyTurn system, which he says officials counted a success, could not actually tell where vaccine physically was (multi-site hospital systems appeared as a single address, creating phantom shortages). He estimates the project "probably saved a high four-figure number of lives." His central lesson about **state capacity**: "abundance is the only cure for scarcity… everything else merely allocates scarcity," and the US "chose, as a nation, that knowing the location of the vaccine was just not a top priority." He characterized residency restrictions and equity-driven allocation as, in effect, "redlining for justice" that left doses unused, and described the situation as a "command economy" outcome produced by missing supply visibility. The story was later written up at length ("The Story of VaccinateCA," Works in Progress / kalzumeus).

## Ideas in Depth (from primary sources)

**"The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero."** Core thesis: businesses rationally treat fraud like rent or marketing — a cost of operating — because checking is itself expensive and friction depresses legitimate revenue ("removing fields from checkout forms increases conversion rates nearly as a rule"). He states the marginal return of permitting some fraud is plausibly greater than zero, so a rational firm "should welcome greater than zero fraud." He stresses this is by design: "The very best minds in government, the financial industry, the payments industry, and business have gotten together and decided that they want this fraud to be possible." High-margin businesses (SaaS, games at 90%+ margins) tolerate more fraud to maximize acquisition; low-margin sellers (Apple, console makers) screen harder. He pegs US payments fraud at roughly $10–20 billion a year and notes US liability rules route losses cardholder → issuer → merchant automatically.

**Interchange and card networks.** Interchange is the fee paid by the merchant's acquiring bank to the cardholder's issuing bank on each card transaction, set by the networks (Visa/Mastercard) but flowing largely to issuers; it funds the rewards economy. He has argued against the common claim (advanced in outlets like The Atlantic) that the poor straightforwardly subsidize the rich's credit-card rewards, treating it as more complicated than the slogan. He frames the financial system broadly as a "series of ledgers" and money as moving by message-passing between banks rather than by physically moving cash.

**KYC/AML and money laundering.** In "KYC and AML: Beyond the Acronyms" he argues the regimes are not really designed to make banks know customers well or to stop laundering directly; they exist "to stochastically manage crime and terrorism at the margins" by deputizing banks as a porous, overlapping filtering layer — "KYC and AML don't have to be effective in their own terms" to serve that purpose. It is regulation of process, not outcome. A key mechanism: criminals must eventually touch the banking system and lie to it, and the state prefers this because lying to banks is itself a prosecutable, easy-to-prove crime independent of the underlying offense. Money laundering is a "process crime" criminalized because it obscures the link between a crime and its proceeds; the canonical stages are placement, layering, integration. He notes openly that "the optimal amount of KYC compliance is not 100%," that the regime imposes real access costs (debanking, exclusion of the unbanked), and that "socially-aware escape hatches" could exist "if that was a priority" — society makes these tradeoffs without admitting them.

**Deposits, bank runs, and deposit insurance.** In "Banking in very uncertain times" (March 2023, around SVB) he explains banks do **maturity transformation** — "borrowing short and lending long" — holding demand deposits while owning longer-duration assets, a mismatch he argues "must exist somewhere" because the alternative (callable mortgages) would be worse. Rising rates cut asset values (roughly 1% per year of duration per 1% rate rise); he cites about $620 billion in unrealized US bank securities losses (FDIC, Feb 2023) after the most aggressive rate hikes since WWII, and notes at least one failed bank "was not a Problem Bank three weeks" prior, indicating regulators lacked early warning. He explains standard FDIC coverage ($250,000 per depositor, per account type, per institution) and how fintech "For Benefit Of" (FBO) account structures and deposit-insurance-maximization-as-a-service complicate who is actually covered — themes he extended in essays on the Synapse banking-as-a-service collapse, where customer funds were frozen because ledgers across the program could not be reconciled.

**The engineer's mindset applied to institutions.** A throughline: money and institutions are legible, debuggable systems; ordinary people (and engineers especially) can and should reason about plumbing usually treated as opaque. He also repeatedly argues for the career and civic value of writing and owning an audience — his own essays generated business, optionality, and the network that made VaccinateCA possible.

## Positions, Counterarguments, and Tensions

**Optimal fraud is non-zero.** Reasoning: friction and verification cost more than the fraud they prevent past some margin. Strongest counterargument: critics in fraud/risk and consumer-protection circles argue this framing externalizes costs onto victims and merchants and can normalize under-investment in security; bank-secrecy and victims' advocates note "acceptable" fraud is paid disproportionately by the least sophisticated. Internal tension: McKenzie simultaneously defends fraud tolerance and details how the same logic ("optimal compliance is not 100%") produces wrongful debanking and exclusion he treats as real harms.

**KYC/AML as stochastic process-regulation.** Reasoning: deputizing banks scales surveillance cheaply and creates easy prosecutions. Counterargument: civil-liberties and financial-inclusion critics (and academic AML-effectiveness researchers who estimate interdiction rates of well under 1% of laundered funds) argue the regime is enormously costly, catches little, and unjustly excludes the poor and politically disfavored — a critique McKenzie partly concedes. Tension: he describes the system as both rational-by-design and as producing debanking outcomes he criticizes elsewhere, locating the fault in unstated social priorities rather than the mechanism.

**Don't call yourself a programmer / profit-center framing.** Counterargument: engineer-bloggers (e.g., Yossi Kreinin's "Do Call Yourself a Programmer") argue the advice over-optimizes for managerial framing, undervalues craft and technical depth, and pushes engineers toward self-marketing over substance. Tension: McKenzie built his reputation partly on deep technical-marketing competence even while advising against the "programmer" identity.

**State capacity / VaccinateCA.** Reasoning: institutions "underperformed their capabilities," and a tiny volunteer team with a phone and a spreadsheet beat multibillion-dollar systems by optimizing for lives over procedure. Counterargument: defenders of public-health agencies argue equity-prioritizing allocation, privacy law, and procurement constraints reflect legitimate values and statutory limits, not mere incompetence, and that a volunteer scraper benefited from being unaccountable for errors and equity. Tension: McKenzie champions both abundance/visibility and acknowledges the volunteer effort's "command economy" limits and reliance on the very institutions (federal supply, pharmacies) he critiques.
