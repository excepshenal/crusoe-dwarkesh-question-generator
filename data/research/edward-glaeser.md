# Edward Glaeser: A Reference Dossier

## Biography and Career

Edward Ludwig Glaeser was born on May 1, 1967, in New York City, and grew up in Manhattan during the 1970s, an era when the city was visibly in fiscal and social decline. His father, Ludwig Glaeser (1930–2006), was a German-born architect and a curator at the Museum of Modern Art; his mother, Elizabeth, worked in capital markets and finance. Glaeser has repeatedly credited his father with teaching him how the built environment shapes daily life, and his mother with introducing him to economics. He has described his childhood fascination with New York's street life as the origin of his lifelong study of cities.

He attended the Collegiate School in Manhattan, earned an AB in economics from Princeton University in 1988, and completed a PhD in economics at the University of Chicago in 1992 under the supervision of José Scheinkman. He joined the Harvard University economics faculty immediately upon finishing his doctorate in 1992 and has remained there for his entire career. He holds the title of Fred and Eleanor Glimp Professor of Economics. At Harvard he has directed the Taubman Center for State and Local Government and the Rappaport Institute for Greater Boston, both at the Harvard Kennedy School, and he has been involved with the Cities Research Programme at the International Growth Centre.

Glaeser has been a research associate at the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) since the early 1990s and served as editor of the Quarterly Journal of Economics (1998–2008). He is a senior fellow at the Manhattan Institute and a contributing editor of its magazine, City Journal, where many of his policy-oriented essays have appeared; he also writes for outlets including the New York Times "Economix" blog and the Boston Globe. He was elected a Fellow of the Econometric Society (2005) and a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences (2010). Nobel laureate George Akerlof has called him "a genius," and Gary Becker remarked that before Glaeser's work, "urban economics was dried up." Glaeser is among the most heavily cited economists of his generation, with well over a hundred peer-reviewed papers spanning urban economics, public economics, political economy, and health economics.

## Major Works

**"Triumph of the City: How Our Greatest Invention Makes Us Richer, Smarter, Greener, Healthier, and Happier" (Penguin Press, 2011)** is his best-known book and a sustained argument that the city is humanity's greatest invention. Its central claim is that the value of cities lies in proximity—the elimination of the costs of moving goods, people, and especially ideas—and that density makes people more productive, more innovative, and, surprisingly, greener.

**"Survival of the City: Living and Thriving in an Age of Isolation" (Penguin Press, 2021)**, co-authored with Harvard health economist David Cutler, extends the argument into the COVID-19 era. It treats contagious disease as the oldest and "most terrible" demon of density and argues that, while urban life as a phenomenon will survive, individual cities face real risk of decline, and that deep inequities in American health care and education are the central threat to urban futures.

His foundational academic papers include **"Cities and Skills"** (Journal of Labor Economics, 2001), **"Consumer City"** with Jed Kolko and Albert Saiz (Journal of Economic Geography, 2001), **"The Rise of the Skilled City"** (2004), **"Sprawl and Urban Growth"** with Matthew Kahn (Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics, 2004), **"Why Is Manhattan So Expensive? Regulation and the Rise in House Prices"** with Joseph Gyourko and Raven Saks (Journal of Law and Economics, 2005), **"The Greenness of Cities: Carbon Dioxide Emissions and Urban Development"** with Matthew Kahn (Journal of Urban Economics, 2010), **"Learning in Cities"** (1999), and the later **"The Economic Implications of Housing Supply"** with Gyourko (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2018). With Alberto Alesina he wrote "Fighting Poverty in the US and Europe," and with Cutler and Jesse Shapiro he co-authored influential work on the economics of obesity.

## Density, Agglomeration, and Productivity

Glaeser's core empirical claim is that density raises productivity. He cites the finding that worker productivity rises by roughly 15 percent when the population of a metropolitan area doubles, a relationship economists call the agglomeration wage premium. His explanation, drawing on Alfred Marshall, is that cities are engines of learning: people acquire skills by interacting with one another, and dense settings speed up the rate of these interactions. In "Learning in Cities" and "Cities and Skills" he formalized the idea that proximity functions as an informal apprenticeship—younger workers in dense, skilled cities enjoy faster wage growth because they learn more quickly from those around them. He emphasizes that agglomeration effects are strongest in skill-intensive cities, supporting knowledge-spillover theories over explanations rooted in natural advantage or the cheap movement of physical goods.

The strongest counterargument is empirical: critics question how much of the urban wage premium reflects genuine productivity gains versus selection (more able people sorting into cities). Recent work, including a 2025 NBER study, has challenged the supply-elasticity framework by finding that income growth predicted similar house-price, quantity, and population growth regardless of a city's estimated housing-supply elasticity. Andrés Rodríguez-Pose's "left-behind places" literature directly contests the agglomeration-as-engine view, arguing that medium and small cities and lagging regions have economic potential that the dominant urban-economics framework dismisses, and that ignoring them fuels political backlash.

## Cities as Proximity That Survived the Internet

Glaeser has long argued against the "death of distance" thesis—the prediction that telecommunications would make physical cities obsolete. His position is that complex, nuanced information transmits best face-to-face, and that electronic communication and in-person contact are complements rather than substitutes: the more we connect globally, the more valuable it becomes to be physically near collaborators. In the COVID-19 aftermath he reiterated this, arguing that remote work loses something essential, that no one who endured a year of Zoom classes at Harvard could regard it as a true substitute, and that the value of cities to knowledge-intensive industries and young workers would drive recovery. He does not foresee an urban "death spiral," provided no deadlier pandemic arrives.

He also frames remote work as inequality-increasing: a Zoom-centric economy erodes the retail, leisure, and hospitality service jobs that less-educated urban workers depend on—workers who, in his phrasing, cannot "Zoom their way to economic survival." An internal tension in his record is that this strong pro-proximity stance coexists with his acknowledgment that white-collar workers can now disperse; he concedes commercial rents in New York, San Francisco, and London may fall, while predicting space will still be occupied because rents remain high enough to cover costs.

## Housing Supply Regulation and Unaffordability

Glaeser's most policy-consequential body of work argues that housing unaffordability in America's most expensive cities is primarily a problem of restricted supply, not insufficient demand or speculative bubbles. The signature evidence is the gap between home prices and the physical cost of construction. In "Why Is Manhattan So Expensive?" he and his co-authors found that Manhattan condominium prices exceeded marginal construction costs by more than 100 percent, and they argued the implicit "regulatory tax" was roughly twice as large as any genuine negative externality from new construction. In a competitive building industry with no natural barriers to entry, he reasons, prices far above construction cost can only be explained by land-use restrictions—height limits, zoning, and permitting hurdles.

His favored contrast is Houston versus the coastal cities. Between 2001 and 2008, Harris County permitted more than 200,000 single-family homes, which he credits with keeping the average owner-occupied home around $120,000 and making the metro affordable even to the "less successful." He has noted that Los Angeles construction costs about 25 percent more than Houston's, yet LA homes cost roughly 350 percent more—a gap he attributes to regulation. His prescription is summarized in his line that "the only way to provide cheap housing on a mass scale is to unleash the developers," and he favors allowing taller buildings in dense, productive places, including historic-district New York.

This thesis is heavily contested. Paul Krugman has largely endorsed the supply diagnosis (blaming zoning and height limits) but stressed that sprawling cities like Atlanta face physical limits to adding housing by expanding outward. Sharper critics—including economist Cameron Murray and various "supply skeptic" scholars surveyed by NYU's Furman Center—argue that the supply-elasticity story overstates how much new market-rate construction lowers prices for lower-income residents, and that deregulation can accelerate gentrification and displacement. An internal tension Glaeser himself names: aggressively unleashing construction in the Sunbelt rather than in dense coastal cities pushes growth toward high-carbon places, working against his own environmental argument.

## Cities Are Green

Glaeser argues that dense urban living is environmentally superior to suburban living, a claim developed empirically with Matthew Kahn in "The Greenness of Cities." Their finding is that urban residents emit substantially less carbon than suburbanites, with the city–suburb gap largest in older metros like New York. City dwellers use on average about 40 percent less energy than suburbanites. A household in a census tract with more than 10,000 people per square mile uses roughly 687 gallons of gasoline per year, versus about 1,164 gallons in lower-density areas. Fewer than a third of New Yorkers drive to work compared with about 86 percent of American commuters, and roughly 29 percent of all US public-transit commuters live in New York's five boroughs. In "Triumph of the City" he memorably likens a New York apartment's environmental footprint to a size-6 Jimmy Choo stiletto and the average suburban home's to a size-15 hiking boot.

A key corollary is that anti-development environmentalism is counterproductive. He documents a strong negative association between local land-use regulation and emissions: by blocking dense building in low-emission coastal areas (California has among the lowest household emissions), regulation pushes growth toward hot, sprawling, high-emission regions like Texas and Oklahoma. He has stated that "if the entire world starts looking like Houston, the planet's carbon footprint will skyrocket." The tension here is direct: his affordability argument praises Houston's permissiveness while his climate argument warns against replicating it.

## Human Capital as the Engine of Urban Growth

Glaeser's research holds that the strongest predictor of a city's long-run growth is its level of human capital, typically measured by the share of adults with college degrees. For more than a century, he finds, educated cities have grown faster than comparable cities with less human capital, a relationship robust to controls and to tests for reverse causation. He argues the mechanism is productivity, not amenity: skilled cities grow because they become more economically productive, and especially because skilled places adapt better to economic shocks—the skills-growth link is strongest in declining regions. This is why he sees concentrating educated people as the key to a city's ability to pivot into new industries such as technology and finance.

## The Consumer City and Urban Amenities

In "Consumer City," Glaeser, Kolko, and Saiz challenged the traditional view that cities offer production advantages but consumption disadvantages. They argued that density's role in facilitating consumption—restaurants, culture, services, social matching—is large and understudied. Empirically, high-amenity cities grew faster than low-amenity cities, and urban rents rose faster than urban wages, which they read as evidence that demand for urban living rose for reasons beyond wages. The companion observation is reverse commuting and the willingness of high earners to pay premiums to live centrally. The amenity interpretation sits in tension with his human-capital-productivity story; Glaeser acknowledges both interpretations of why skilled places thrive, attributing the historical growth pattern mainly to productivity while granting that amenities and good schools also draw the skilled.

## Poverty, Inequality, and the Meaning of Urban Success

Glaeser argues that high urban poverty rates are a sign of cities' success, not failure: cities don't make people poor, they attract poor people by offering opportunity, transit, and services unavailable in rural areas. In his framing, "the flow of less advantaged people into cities from Rio to Rotterdam demonstrates urban strength, not weakness." He stresses an important caveat—this is benign only if upward mobility actually works, so that poverty for any individual is short-lived. With David Cutler, his earlier research on segregation found its effects so harmful to Black Americans that reducing it could meaningfully narrow racial wage and education gaps. The strongest counter from equity critics is that celebrating in-migration of the poor can rationalize persistent urban inequality and that pro-development policies he favors may displace the very poor he says cities serve.

## Declining Cities: People, Not Places

Glaeser is a prominent skeptic of place-based "save Detroit" policies. His work on urban decline and durable housing notes that eight of the fifteen largest US cities in 1950, Detroit among them, lost population in every subsequent decade, many shedding more than a third of their residents, and that durable housing keeps shrinking cities populated long after their economic rationale fades—home values fall below construction cost yet the structures persist. His policy conclusion follows the traditional economist's preference to "help poor people, not poor places," because subsidizing struggling locations can trap residents rather than enabling them to move toward opportunity. He has cautioned against pouring infrastructure money into declining cities and favored investing in skills and people. An internal tension he has acknowledged: he allows that place-based intervention may be justified in some heartland areas with very low labor-force participation, a partial softening of his general anti-place-based stance.

## Relationship to Jane Jacobs

Glaeser credits Jane Jacobs for her insights into the value of urban density and street-level interaction, and her influence runs through his celebration of proximity. He departs sharply, however, on built form: where Jacobs championed low-rise, organically aged neighborhoods and opposed large-scale redevelopment, Glaeser argues that preservation and height restrictions choke supply and drive up prices, and that not every neighborhood can or should be preserved like Greenwich Village. Critics note that Jacobs herself was aware of gentrification dynamics and that Glaeser's reading of her can be selective. The Jacobs comparison crystallizes the central tension in his thought between the preservationist instinct that protects beloved urban fabric and the growth imperative that demands new construction.
