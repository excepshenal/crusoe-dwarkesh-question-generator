# François Chollet — Blind Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled entirely from Chollet's prior public record: his 2019 paper "On the Measure of Intelligence," the ARC-AGI benchmark and ARC Prize results, the Keras documentation and ecosystem, his book "Deep Learning with Python," the Ndea launch materials, and his public writing and posts. Numbers and quotations are drawn from primary sources cited at the end.

## Biography and Career at Google

François Chollet is a French software engineer and AI researcher, born October 20, 1989. He earned a Diplôme d'Ingénieur (Master of Engineering) in 2012 from ENSTA Paris, a constituent school of the Institut Polytechnique de Paris. He released the Keras deep-learning library in March 2015 and joined Google shortly afterward, where he worked for more than nine years before departing in November 2024.

At Google his research spanned computer vision, the application of machine learning to formal reasoning and abstraction, and the broader question of how to achieve greater generality in AI systems. His most-cited technical contribution from that period is the 2017 paper "Xception: Deep Learning with Depthwise Separable Convolutions," which reframed the Inception architecture as an extreme form of depthwise-separable convolution; it is among the most-cited papers in the CVPR proceedings, with more than 18,000 citations. He was named to TIME's inaugural list of the 100 most influential people in AI in September 2024, and won the Global Swiss AI Award in December 2021.

## Keras, TensorFlow, and Developer Tooling

Keras, released in 2015, became the dominant high-level API for building neural networks in Python, valued for a design philosophy that prioritized ease of use, modularity, and minimizing the steps required to go from idea to running model. As deep learning expanded in 2015–2016, Keras became the most common on-ramp for newcomers to the field. Keras was subsequently adopted as the official high-level API of TensorFlow (`tf.keras`), tightly integrating Chollet's framework into Google's flagship deep-learning platform and giving it a user base estimated in the millions of developers.

In 2023 Keras was re-architected as Keras 3 (also marketed as "Keras Core"), a multi-backend rewrite able to run on top of TensorFlow, PyTorch, and JAX — positioning the library as a framework-agnostic interface rather than being tied to a single backend. This evolution preserved Keras's original thesis (a simple, consistent API for practitioners) while decoupling it from the framework competition beneath it.

A tension running through Chollet's public profile is that his best-known and most widely used work — Keras and TensorFlow tooling — sits squarely within the deep-learning paradigm whose limits he most forcefully argues against in his research on intelligence. He has framed these as complementary rather than contradictory: deep learning is, in his account, an extraordinarily powerful tool for perception and pattern interpolation, but not a sufficient substrate for general intelligence.

## "Deep Learning with Python" (2017 / 2021 / 2025)

Chollet authored "Deep Learning with Python," published by Manning. The first edition appeared in 2017, a substantially expanded second edition in 2021, and a third edition in 2025 (co-authored with Matthew Watson) that added coverage of generative AI, Keras 3, PyTorch, and JAX. The book has sold over 100,000 copies and is one of the most widely used practitioner introductions to the field. It teaches deep learning hands-on through Keras and emphasizes intuition over heavy mathematics. A related title, "Deep Learning with R," was co-authored with collaborators including Tomasz Kalinowski. Even within this practitioner-facing book, Chollet repeatedly distinguishes what deep models do well (learning continuous geometric transformations of data) from the kinds of abstract reasoning and generalization he argues they cannot reach.

## "On the Measure of Intelligence" (2019)

Chollet's central theoretical work is "On the Measure of Intelligence," posted to arXiv on November 5, 2019 (arXiv:1911.01547). Its core argument is that the AI community has conflated *skill at a task* with *intelligence*. The paper states: "we argue that solely measuring skill at any given task falls short of measuring intelligence, because skill is heavily modulated by prior knowledge and experience: unlimited priors or unlimited training data allow experimenters to 'buy' arbitrary levels of skills for a system, in a way that masks the system's own generalization power." On this view, a chess engine or a Go program demonstrates skill that says little about general intelligence, because performance was purchased through enormous task-specific priors and training.

Drawing on Algorithmic Information Theory, Chollet offers a formal definition: "The intelligence of a system is a measure of its skill-acquisition efficiency over a scope of tasks, with respect to priors, experience, and generalization difficulty." Intelligence, in this framing, is not a stock of capabilities but a *rate* — how efficiently a system converts priors and limited experience into skill on tasks it was not built for. The definition foregrounds four variables: **scope** (the breadth of tasks), **generalization difficulty** (how far a solution must reach beyond what was given), **priors** (innate or built-in knowledge), and **experience** (exposure/training data). High measured skill achieved through large priors or large experience should be *discounted* when estimating intelligence.

The paper distinguishes degrees of generalization: from no generalization, to local generalization (robustness within a known distribution), to broad generalization (handling a wide distribution of tasks within a domain), to extreme generalization (handling novelty across unrelated domains) — with human-level "developer-aware generalization" as the target. It is explicitly influenced by psychometrics and the theory of fluid intelligence (the capacity to reason and solve novel problems independent of acquired knowledge), and by developmental psychology's account of **Core Knowledge**: a small set of innate priors that humans, including young children, possess. Chollet identifies four such systems as the only assumed priors for his benchmark: **objectness and elementary physics** (object cohesion, persistence, contact), **agentness and goal-directedness**, **natural numbers and elementary arithmetic** (counting, basic integer operations), and **basic geometry and topology** (symmetry, lines, connectivity, containment). The thesis is that a fair intelligence test must specify priors explicitly and then measure generalization *given only those priors*.

## The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC) / ARC-AGI-1

To operationalize the definition, the paper introduces the **Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus (ARC)**, later renamed ARC-AGI-1. Each task presents a handful of input–output example pairs rendered as colored grids (up to 30×30 cells, 10 colors), and the solver must infer the transformation rule from typically two to five demonstrations and apply it to a new test input. The corpus contains 1,000 unique tasks: 400 public training tasks, 400 public evaluation tasks, and 200 private (held-out) test tasks. Crucially, every task is intended to be novel and to rely only on Core Knowledge priors — so a system cannot succeed by memorizing task-specific patterns or by training on near-duplicates. The public training set is designed to expose a solver to all the Core Knowledge priors needed; it is not a distribution to be fit by a statistical model.

Human performance on ARC-AGI-1 is high: studies place average human accuracy on the public evaluation set at roughly 64% (reported range ~56–69%) and on the training set at roughly 76%, while early testing of the private evaluation set by two individuals produced scores of 97% and 98%. The benchmark's headline framing is that the tasks are "easy for humans, hard for AI." For years, machine systems scored in the single digits to low tens of percent, which Chollet held up as evidence that prevailing approaches were not generalizing but interpolating.

## The ARC Prize, o3, and the Results

In June 2024 Chollet, with Mike Knoop (co-founder of Zapier), launched the **ARC Prize**, a public competition with a stated pool exceeding US$1 million, including a **$600,000 Grand Prize** reserved for an open-sourced solution exceeding **85%** on the private ARC-AGI-1 set within strict compute limits. In the **2024** Kaggle competition, 1,430 teams submitted 17,789 entries; state-of-the-art on ARC-AGI-1 rose from 33% to 55.5% over the contest. The team "MindsAI" reached 55.5% but did not open-source and was therefore ineligible; "the ARChitects" won first place at 53.5% (using test-time training), earning $25,000, with subsequent high-score awards of $10,000, $5,000, $5,000, and $5,000. Paper awards ran $50,000 / $20,000 / $5,000 plus five runner-up prizes of $2,500. The Grand Prize went unclaimed.

In December 2024 Chollet reported that OpenAI's **o3** model scored **75.7%** on the semi-private ARC-AGI-1 evaluation in a high-efficiency configuration (about $26 per task; ~$2,680 total) and **87.5%** in a low-efficiency, high-compute configuration using roughly 172× more compute (on the order of $4,560 per task). On the public set the figures were 82.8% and 91.5%. He called this "a genuine breakthrough" and "a surprising and important step-function increase," but was explicit that "I don't think o3 is AGI yet" and that "passing ARC-AGI does not equate to achieving AGI." He attributed o3's leap to a mechanism resembling "natural language program search and execution" via chains of thought — i.e., test-time recombination of knowledge — a capability he argued earlier LLMs lacked. For context, the prior frontier models scored far lower on ARC-AGI-Pub: GPT-4o ~5%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet ~14%, o1-preview ~18%.

## ARC-AGI-2 and ARC Prize 2025

On March 24, 2025, the ARC Prize Foundation released **ARC-AGI-2**, designed to remove tasks vulnerable to brute-force program search and to target symbolic interpretation, compositional reasoning, and contextual rule application. Controlled testing with over 400 participants confirmed that every evaluation task is solvable by at least two people within two attempts; the average individual human scores about **60%**, while a "panel" of at least two humans solves effectively 100%. The benchmark adds an explicit **efficiency/cost axis**, requiring solutions to demonstrate not just capability but economical use of compute. At launch, frontier reasoning models scored near zero to low single digits — o3 was reported at about 3% — underscoring the gap ARC-AGI-2 was built to expose.

**ARC Prize 2025** ran March 26 to November 3, 2025; 1,455 teams submitted 15,154 entries and 90 papers (up from 47). On ARC-AGI-2 the Kaggle leaderboard winner "NVARC" reached 24.03% (heavy use of synthetic-data generation atop test-time training), followed by the ARChitects at 16.53% and MindsAI at 12.64%. High-score awards again ran $25,000 / $10,000 / $5,000×3; paper awards $50,000 / $20,000 / $5,000 plus runners-up. The 85% Grand Prize threshold was not met, so the **$500,000 Grand Prize remained unclaimed**. Verified commercial models showed the score-versus-cost tension Chollet emphasizes: Claude Opus 4.5 (thinking, 64k) at 37.6% for $2.20/task, with higher public-leaderboard scores (e.g., Gemini 3-class systems reported in the 50–85% range) achieved only at far higher per-task cost.

## Critique of LLMs, Scaling, and Memorization vs. Reasoning

Chollet's most-publicized position is that large language models, by themselves, are not a path to general intelligence. His argument: LLMs are fundamentally interpolative — they store and retrieve a vast library of memorized programs and templates, and solve problems by fetching and lightly recombining patterns close to their training distribution. They excel where a task resembles something seen in training, but degrade on genuine novelty. In his words, "memorization is useful, but intelligence is something else." He has repeatedly argued that ARC-AGI is distinctive precisely because it is built to resist memorization, so that high scores require on-the-fly skill acquisition rather than retrieval.

A linked claim is his skepticism that *scaling alone* yields intelligence. He argues that adding parameters and data raises measured skill on benchmarks that overlap training data, but does not improve the underlying skill-acquisition efficiency; benchmark gains can therefore overstate progress toward generality. He has criticized the field's reliance on benchmarks that fail to control for priors and exposure, the core flaw "On the Measure of Intelligence" was written to address.

The strongest counterposition comes from scaling proponents. Researchers and labs associated with the scaling hypothesis — including figures such as OpenAI's leadership and Anthropic's Dario Amodei, and the empirical "scaling laws" tradition (Kaplan et al.; the Chinchilla work from DeepMind) — argue that capabilities including multi-step reasoning emerge predictably as compute, data, and parameters grow, and that emergent in-context learning already constitutes a form of rapid adaptation. The arrival of o3, which substantially beat ARC-AGI-1 using a scaled reasoning model with test-time search, is cited by critics as evidence that the LLM paradigm — augmented with inference-time compute — can attack exactly the novelty Chollet claimed it could not. Chollet's response acknowledges the result as a real breakthrough and credits a program-search-like mechanism, while maintaining that the cost profile (far above the roughly $5/task humans need) and the subsequent collapse of those same systems on ARC-AGI-2 show that brute interpolation plus search is not yet efficient general intelligence.

Two internal tensions in his position are matters of fact. First, his interpretation of o3 attributes its success to "natural-language program search," which concedes that an LLM-centered system can perform the test-time recombination he had argued LLMs lacked — narrowing the gap between his view and the scaling camp's. Second, ARC-AGI-1 itself was partially defeated within roughly five years by methods (test-time training, large-scale synthetic data, LLM-guided search) heavier on learned priors than the benchmark's spirit implied, which is part of why ARC-AGI-2 was created with explicit anti-brute-force and efficiency constraints.

## Program Synthesis vs. Deep Learning

Chollet's constructive alternative is **program synthesis**: rather than interpolating between points in a learned embedding space, a system should *search the space of discrete programs* for one that exactly explains the observed examples. Because an explicit program can capture an exact rule from very few demonstrations, this approach promises far stronger generalization with far less data — matching the few-shot structure of ARC tasks. The difficulty is combinatorial: the space of programs is vast and discrete, making naive search intractable. His proposed remedy is **deep-learning-guided program synthesis** — using neural networks' intuitive pattern recognition to guide and prune the discrete search, combining the strengths of both paradigms (perception/intuition from deep learning, exact reasoning and recombination from program search).

## Ndea

On January 15, 2025, Chollet announced **Ndea**, an AI research lab co-founded with Mike Knoop, via a post stating they were betting on "deep learning-guided program synthesis" as "a different path to build AI capable of true invention, adaptation, and innovation." Ndea's public materials describe an approach that "integrates intuitive pattern recognition with rigorous reasoning, enabling autonomous abstraction and skill acquisition from minimal data," and that "searches for discrete programs, or models, that perfectly explain observed data" rather than interpolating between embeddings. Ndea argues that conventional deep learning "excels at known tasks but crumbles when faced with open-ended problems," that its limitations are "fundamental and cannot be addressed superficially," and that the lab aims to build AI "capable of independent invention and discovery" to accelerate science. Reporting around its emergence from stealth describes roughly $43M raised and an association with Y Combinator (W2026 cohort), structured as a distributed, fully remote research team. The founding restates the through-line of Chollet's decade of public work: deep learning as a component, not the whole, of a system whose defining property is efficient generalization to genuine novelty.
