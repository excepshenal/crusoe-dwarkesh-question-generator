## Biography and Affiliations

Arthur R. Kroeber is an American China-economy analyst, the founding partner and head of research of Gavekal Dragonomics, and the author of the standard non-specialist reference *China's Economy: What Everyone Needs to Know* (Oxford University Press; 1st ed. 2016, 2nd ed. 2020). He took an A.B. in Comparative Religion from Harvard College in 1984, then spent roughly fifteen years working as a financial and economic journalist in China and South Asia before turning to research. In 2002 he co-founded the Beijing-based, China-focused research firm Dragonomics and served as editor of its flagship journal, the *China Economic Quarterly* (CEQ), a role he held through 2017. Dragonomics merged with the Hong Kong financial-services and research firm Gavekal in 2011, and since then Kroeber has been head of research for the combined Gavekal Dragonomics operation, which sells macro research to institutional investors out of Beijing and Hong Kong. He is an adjunct professor of economics at the NYU Stern School of Business (since 2018), has been an adjunct at Columbia University's School of International and Public Affairs and a senior non-resident fellow at the Brookings-Tsinghua Center in Beijing, and is a member of the Council on Foreign Relations and the National Committee on U.S.-China Relations. His most recent major publication is "The Real China Model: Beijing's Enduring Formula for Wealth and Power," co-authored with Dan Wang in *Foreign Affairs* (September/October 2025). His public positioning is consistently that of a centrist empiricist who attacks the conventional wisdom of both "China collapse" bears and uncritical bulls.

## Major Works and the Core Framing

The central work is *China's Economy: What Everyone Needs to Know*, structured around plainly posed questions and intended for a general audience. Reviewers describe its through-line as a deliberate "middle way": Kroeber launches a courteous but sustained assault on conventional wisdom from two opposing camps — the imminent-collapse doomsayers and the China-will-effortlessly-dominate optimists — and lands on an economy facing serious, possibly intractable, challenges that nonetheless do not spell collapse. The book covers the reform-and-opening growth surge, the slowdown, state-owned enterprises, local-government debt, urbanization and the hukou system, the renminbi's internationalization, the financial system, and rebalancing toward consumption and services. Alongside the book, his durable body of work is the CEQ essay tradition and Gavekal Dragonomics notes, plus op-eds (notably in the *New York Times*) and the 2025 *Foreign Affairs* essay. A recurring rhetorical signature is his framing of the Chinese state as something like "a giant venture-capital fund": it picks broad priorities, spends hundreds of billions subsidizing ruthless competition at the local level, and tolerates losing large sums on most bets so that a few pay off spectacularly.

## The Growth Model and Financial Repression

Kroeber's foundational analytical claim is that China grew through an investment-led, high-savings model made possible by **financial repression**, which he defines as a bundle of three policies: (1) regulated, artificially low deposit rates on bank savings, typically set below inflation, so that household savers implicitly subsidize borrowers; (2) a highly managed and historically undervalued exchange rate that cheapens exports; and (3) capital controls that prevent savings from fleeing abroad. The mechanism: by capturing household savings cheaply and channeling them through state-dominated banks, the state directs capital into infrastructure and industrial investment favored by its development strategy. He stresses that infrastructure and property development have been core pillars of growth, financed for decades primarily by debt raised at the local level.

His diagnosis here overlaps substantially with Michael Pettis's: both identify suppressed household consumption, negative real deposit rates, an undervalued currency, and a thin social safety net as features (not bugs) of a system that transfers income from households to producers and the state. The divergence is in emphasis and prognosis, treated in the rebalancing section below.

## Debt: Serious, Not Fatal

On debt, Kroeber is among the most prominent skeptics of the imminent-crisis thesis. He accepts that China's leverage is genuinely high — citing gross debt of roughly 290–300% of GDP (in his 2021 *NYT* op-ed, about 290% of GDP by 2021, roughly double the 2008 level), a ratio he concedes is "high for a middle-income country" and far above peers at China's income level. But he argues a systemic financial collapse is unlikely, for several structural reasons:

- **Debt is overwhelmingly domestic and renminbi-denominated**, financed out of an enormous domestic savings pool, so China faces no external creditor run of the kind that triggers emerging-market crises.
- **The financial and corporate systems are compartmentalized.** Unlike Japan, where cross-shareholding tightly linked banks and corporations, China kept these systems relatively separate, so a localized debt problem is less likely to cascade system-wide. Major banks are "pretty well capitalized," and post-2017 reforms cut shadow-banking exposure.
- **The real burden sits at local government financing vehicles (LGFVs)**, not central government, whose debt he notes has stayed near ~22% of GDP for about two decades.
- **A "double standard."** In a 2017 talk he argued analysts "invented the concept of productivity of credit to say bad stuff about China," noting that the US, Japan, and Canada all saw GDP growth decelerate while credit kept building from the 1960s–1990s without economists predicting collapse.

His reasoning leads to a characteristic formulation: China's debt is "unsustainable" in its current trajectory but not catastrophic if restructured; its main cost is a drag on growth — diverting resources from more productive uses — rather than a Lehman-style detonation. He locates the root in fiscal dysfunction: the central government pushes spending obligations and growth targets onto localities without adequate revenue transfers, so localities borrow against land and unrealistic targets, while a politically sensitive property tax remains unimplemented after decades of tax-free housing appreciation.

**Strongest counterarguments.** The bear camp — most loudly Gordon Chang, author of *The Coming Collapse of China* (2001), and analysts like Anne Stevenson-Yang (J Capital Research) — argues that opacity, hidden LGFV liabilities, and a property bubble mean the true debt is worse than reported and a reckoning is overdue. Kroeber's own admission that "we do not know precisely" the total debt level is a concession the bears press. A more analytically serious challenge comes from Michael Pettis, who agrees a sudden crisis is unlikely (because Beijing controls the banks and can roll debt forward) but argues that this very ability to suppress a crisis guarantees a prolonged, Japan-style growth grind, and that the debt cannot be "restructured away" without forcing losses onto some politically powerful claimant. The tension within Kroeber's own position is that "not fatal" and "will keep slowing growth by diverting resources" are doing a lot of work simultaneously — he treats as benign a debt overhang whose growth cost he concedes is large and persistent.

## Rebalancing Toward Consumption — the Pettis Debate

This is the most-discussed point of contrast between Kroeber and Michael Pettis, and it is a live debate in the China-economy field.

**Pettis's position:** China's low household-consumption share of GDP (household income near ~50% of GDP, among the lowest ever recorded) is the structural core of the problem. High savings is "an income problem," not a cultural preference — households receive a disproportionately small share of national income, so investment and exports must absorb the rest. Genuine rebalancing therefore requires transferring income/wealth back to households; absent that, China either runs ever-larger trade surpluses (exporting its overcapacity) or piles up unproductive investment. Pettis has argued that for China to truly rebalance, consumption would have to represent roughly 85–90% of GDP growth for a decade, a threshold China has approached only once (about 82.5% in 2023). He holds that no economy with Chinese-scale imbalances has rebalanced without a sharp growth slowdown or contraction.

**Kroeber's position:** He accepts that consumption is structurally suppressed and that rebalancing is necessary and underway, but he is markedly less alarmed about the consumption share as a master variable, and far more impressed by the productive payoff of China's investment. Where Pettis treats investment-heavy growth as inherently wasteful past a point, Kroeber argues much of the investment built a genuinely world-beating manufacturing base, and that the "investment is wasted" narrative is overstated. He attributes weak post-Covid consumer demand substantially to **lower household income** — China provided essentially no direct income support during lockdowns, unlike the US — rather than to a collapse of consumer confidence or a purely structural ceiling. In his telling, Beijing is deliberately reallocating capital out of property and old-line infrastructure and into high-tech manufacturing, accepting slower headline growth as a policy choice rather than suffering it as a failure.

**The crux of the disagreement:** Pettis sees suppressed consumption as the binding constraint that will force either painful rebalancing or stagnation; Kroeber sees it as one problem among several, outweighed by the real productivity gains of China's industrial build-out, and curable in part through ordinary income recovery and fiscal support. Commentators (e.g., Noah Smith, Asia Times) have noted the "Pettis paradigm" has gained ground in policy circles, which sharpens the contrast with Kroeber's more sanguine read on investment.

## State vs. Private Sector: "Directional Illiberalism"

Kroeber rejects the simple "the state advances, the private sector retreats" (国进民退) slogan as the whole story. His framing is a shift from **"directional liberalism"** — the pre-Xi era in which entrepreneurs invested despite weak institutions because they were betting the system was liberalizing — to **"directional illiberalism"** under Xi Jinping: a state "more controlling, much less concerned with economic growth, much more concerned with security." He notes Xi's five-year Party-congress speeches feature security and technology more than growth.

Yet he insists the picture is contradictory rather than monotonic. Private manufacturing investment has remained "very strong"; real opportunity persists in cancer drugs, robotics, and clean energy where markets are allowed to function; and he draws a generational distinction — older entrepreneurs are "extremely negative" while younger ones focus on present opportunities. His VC-state metaphor cuts both ways: the state sets direction and subsidizes sectors, but lets brutal private competition determine winners.

**Counterargument.** Bears argue the crackdowns (Ant Group/Jack Ma in 2020, the tech and tutoring sectors, "common prosperity") show political control now systematically overrides commercial logic and has permanently raised the risk premium on Chinese private enterprise. The tension in Kroeber's view is between his "directional illiberalism" thesis (a state hostile to private dynamism) and his repeated insistence that private manufacturing dynamism persists.

## Property Sector and Evergrande

In his October 2021 *New York Times* op-ed "Evergrande Isn't China's Only Economic Worry," Kroeber argued the Evergrande default would not be China's "Lehman moment" — i.e., a single firm's implosion triggering systemic collapse — because of China's "enormous pool of savings" and because the government had become adept at managing meltdowns of major firms (he cited HNA, Anbang, Baoshang Bank, and the state asset manager Huarong). His key reframing: the property squeeze, the coal-cuts power shortages, and the tech crackdown were all **government-triggered**, deliberate attempts to fix deep structural problems and break the economy's "addiction to debt," not accidents. He simultaneously warned the deleveraging would depress growth — noting a quarter where GDP rose just 0.2% versus the prior quarter — predicting a serious property slowdown (falling prices, sales, construction) that would drag growth for several quarters but not detonate the financial system. He has since emphasized a demand shortfall in lower-tier cities that may require a "government source of demand" to buy up unfinished units for conversion to rental housing.

**Counterargument.** Bears (and some mainstream economists) argue property's outsized share of GDP and household wealth — and the multi-year, ongoing nature of the downturn since 2021 — make it a slow-motion systemic event, and that the "not Lehman" framing understated the persistent drag on confidence and local-government finances.

## Manufacturing Dominance and Industrial Policy (Made in China 2025)

Kroeber's most distinctive recent emphasis is the strength of China's manufacturing **ecosystem**. He cites China at roughly 30% of global manufacturing by value, and about 20% of global manufactured-goods exports (up from ~5% two decades ago), and describes "the largest and most effective manufacturing ecosystem in the world" — industrial clusters, logistics, infrastructure, and a pipeline of roughly 10 million tertiary graduates a year — which is "very difficult either to dislodge or to replace" and rests on more than cheap labor.

On industrial policy, he argues China's success comes less from subsidizing individual national champions and more from **sector-level support plus brutal competition**: the state subsidizes an industry, then forces firms into "Hunger Games-style" competition where exporting (surviving global markets) is the selection mechanism — "you export, you win; you fail to export, you go out of business." The huge, relatively open domestic market functions almost like an export market in its competitive intensity. In clean energy, he notes China controls 70–90% of production at every stage of EV-battery and solar manufacturing, and that domestic auto output now rivals the combined output of the US, EU, and Japan.

In "The Real China Model" (*Foreign Affairs*, Sept/Oct 2025, with Dan Wang), the thesis is that the US fundamentally misunderstands Chinese success: the problem is not that China "cheats," but that it has built deep infrastructure and an ecosystem enabling rapid innovation. The policy implication is that Washington must "start to think in ecosystem terms, as China has," rather than treating Chinese competitiveness as mere subsidy or theft. The piece reads partly as a corrective to "Made in China 2025 failed / China just steals IP" narratives.

**Overcapacity tension.** Kroeber does not deny overcapacity: he notes annual new loans to manufacturing rose roughly sixfold, from ~500 billion to ~3 trillion renminbi between 2019 and 2023, creating excess capacity that consolidates very slowly because local governments protect marginal producers. This is exactly the dynamic Pettis frames as the export of China's imbalances (the "second China shock"). The tension within Kroeber's own view is between celebrating the manufacturing ecosystem as a durable strength and acknowledging that the same credit surge produces gluts and razor-thin profitability — "islands of success in an otherwise very stagnant sea," as he puts it, where high-tech manufacturing generates most productivity growth while the service sectors that employ most workers stagnate.

## Growth Trajectory, Middle-Income Trap, and Demographics

Kroeber expects trend growth to settle around **3–3.5% annually** this decade, down from pre-Covid rates above 5%, and frames this deceleration as partly natural (a large, developing economy matures) and partly a policy choice under Xi to prioritize security and technology over headline GDP. He has argued the slowdown was, in effect, "always part of Xi's plan." On whether China overtakes the US economy, he is conditional: catching up would require a significant change in political direction enabling another decade of ~5% growth, which he considers unlikely under current leadership — so he leans toward China not catching up, while rejecting determinism in either direction.

On the **middle-income trap**, his book's posture is that China's challenges are real but not destiny — the trap is a risk to be managed through continued reform (rebalancing, financial liberalization, hukou reform, urbanization), not an inevitability. On **demographics**, he treats an aging population and shrinking workforce as a genuine headwind that lowers the growth ceiling but, characteristically, not as a free-standing cause of collapse. On the **renminbi**, his long-standing view (in the book and CEQ work) has been that internationalization is real but gradual and bounded by China's unwillingness to fully open the capital account — the currency will not soon displace the dollar, and capital controls remain central to the financial-repression model.

## US-China Decoupling and Foreign Capital

Kroeber's concern about decoupling is less about goods trade than about **ideas**. He has argued that declining foreign direct investment threatens China's innovation because "with the flow of foreign capital comes a flow of ideas"; domestic capital is abundant, but the dynamism from foreign ideas is being lost. He has also questioned the international competence of Xi's economic team, noting it now has "many fewer people with any kind of international experience." In the *Foreign Affairs* framing, decoupling that severs the US from Chinese supply chains and engagement risks leaving Washington unable to compete on ecosystem terms. This sits in tension with the rest of his manufacturing-strength thesis: he argues China's ecosystem is nearly impossible to replicate, yet also warns that cutting off foreign ideas could erode Chinese dynamism — implying the ecosystem is more dependent on openness than the "unassailable" framing alone would suggest.

## Where Kroeber and Pettis Agree and Differ (Summary)

**Agree:** China's growth was built on financial repression (suppressed deposit rates, undervalued currency, capital controls) that transfers income from households to producers/state; household consumption is structurally low; a sudden Western-style financial crisis is unlikely because the state controls the banking system and debt is domestic; the current model is reaching limits.

**Differ:** Pettis treats the low consumption share as *the* binding constraint and investment past a point as fundamentally wasteful, predicting stagnation absent a politically painful income transfer to households; Kroeber treats suppressed consumption as one problem among several, is far more impressed by the productivity payoff of China's investment and manufacturing ecosystem, and reads slower growth as a manageable, partly deliberate adjustment rather than a looming wall. The debate over the consumption share of GDP, the meaning of "overcapacity," and whether China's investment was largely productive or largely wasted is the sharpest live fault line between them.
