# Kenneth Rogoff — Reference Dossier

Kenneth Saul Rogoff (born March 22, 1953, in Rochester, New York) is an American economist and chess Grandmaster, the Maurits C. Boas Chair of International Economics and Thomas D. Cabot Professor of Public Policy at Harvard University, where he has taught since 1998. He grew up in a Jewish family; his father was a radiology professor at the University of Rochester. As a teenager he dropped out of high school to pursue chess, won the U.S. Junior Championship in 1969, and lived and competed in Europe before choosing academia. He earned a B.A. and M.A. from Yale (summa cum laude, 1975) and a Ph.D. in economics from MIT (1980), with a dissertation on expectations and exchange-rate volatility. He worked as an economist at the Federal Reserve Board and the IMF, taught at Princeton, and served as Economic Counsellor and Chief Economist (Director of Research) of the International Monetary Fund from August 2001 to September 2003. He is best known for his theoretical work in international macroeconomics, his historical analysis of financial crises with Carmen Reinhart, and his policy arguments about cash, debt, and the dollar. His doctoral students include Gita Gopinath and Keyu Jin.

## Chess Career

Rogoff was a genuine elite chess player before becoming an economist. He met Bobby Fischer as a teenager; Fischer reportedly praised his "self-assured style." He became an International Master in 1974 and was awarded the International Grandmaster title in 1978 (the highest in chess). His peak FIDE rating was about 2520 (January 1977) and his peak world ranking around No. 61 (January 1980). Tournament results included third place at the 1971 World Junior Championship, second at the 1975 U.S. Championship, and a shared 13th–15th at the 1976 Biel Interzonal. He chose economics over a full-time chess career around age 18. Decades later he drew a blitz game against world champion Magnus Carlsen (2012). The chess background is frequently invoked as shaping his analytic, strategic temperament.

## International Macroeconomics: Theoretical Contributions

Before his public-facing work, Rogoff built a major reputation in academic international finance.

**The "conservative central banker" (1985).** In "The Optimal Degree of Commitment to an Intermediate Monetary Target" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, vol. 100, pp. 1169–1189), Rogoff formalized one of the foundational results in the theory of central bank independence. Building on the Kydland-Prescott / Barro-Gordon time-inconsistency problem—where a discretionary central bank produces an inflationary bias because it is tempted to exploit the short-run output-inflation tradeoff—Rogoff showed that society can be made better off by delegating monetary policy to an agent who places a *larger* weight on inflation stabilization (relative to employment) than society itself does. Mechanism: appointing such a "conservative" central banker lowers the time-consistent (average) inflation rate, but at a cost—when large supply shocks hit, the overly inflation-averse banker stabilizes output too little, raising employment variance. Using an envelope-theorem argument, Rogoff showed the optimal degree of conservatism is "large but finite," not infinite. This result became a standard justification for independent, inflation-focused central banks and helped underpin the later move toward inflation targeting.

**The Purchasing Power Parity Puzzle (1996).** In "The Purchasing Power Parity Puzzle" (*Journal of Economic Literature*, vol. 34, pp. 647–668), Rogoff articulated a puzzle that still defines the field. He noted a "remarkable consensus" that deviations from purchasing power parity (the law of one price across currencies) damp out with a *half-life of roughly three to five years*. The puzzle: this is far too slow to be explained by nominal price stickiness (which lasts months, not years), yet far too fast to be explained purely by real shocks to fundamentals (which should produce near-permanent deviations). No single model comfortably reconciles the high short-run volatility of real exchange rates with their slow long-run mean reversion. The framing organized two decades of subsequent open-economy research.

**Exchange Rate Dynamics Redux (1995) and "New Open Economy Macroeconomics."** With Maurice Obstfeld, Rogoff published "Exchange Rate Dynamics Redux" (*Journal of Political Economy*, vol. 103, June 1995, pp. 624–660), which launched what became known as the "new open economy macroeconomics." The paper built a tractable two-country general-equilibrium model marrying intertemporal optimization (forward-looking households and current-account dynamics) with monopolistic competition and sticky nominal prices. It updated the older Mundell-Fleming-Dornbusch sticky-price tradition with explicit microfoundations, allowing welfare analysis of monetary and exchange-rate policy. Their 1996 graduate textbook *Foundations of International Macroeconomics* (MIT Press, ~800 pp.) became the standard advanced reference in the field. With Obstfeld he also wrote on the "six major puzzles in international macroeconomics" (2000) and on global current-account imbalances and their potential unwinding (the mid-2000s "rebalancing" debate).

## "This Time Is Different" and the History of Financial Crises

Rogoff's most widely read scholarship is *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of Financial Folly* (Princeton University Press, 2009), co-authored with Carmen Reinhart and preceded by NBER working papers (notably "This Time Is Different: A Panoramic View of Eight Centuries of Financial Crises," NBER w13882, 2008). The book assembled a large new dataset spanning roughly 66 countries and about 800 years of data—from England's fourteenth-century default through the 2008 subprime crisis.

Core claims and mechanisms:

- **The "this-time-is-different" syndrome.** The recurring delusion, in each boom, that old rules of valuation and debt no longer apply—because of new technology, better institutions, or smarter policy. The historical record shows the rules generally do still apply.
- **Serial default.** Repeated sovereign default on external debt is "the norm" across regions and centuries, including in countries (in Europe and Asia) later considered safe; emerging economies tend to "graduate" from serial default only slowly.
- **Debt intolerance.** Countries have very different thresholds for sustainable debt; some become vulnerable to crisis and default at debt-to-GDP ratios that would be unremarkable elsewhere, reflecting weak institutions and default histories.
- **Crisis companions.** High inflation, currency crashes, debasement, and banking crises tend to cluster with sovereign defaults; banking crises are typically followed by sharp rises in government debt and prolonged output and employment declines.
- **Capital-flow bonanzas.** Sudden large capital inflows frequently precede crises; "this time" rarely turns out to be different.

The work was credited (e.g., by Alan Blinder) with illuminating key features of the 2008 crisis and the slow recovery that followed.

## The Reinhart-Rogoff Debt-Growth Controversy

The most consequential dispute of Rogoff's career concerns a separate, shorter paper: Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff, "Growth in a Time of Debt" (*American Economic Review* Papers & Proceedings, 2010). It reported that across a panel of advanced economies, countries with gross public-debt-to-GDP ratios above 90% experienced markedly lower median and mean growth—the headline figure being a mean real GDP growth rate of about **−0.1%** for the over-90% group, versus solidly positive growth below that level. The finding was widely cited during the post-2008 austerity debates, invoked by figures such as U.S. Congressman Paul Ryan, EU officials, and UK Conservative policymakers, as evidence that high debt is dangerous to growth and that fiscal consolidation was urgent.

**The Herndon-Ash-Pollin critique (2013).** Graduate student Thomas Herndon and economists Michael Ash and Robert Pollin of the University of Massachusetts Amherst obtained the original spreadsheet and, in "Does High Public Debt Consistently Stifle Economic Growth? A Critique of Reinhart and Rogoff," identified three problems:
1. **A coding error in Excel:** the averaging formula omitted five countries (rows for Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, and Denmark), because the formula was not dragged down to include them.
2. **Unconventional weighting:** each *country* was weighted equally regardless of how many years it spent in the high-debt category, so a single bad year for one country (notably New Zealand's −7.6% in 1951) could swing the average heavily.
3. **Selective data exclusion:** some available high-debt postwar years (e.g., for New Zealand, Australia, Canada) were left out.

After corrections, Herndon-Ash-Pollin found that the over-90% group's average real growth was about **+2.2%, not −0.1%**—still lower than for lower-debt countries, but with no sharp, universal cliff at 90%. The dramatic nonlinear "threshold" disappeared.

**Rogoff and Reinhart's response.** They published a detailed rebuttal (including in *The New York Times* and a formal "Response to Herndon, Ash and Pollin"). They conceded the spreadsheet coding error and called it regrettable, but argued it changed their central conclusion little: across alternative methodologies (including their preferred *median* growth figures, which were less affected), higher debt remained associated with lower growth. They stressed that they had never claimed a precise causal tipping point and complained that the "politically charged discussion" had "falsely equated our finding of a negative association between debt and growth with an unambiguous call for austerity."

**The deeper, unresolved dispute is causation.** Critics, drawing on work by Arindrajit Dube, argued the correlation likely runs the other way: slow growth tends to *precede* and *cause* rising debt-to-GDP ratios (denominator effect plus weak revenues), rather than high debt causing slow growth. Rogoff has continued to maintain that high debt carries real risks and constrains a government's room to respond to future crises, even if a clean threshold does not exist. The episode became a landmark case in debates over research transparency, replication, and the policy use (and misuse) of academic findings.

## "The Curse of Cash" — Paper Money, Crime, and the Zero Lower Bound

In *The Curse of Cash: How Large-Denomination Bills Aid Crime and Tax Evasion and Constrain Monetary Policy* (Princeton University Press, 2016), Rogoff made two linked arguments for phasing out large-denomination notes (in the U.S., the $100, $50, and eventually $20), while retaining small bills and coins:

1. **Crime and tax evasion.** Most physical cash is held not for everyday transactions but as a store of value and a medium for the underground economy. He noted that U.S. currency in circulation was roughly $1.4 trillion—about $4,200 per American—with the great majority in $100 bills, far more than legitimate domestic transactional demand. Large notes, he argued, disproportionately facilitate drug trafficking, money laundering, corruption, illegal immigration, and tax evasion, costing governments substantial revenue.
2. **Monetary policy and the zero lower bound.** Because savers can always hold paper currency at a zero nominal return, central banks face an effective floor on interest rates: cutting deeply negative would trigger mass hoarding of cash. This "zero lower bound" paralyzed monetary stimulus across advanced economies after 2008. Phasing out most cash (or otherwise penalizing large-scale cash hoarding) would let central banks set substantially negative interest rates in deep recessions, restoring a powerful stabilization tool.

Rogoff emphasized this is a *less-cash*, not *cashless*, society, with provisions for low-income and privacy-sensitive users (e.g., keeping small bills, possibly free debit accounts). 

**Counterarguments.** Critics—including libertarian and Austrian-leaning economists (e.g., reviewers in *Econ Journal Watch*) and civil-liberties scholars—argue that: cash provides privacy and autonomy from surveillance by states and corporations; the case rests on giving the state more power to tax, track, and impose negative rates on citizens' savings; criminals would substitute into other instruments (foreign currency, gold, cryptocurrency); and negative rates themselves are a dubious goal that effectively expropriates savers. Some see the proposal as paternalistic financial repression. Rogoff responds that small bills remain available for privacy and that the social costs of large-note anonymity outweigh these concerns.

## Debt, Interest Rates, and the "Debt Supercycle" vs. "Secular Stagnation"

Rogoff has been a prominent interlocutor in the debate over why interest rates and growth were so low after 2008. Lawrence ("Larry") Summers revived Alvin Hansen's **"secular stagnation"** hypothesis: a chronic excess of desired saving over investment depresses the natural (real) interest rate r* indefinitely, implying that low rates and weak demand are a long-run structural condition requiring sustained fiscal expansion.

Rogoff has offered an alternative diagnosis—the **"debt supercycle."** On his account, the post-2008 slump was primarily the slow, predictable aftermath of a massive leveraging-and-deleveraging cycle (a financial-crisis hangover), consistent with the *This Time Is Different* finding that recoveries from deep systemic financial crises are protracted. The mechanism is balance-sheet repair and credit overhang, not a permanent collapse in the equilibrium real rate. The crucial difference: in Rogoff's view the depressed rates and growth are *temporary*—they fade as deleveraging completes—whereas Summers's secular stagnation implies a permanent or quasi-permanent state. This distinction has policy stakes: secular stagnation argues for sustained debt-financed public investment; the debt-supercycle view counsels caution about piling on more debt and predicts that rates will eventually normalize.

**Recent positioning (2023–2025).** Rogoff has argued the era of ultra-low rates is over. In Project Syndicate commentaries such as "Higher Interest Rates Are Here to Stay" (December 2023), he contended that even as inflation recedes, deglobalization, high debt levels, defense and green-transition spending, aging-related costs, and populist political pressures will keep real interest rates structurally higher over the coming decade than in the 2010s. In "The End of Magical Debt Thinking" (April 2024) he argued that the notion that any problem can be solved with more government borrowing is untenable once rates are no longer near zero, because rising debt-service burdens crowd out other priorities and erode crisis-fighting capacity. In "Central Banks' New-Old Inflationary Bias" (May 2024) he warned that political and fiscal pressures could push central banks toward tolerating higher inflation again. This represents a partial vindication narrative for the debt-supercycle camp over secular stagnation, though the debate is not settled.

## The Dollar: "Our Dollar, Your Problem" (2025)

Rogoff's 2025 book *Our Dollar, Your Problem: An Insider's View of Seven Turbulent Decades of Global Finance and the Road Ahead* (Yale University Press) examines the rise, dominance, and possible relative decline of the U.S. dollar as the world's leading reserve and transaction currency. (The title alludes to U.S. Treasury Secretary John Connally's 1971 quip to foreign counterparts: "the dollar is our currency, but it's your problem.")

Central arguments:
- Reserve-currency dominance changes hands rarely—historically over spans of roughly one to two centuries—so the dollar's roughly century-long reign is, in his metaphor, "late middle-aged" rather than imminently dead.
- Past challengers (the Soviet ruble, the Japanese yen, the euro) failed to displace the dollar; China's renminbi is the current would-be rival but faces serious obstacles (capital controls, weak rule of law, lack of deep open financial markets, political risk).
- The greater threat to dollar primacy is *self-inflicted*: chronic U.S. fiscal deficits and rising debt, politicized monetary policy, weaponization of dollar-based financial sanctions (which gives others incentive to seek alternatives), and erosion of institutional credibility. The "exorbitant privilege" of cheap borrowing is not guaranteed forever.
- The likely path is gradual erosion of dollar share rather than sudden collapse, but a meaningful loss of privilege would raise U.S. borrowing costs and constrain policy.

## Positions on Globalization, the IMF, and the Stiglitz Dispute

As IMF Chief Economist, Rogoff publicly defended the institution against Joseph Stiglitz's 2002 book *Globalization and Its Discontents*. In an "Open Letter to Joseph Stiglitz" (July 2, 2002, published via the IMF), Rogoff sharply rebutted Stiglitz's criticisms of IMF crisis management (especially regarding the Asian financial crisis and the role of high interest rates and fiscal conditionality), accusing Stiglitz of overconfidence, selective memory about his own record, and offering simplistic prescriptions. The exchange crystallized a substantive divide: Stiglitz emphasized market failures, the dangers of premature capital-account liberalization, and IMF "market fundamentalism"; Rogoff defended the difficult tradeoffs of crisis lending, the need to restore confidence and currency stability, and the constraints policymakers actually face. The letter is widely cited as a vivid artifact of the early-2000s globalization debate. Critics later noted that Rogoff's 2010 austerity-linked work would itself become the target of the kind of empirical scrutiny he had marshaled against others.

Rogoff has generally defended an open global trading and financial system while warning about the risks of poorly sequenced liberalization and of unsustainable external imbalances.

## Influences, Interlocutors, and Intellectual Tensions

- **Collaborators/influences:** Maurice Obstfeld (his principal academic co-author), Carmen Reinhart (financial-crisis history), and the time-inconsistency tradition of Finn Kydland, Edward Prescott, Robert Barro, and David Gordon that underlies his central-banking work. He is often grouped with New Keynesian macroeconomics.
- **Interlocutors/opponents:** Joseph Stiglitz (globalization, IMF); Lawrence Summers and Paul Krugman (Krugman was among the most prominent critics of the debt-growth paper and of austerity); Thomas Herndon, Michael Ash, and Robert Pollin (the empirical critique); Arindrajit Dube (reverse-causation argument); and, broadly, advocates of cash and financial privacy who oppose *The Curse of Cash*.
- **Internal tensions in his thought.** (1) He champions central-bank independence and credible low inflation (1985 conservative-central-banker result), yet *The Curse of Cash* argues for arming central banks with the power to drive rates sharply negative—a more activist, interventionist monetary stance. (2) He warns repeatedly about the dangers of high public debt, yet his debt-supercycle framework acknowledges that some crisis aftermaths and emergencies justify large temporary borrowing—the line between prudent and "magical" debt thinking is one he must continually adjudicate. (3) His historical work stresses that crises are predictable and rules persist ("this time" is rarely different), yet his policy proposals (phasing out cash, embracing negative rates) are themselves bets that this time *can* be different if institutions adapt. (4) The 2010 debt-growth paper's translation into a blunt 90% austerity rule—against his later protest that he never intended a hard causal threshold—illustrates the recurring tension between an economist's hedged empirical findings and their politically charged real-world deployment.
