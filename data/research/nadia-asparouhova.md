# Nadia Asparouhova — Blind Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference on Nadia Asparouhova (formerly Nadia Eghbal), writer and independent researcher best known for *Working in Public* (2020) on open-source software and for subsequent work on tech wealth, philanthropy, "idea machines," and the spread (and non-spread) of ideas.

## Biography and Career Arc

Nadia Asparouhova is an American writer and researcher whose work spans open-source software, digital public goods, science funding, and the social and political influence of the technology industry. She published her early work under the name Nadia Eghbal; "Asparouhova" is her current surname. Her personal site is nadia.xyz; she writes a Substack newsletter ("Monomythical") and uses the handle @nayafia.

Her career began in venture and startups rather than academia. Before her open-source work she was the technical cofounder of a venture-backed food company and a seed-stage investor at Collaborative Fund, where she initially joined to help launch a non-traditional fund but ended up in a more conventional venture role. She has described leaving venture capital by asking herself "What is not venture backable in tech right now?" — concluding that open-source infrastructure was the one item left on her list, which set up the research that became *Roads and Bridges*.

From roughly 2015 to 2019 she worked independently and at GitHub to improve the open-source developer experience, contributing to projects such as GitHub's Open Source Guides and the 2017 Open Source Survey and interviewing hundreds of developers. She later spent time at Substack (focused on the writer experience) and worked as an independent researcher associated with Protocol Labs. Her affiliations and grants over time have included the Ford Foundation, Harvard University (a stint affiliated with the Digital HKS group), Schmidt Futures, the Ethereum Foundation (as a core researcher in the Summer of Protocols program, an 18-week initiative), Protocol Labs, Emergent Ventures, and the Cosmos Institute.

From the early 2020s her focus broadened from open source to tech's cultural, political, and philanthropic footprint. She launched a multi-year project on "tech's social and political influence," supported in part by an Emergent Ventures grant, and co-organized a related seminar at the Sloan Foundation's offices in 2024. She characterizes her method as "zero to one" research — investigating questions about the world that "lack a descriptive framework" — and says "theory should derive from practice," citing Jane Jacobs's ethnographic, observation-first approach as a model.

## Major Works

### Roads and Bridges: The Unseen Labor Behind Our Digital Infrastructure (Ford Foundation, July 2016)

A long report published free by the Ford Foundation, framing open-source code as critical public infrastructure analogous to physical "roads and bridges" that society depends on but systematically underinvests in. Central argument: much of the public code underpinning modern software and the internet is built and maintained almost exclusively by unpaid volunteers — motivated by reputation, obligation, or labor of love — and this volunteer-dependent model leaves infrastructure fragile, prone to security failures and service interruptions. Her recurring example is OpenSSL: a library critical to internet security that, around the 2014 Heartbleed vulnerability, was revealed to be maintained by a tiny, badly funded team. Recommendations: treat digital infrastructure as a necessary public good; embrace decentralization rather than top-down control; work with existing software communities; provide long-term, proactive, and holistic support; diversify the contributor pool; develop shared best practices; and raise public awareness of sustainability challenges. The report circulated widely in tech communities and is credited with helping catalyze later open-source funding efforts (e.g., GitHub Sponsors and similar programs). Real-world episodes after publication — the 2021–2022 "colors/faker" sabotage by maintainer Marak Squires, and core-js maintainer Denis Pushkarev's public appeals for funding — are frequently cited as vindications of the report's diagnosis.

### Working in Public: The Making and Maintenance of Open Source Software (Stripe Press, 2020)

Her first book, published by Stripe Press (~256 pp, list price ~$22), drawing on hundreds of developer interviews. The book traces a two-decade shift: open source moved from an optimistic model of mass public collaboration toward dependence on often-invisible solo maintainers whose code is consumed by millions. Core claims and frameworks:

- **A 2×2 taxonomy of open-source projects**, sorted by *contributor growth* (high/low) and *user growth* (high/low):
  - **Federations** — high contributors, high users (e.g., Linux, large distributed communities) where a self-governing contributor community forms.
  - **Clubs** — high contributors, lower users; niche projects with active participant communities serving a narrow audience.
  - **Stadiums** — low contributors, high users; highly centralized, where a few maintainers serve a huge audience. She argues this is the increasingly dominant and most stressful shape.
  - **Toys** — low contributors, low users; small projects that may still be useful.
- **The shift from "commons" to "stadium."** The romantic image of open source as a peer-produced commons (Eric Raymond's "bazaar," Yochai Benkler's commons-based peer production) has, she argues, given way to a "stadium" model with 1-to-many (1:N) communication between a few producers and many consumers.
- **Producer/consumer asymmetry and the economics of attention.** She argues that to understand sustainability one must separate *production* from *consumption* and treat them as different economic goods. Open-source code in its static, finished state is a *public good* (non-excludable, non-rivalrous). But its *production* behaves like a *commons* (non-excludable but rivalrous), where the scarce, rivalrous resource is the maintainer's attention. Reframing the problem this way, she contends, the bottleneck is not money but maintainer attention.
- **Maintainer burnout and "extractive" contributions.** Because attention is scarce, low-quality bug reports, demanding users, and drive-by pull requests can be *extractive* — they cost a maintainer more than they add. This reframes the well-meaning slogan that more contributors are always good.

### Antimemetics: Why Some Ideas Resist Spreading (Dark Forest Collective / Metalabel, 2025)

Her second book, released in 2025 via the Dark Forest Collective. Central thesis: alongside "memes" — self-replicating units of culture that thrive in an attention economy — there exist **antimemes**, "ideas that actively resist being remembered or shared, despite their importance." She defines antimemes as a broad category of "self-censoring" ideas, including taboos (things everyone knows but cannot say), cognitive biases (things we cannot see about ourselves), and memory lapses (things we know we should prioritize but keep forgetting). The book's paradox: even as it has never been easier to share ideas publicly, many of the most interesting ideas are "burrowing underground," circulating in private and semi-private spaces — group chats, texts, Google Docs, newsletters marked "don't share." She explicitly connects this to *Working in Public*: what happened to open-source developers (withdrawing from public attention as audiences grew) is, she argues, "going to happen to all of us." Related concepts she develops include strategic illegibility (deliberately making ideas hard to pin down to protect them from co-optation, citing Curtis Yarvin's circuitous style), and a "Great Apostle Theory" in place of the "Great Man Theory" — individuals as vessels or "champions" infected by ideas rather than autonomous architects of them.

## Ideas in Depth (from primary sources)

### Idea machines as funding/ideology structures

In "Idea machines" she defines an **idea machine** as "a self-sustaining organism that contains all the parts needed to turn ideas into outcomes." The anatomy: an **ideology** (a distinct worldview that acts as a "memetic engine") attracts a **community**, whose members generate ideas and form an **agenda** (how the ideology enters the world); the agenda is capitalized by one or more major **funders**; **scene builders** sustain and grow the community; **operators** execute initiatives; and **support organizations** reinforce the machine's values, producing measurable outcomes. Her worked examples: **effective altruism** as the most mature, fully built machine (utilitarian ideology incubated at Oxford and on LessWrong, capitalized by Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna via Good Ventures, producing GiveWell and 80,000 Hours); **progress studies** as a developing machine with ideology and support organizations but few operating initiatives; **Schmidt Futures** as an "inverse" case with agenda and funding but underdeveloped ideology/community; and "It's Time to Build" as incomplete.

She frames philanthropy as "a type of idea marketplace for public goods" that requires pluralism: "everyone has a different vision for the world," so effective altruism cannot and should not monopolize philanthropic thinking. Her normative conclusion is that society needs *more* idea machines, not fewer — a "more liquid idea marketplace" so that operators (talented people who are not themselves wealth generators) can "shop around" among funders rather than depend on one or two patrons (which she identifies, at the time of writing, as EA and Thiel's network). The recent explosion of tech and crypto wealth, in her telling, expands access and lets underexplored ideas (charter cities, metascience) find homes.

### The new tech elite vs. Gilded Age philanthropists

She argues that 20th-century institutional philanthropy (the Rockefeller/Carnegie foundation model pioneered around the 1910s) once "housed" idea machines but became bureaucratic and constrained — partly by regulation such as the 1969 Tax Reform Act. The contemporary tech elite, she contends, are reverting to a more experimental, *pre-welfare-state* posture reminiscent of the original Gilded Age, when self-made wealthy people improvised public goods because government did little; the mid-20th-century expansion of government as primary provider then crowded philanthropy into a smaller, more institutional role. Concretely she points to the rise of LLC-based giving vehicles over traditional 501(c)(3) foundations — the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, Emerson Collective, and similar structures — because LLCs allow more flexibility (political donations, for-profit investment, advocacy) than a charitable foundation. She is interested in the psychological transition by which elites pivot from making money for themselves to thinking about their "place in broader public society," and argues the new tech elite tends to *build movements* and *engage public institutions* rather than simply endow established causes. In "Silicon Valley's Civil War" and "Rewriting the Californian Ideology" she pushes the related claim that tech's politics are mischaracterized: not a swing right, not previously left, not even libertarian, but an unstable "counterelite" in conflict with existing social institutions.

### Ideas as infectious agents; attention as reality-construction

Across the antimemetics work she advances a "microbiome" model of cognition: "we're just infected by ideas... we have very little agency around which ideas take hold of us." She treats attention as foundational — "attention is how we carve out our personal realities" — and contrasts "truth-tellers" (who can voice uncomfortable truths precisely because they lack network stakes) with "shameless shills" (who escalate extreme positions for status). She also documents the internet's migration from a mid-2000s "global village" toward fragmented private and semi-private spaces.

## Positions, Counterarguments, and Internal Tensions

**Position: open-source sustainability is fundamentally an attention problem, not a money problem.** *Strongest counterargument:* maintainers such as core-js's Denis Pushkarev and faker/colors' Marak Squires frame it explicitly as a *money* problem — companies extract billions in value while maintainers go unpaid — and the funding-first response (GitHub Sponsors, Tidelift, Open Collective) treats compensation, not attention triage, as the lever. Critics in the commons tradition (e.g., scholars building on Elinor Ostrom and Yochai Benkler) argue robust governance and institutional design, not individual attention management, determine sustainability. *Internal tension:* her own *Roads and Bridges* helped popularize the funding framing she later complicates with the attention framing in *Working in Public*; the books point at different levers for the same problem.

**Position: the "stadium" (1:N, solo-maintainer) model now dominates open source, displacing the collaborative commons.** *Strongest counterargument:* large federated, multi-contributor communities (Linux, Kubernetes, major Apache and CNCF projects) remain central to critical infrastructure, and corporate-employed maintainers — not lone volunteers — do much of the heavy work, suggesting the lone-genius-stadium image is partial. *Internal tension:* her taxonomy treats project shape as descriptive, yet her sustainability recommendations implicitly favor preserving stadium maintainers' attention, which can read as normatively privileging one quadrant.

**Position: society benefits from proliferating idea machines and a pluralistic "idea marketplace" for philanthropy.** *Strongest counterargument:* democratic-theory and inequality critics argue that channeling public-goods provision through the private preferences of a few billionaires (whether via foundations, LLCs, or idea machines) is unaccountable and anti-democratic — a critique associated with writers like Anand Giridharadas (*Winners Take All*) and political theorist Rob Reich (*Just Giving*), who hold that big philanthropy is an exercise of plutocratic power, not a neutral marketplace. Effective-altruism-adjacent commenters on the EA Forum have separately contested her characterization of EA as merely one machine among many. *Internal tension:* she celebrates pluralism and decentralization while acknowledging that, in practice, idea machines are gated by a small number of mega-funders — so the "liquid marketplace" she advocates coexists with concentrated capital that can steer entire fields.

**Position: tech is politically *sui generis* — neither libertarian, left, nor conservative.** *Strongest counterargument:* critics across the spectrum read the post-2020 tech elite as having made a concrete rightward, anti-regulatory turn (visible in figures she names, such as Andreessen, Musk, and Thiel), making "undefinable" look like a way of declining to assign responsibility. *Internal tension:* she simultaneously argues tech lacks a coherent ideology and that it is now "building movements" with agendas — a movement-building program implies more ideological coherence than the "undefinable" thesis allows.

**Position (antimemetics): the most important ideas are increasingly hidden, resisting spread.** *Strongest counterargument:* in an era of mass leaks, virality, and surveillance, the more salient problem may be that *too much* spreads and nothing stays private; the claim that vital ideas are "burrowing underground" is hard to measure and risks being unfalsifiable. *Internal tension:* she writes publicly and at length about antimemes, which by her own definition resist being shared — making the act of cataloguing them in a published book a self-undermining exercise she only partially addresses via "strategic illegibility."
