## Broad research

# Holden Karnofsky — Reference Dossier

Holden Karnofsky (born c. 1981) is an American philanthropist, researcher, and writer best known for co-founding the charity evaluator GiveWell (2007) and the grantmaking foundation Open Philanthropy (2014, since renamed Coefficient Giving), and for the "most important century" essay series on his blog Cold Takes. A Harvard social-studies graduate (2003) who began his career at the hedge fund Bridgewater Associates, he became one of the architects of the effective-altruism movement's intellectual apparatus: rigorous cost-effectiveness analysis, "worldview diversification" in grantmaking, and—later—a focus on risks from transformative artificial intelligence. He led Open Philanthropy as CEO and then co-CEO until taking a leave of absence in 2023 to work directly on AI safety, became a visiting scholar at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and in January 2025 joined Anthropic as a member of technical staff working on its Responsible Scaling Policy and safety planning.

## Biography and Career Arc

Karnofsky graduated from Harvard in 2003 with a degree in social studies and went to work at Bridgewater Associates, where he met Elie Hassenfeld. In 2006 the two organized an informal "charity club" of Bridgewater colleagues who pooled money and tried to find the most cost-effective places to give—and discovered that the information they wanted (rigorous evidence on which charities actually save or improve the most lives per dollar) essentially did not exist in public form. In 2007 they quit to found **GiveWell**, a nonprofit that publishes in-depth evaluations and a short list of "top charities," historically dominated by global-health and direct-cash interventions (e.g., the Against Malaria Foundation, Malaria Consortium's seasonal chemoprevention, GiveDirectly's unconditional cash transfers).

GiveWell's early history includes a well-documented scandal. In late December 2007, Karnofsky used sockpuppet accounts to anonymously promote GiveWell on forums including Ask MetaFilter and Luxist without disclosing his affiliation. After being caught, he publicly apologized ("I had a lapse in judgment, did a horrible thing, and I apologize," GiveWell blog, Dec. 31, 2007); the board removed him as Executive Director effective Jan. 3, 2008, fined him, and demoted him (he was later restored to leadership). The episode is frequently cited by both critics and the organization itself as a formative ethics lesson and a test of GiveWell's commitment to transparency about its own mistakes—a value the organization made central.

Around 2011 GiveWell launched "GiveWell Labs" to investigate broader, harder-to-measure causes; this evolved into the **Open Philanthropy Project** (named 2014, independent operations from 2017), funded primarily by Facebook/Asana co-founder **Dustin Moskovitz** and **Cari Tuna** through their Good Ventures foundation. Karnofsky was Open Philanthropy's CEO and later co-CEO (alongside Alexander Berger). By 2025 Open Philanthropy had directed more than $4 billion in grants across global health and development, farm-animal welfare, scientific research, criminal-justice reform, biosecurity, and AI risk. In March 2023 Karnofsky took a leave of absence to work directly on AI safety; Berger became sole CEO (Berger leans toward "global health and wellbeing" causes, in contrast to Karnofsky's longtermist emphasis). Karnofsky then served as a visiting scholar at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace before joining **Anthropic** in January 2025.

## Cost-Effectiveness, Evidence, and "Bayesian Adjustment"

Karnofsky's foundational intellectual contribution at GiveWell was insistence on hard, transparent evidence (ideally randomized controlled trials) and on cost-per-outcome estimates. But he is equally associated with a famous caution against taking those estimates literally. In the 2011 GiveWell blog post **"Why we can't take expected value estimates literally (even when they're unbiased)"**, he argued that an explicit expected-value calculation is only a noisy *measurement* of the truth and must be combined with a prior probability distribution over cost-effectiveness via a **Bayesian adjustment**. The mechanism: extreme cost-effectiveness claims have low prior probability, so a shaky estimate of an astronomically high payoff should be heavily "regressed" toward the prior; more robust evidence licenses a larger update. His practical conclusion was that one should *not* give purely by ranking explicit expected-value formulas, and should prefer well-grounded estimates over speculative ones.

This stance generated a notable internal-EA tension and debate. Critics—including discussion on LessWrong (e.g., "Bayesian Adjustment Does Not Defeat Existential Risk Charity")—pointed out that a sufficiently strong prior favoring "ordinary" interventions can be used to dismiss any high-stakes speculative cause (such as existential-risk reduction) almost regardless of argument, which sits awkwardly with Karnofsky's own later embrace of speculative AI work. The tension is real and self-acknowledged: the same epistemic humility he used to *discount* wild expected-value claims is something he later had to argue *around* when making the case that low-probability, high-stakes AI scenarios deserve major resources.

## Worldview Diversification

As Open Philanthropy expanded beyond measurable global-health interventions into causes with deeply uncertain or incommensurable payoffs (animal welfare, the far future, scientific research), Karnofsky articulated **"worldview diversification"** (2016): rather than forcing all causes onto a single utility metric and funding only the apparent top, Open Philanthropy allocates capital across multiple plausible "worldviews" (e.g., a near-termist human-welfare view, an animal-inclusive view, a longtermist view), funding within each until diminishing returns appear. The reasoning is that moral and empirical uncertainty is too deep to justify betting everything on one framework, and that different worldviews can imply wildly different "best" actions. The strongest internal objection is that this is a partial abandonment of the maximizing logic that justified EA in the first place—an admission that the movement cannot actually rank its causes—and that the boundaries between "worldviews" and the budgets assigned to each are somewhat arbitrary and politically negotiated rather than derived.

## The Singularity Institute Critique (2012)

In May 2012 Karnofsky posted **"Thoughts on the Singularity Institute (SI)"** on LessWrong—at the time the most-upvoted post in the site's history—a detailed, largely skeptical evaluation of the organization now called the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (then led by Eliezer Yudkowsky's institute). Two substantive objections stood out. First, the **"tool AI" vs. "agent AI"** distinction: Karnofsky argued SI neglected the possibility that powerful AI could be built as a passive question-answering tool rather than an autonomous goal-pursuing agent, undercutting some doom arguments. Second, he argued that deliberately building an AGI to maximize a "Friendly" utility function is itself extraordinarily dangerous, and questioned SI's organizational competence and track record. SI's Luke Muehlhauser conceded several points (especially on past organizational competence) while contesting others. The post is historically significant partly because Karnofsky later substantially reversed his relative optimism, becoming a leading funder and advocate of AI-safety work—an evolution he has openly described.

## The "Most Important Century" Thesis (Cold Takes, 2021–2022)

Karnofsky's best-known body of writing is the **"most important century"** series on Cold Takes (launched September 2021, ~20+ interconnected essays). Its core claim: the 21st century may be the most important in human history because it could see the development of AI powerful enough to trigger a runaway acceleration of technological progress, locking in a long-lasting—possibly galaxy-spanning—future that could be utopian, dystopian, or anything between.

Key building blocks:

- **"This Can't Go On"** (Aug. 3, 2021): argues that the few-percent-per-year economic growth we treat as normal is historically bizarre and physically unsustainable. He notes that 2% annual growth sustained for ~8,200 years would make the economy ~3×10^70 times its current size—larger than the roughly 10^70 atoms in the galaxy could support, i.e., "multiple economies as big as today's entire world economy per atom." Therefore the present few centuries are either near a unique growth peak followed by stagnation, an even faster explosion, or collapse—none of which is "business as usual."
- **PASTA** ("Process for Automating Scientific and Technological Advancement"): his label for AI that can fully automate the innovation process itself, creating a feedback loop—"more resources → more AIs → more ideas → more resources"—that standard economic growth models suggest could produce an "economic singularity," a sustained super-exponential takeoff.
- **"Digital People Would Be An Even Bigger Deal"** (July 27, 2021): imagines conscious software emulations of specific people, copyable and runnable at high speed in virtual environments. He argues they could drive explosive productivity, enable controlled "social science" experiments, allow rapid space settlement (designing self-replicating manufacturing), and—crucially—enable **"lock-in,"** where a set of values or a power structure becomes effectively permanent. He stresses that initial conditions (e.g., whether digital people's basic rights are protected) could irreversibly determine whether the long-run future is extremely good or extremely bad.
- **"Forecasting Transformative AI: What's the Burden of Proof?"** and the **biological anchors** summaries: Karnofsky takes seriously the intuitive objection that "it's wild and out-there to claim transformative AI is coming this century, so your arguments had better be extraordinary." He grants the reaction is reasonable, then argues the prior should not be so punishing, drawing on Open Philanthropy reports he commissioned—Ajeya Cotra's "Forecasting Transformative AI from Biological Anchors" (which estimated ~10% chance of transformative AI by 2031, ~50% by 2052, ~80% by 2100), Tom Davidson's semi-informative-priors report, and Karnofsky's own report on human brain computation. His headline numbers: **>10% chance of transformative AI by 2036, ~50% by 2060, ~2/3 by 2100**, and a 15–30% chance that this is "the most important century" in some strong sense.

He frames the appropriate response not as excitement but as a **"Call to Vigilance"**: take "robustly good" actions now and position to make wise decisions later.

The biological-anchors method drew sustained criticism. Scott Alexander's "Biological Anchors: A Trick That Might Or Might Not Work" and Eliezer Yudkowsky (in a widely circulated critique of Cotra's report) argued that anchoring AI timelines to the computational cost of evolving or running a human brain is methodologically fragile—the result depends heavily on free parameters and may launder intuition into apparent rigor. Karnofsky has acknowledged the method is only one input among several.

## Historical and Cultural Essays (Cold Takes)

Beyond AI, Cold Takes includes essays probing whether progress is real and where it comes from. **"Has Life Gotten Better?"** (Oct. 5, 2021) tries to chart average quality of life across all of human history. **"Where's Today's Beethoven?"** (Jan. 4, 2022) poses the puzzle of apparent **innovation stagnation**: despite vastly larger, better-educated populations and democratized tools, the most celebrated figures in many artistic and scientific fields cluster in the past. He lays out three hypotheses—the "golden age" view (past people were genuinely better innovators), the "bad taste" view (canonical figures are overrated by reputation/lock-in), and the "innovation as mining" view (good ideas get harder to find as the easy ones are used up)—and treats the question as evidence about whether sustained progress is fragile. He also wrote **"Learning By Writing,"** describing his own research method of forming and stress-testing a hypothesis through writing.

## Career Philosophy: "Building Aptitudes" and "Kicking Ass"

In a 2021 EA Forum post and the accompanying 80,000 Hours interview ("building aptitudes and kicking ass"), Karnofsky argued—against an over-eager focus on immediate impact—that early-career people (especially aspiring longtermists) should concentrate on building transferable **aptitudes** such as organization-building, empirical/conceptual research, communication, and people-management, and on "learning to kick ass at something," since most of one's impact tends to arrive decades into a career. His practical heuristic: join "small things that are getting bigger." This reflects his own path (Bridgewater → GiveWell → Open Philanthropy) and his stated preference for *building multiple organizations* rather than running one indefinitely—a rationale he later cited for stepping back from Open Philanthropy.

## AI Risk: The Takeover Argument and the Safety Playbook

In 2022 Karnofsky wrote a sequence on misalignment risk. **"AI Could Defeat All Of Us Combined"** (June 9, 2022) makes the deliberately modest argument that AI need not be qualitatively *superhuman* to be dangerous: a population of merely human-level AIs, cheaply copyable and run fast, could quickly come to rival all of human civilization in numbers and effective labor, and—if aimed at it—could "defeat" humanity, meaning gain enough control that humans can no longer determine outcomes (whether by killing or simply "containing" us). Companion essays—**"Why Would AI 'Aim' To Defeat Humanity?"**, **"AI Safety Seems Hard to Measure,"** **"How We Could Stumble Into AI Catastrophe,"** and **"High-Level Hopes for AI Alignment"**—lay out misalignment mechanisms, the difficulty of verifying safety (a safe-looking system may be deceptively so), and tractable directions (interpretability, adversarial testing, limited/auditable deployment).

Moving from theory to policy, his Carnegie report **"If-Then Commitments for AI Risk Reduction"** (Sept. 2024) crystallized the framework behind responsible-scaling policies: commitments of the form *if* a model demonstrates capability X, *then* mitigations Y must be in place, with development or deployment delayed if they are not. He helped design Anthropic's original Responsible Scaling Policy, described as the strongest safety commitment any frontier lab had made.

## Joining Anthropic and the "Race" Debate

Karnofsky joined Anthropic in January 2025 as a member of technical staff focused on the Responsible Scaling Policy. His public stance combines alarm with a particular theory of the strategic situation. He has said humanity is handling the arrival of advanced AI "about as badly as possible," yet puts roughly **50/50 odds** on things going well *even if* we remain reckless—while insisting we are taking far more risk than we should. Crucially, he **rejects the framing that the AI race is a pure prisoner's dilemma / coordination failure** in which everyone secretly wants to slow down but is forced to race; he argues many players genuinely do not want to slow down at all, so the path to safety runs less through hoped-for mutual pauses and more through a long list of cheap interventions ("dozens of opportunities lying on the table") that improve safety without costing a lab the race.

This position is the locus of his sharpest current controversies, on two fronts:

- **The RSP "v3" retreat.** Karnofsky publicly defended Anthropic's loosening of its Responsible Scaling Policy, including dropping certain binding commitments (such as the prior commitment not to proceed if doing so would be dangerous) in favor of more "aspirational" goals. His stated reasoning: rigid, specific commitments created "an enormous amount of pressure to declare our systems lack relevant capabilities" and threatened "commercially devastating" slowdowns while competitors kept building. Critics (e.g., Zvi Mowshowitz's detailed RSP-v3 analysis, and reporting in Transformer) argue this is exactly the predicted failure mode—voluntary commitments quietly weakened under commercial pressure—and that it undermines the credibility of the entire if-then approach he pioneered.
- **Working inside a frontier lab.** Going to Anthropic while believing the technology carries large existential risk invites the standard objection that safety-motivated insiders accelerate and legitimize the very race they fear, and that equity-based compensation creates a conflict of interest in safety judgments. (His former colleague Joe Carlsmith, who made a parallel move, addressed these concerns explicitly; Karnofsky's defense rests on the "cheap safety wins" thesis and on comparative advantage.)

## Critics, Interlocutors, and Tensions

- **Longtermism critics.** Émile P. Torres and others (e.g., in *Current Affairs* and the "TESCREAL" critique) argue that "most important century"/longtermist framings use astronomical future stakes to rationalize present-day priorities and downplay nearer harms, and that the speculative nature of AI/digital-people scenarios makes their cost-effectiveness claims unfalsifiable. Karnofsky's "Call to Vigilance" framing and his "robustly good actions" hedge are partly responses to this charge.
- **AI-timelines skeptics.** Beyond the bio-anchors methodological critiques, "red-teaming Holden Karnofsky's AI timelines" (EA Forum) noted that a large share of the evidence he cites originates from reports Open Philanthropy itself commissioned, raising echo-chamber concerns.
- **The MIRI/Yudkowsky axis.** Karnofsky's intellectual relationship with Yudkowsky's circle runs from his skeptical 2012 SI critique to substantial later agreement on the seriousness of misalignment risk—while still differing on tractability and on whether engagement with frontier labs helps or harms.
- **Self-tensions.** The throughline tension in his record is between *epistemic conservatism* (the Bayesian-adjustment skepticism of wild expected-value claims; the demand for hard evidence at GiveWell) and *high-variance longtermist betting* (funding and now working on speculative AI scenarios). A second tension is between *binding accountability* (his original case for specific if-then commitments) and his subsequent defense of softening them under competitive pressure. He has generally addressed these openly rather than denied them, which is itself characteristic of his "transparency about our own mistakes" ethos.
