## Broad research

# Lars Doucet — Reference Dossier

Lars Andreas Doucet is an American game developer, writer, and entrepreneur best known for popularizing **Georgism** — the economic philosophy of Henry George centered on the land value tax (LVT) — to a contemporary online audience. A Texas A&M graduate (class of 2006) with a master's in Visualization Sciences, he spent roughly the first phase of his career making games: educational Flash titles (*Super Energy Apocalypse*, 2008–2009; *CellCraft*, 2010) and the commercially successful RPG/tower-defense hybrid *Defender's Quest: Valley of the Forgotten* (2012) under his studio Level Up Labs in Bryan, Texas, with a sequel released in January 2025. Alongside game work he became a widely read analyst of digital-distribution economics (Steam, app stores) for *Game Developer*. In 2021 he won Scott Alexander's Astral Codex Ten Book Review Contest with a review of George's *Progress and Poverty*; he expanded the research into a multi-part empirical series and the 2022 book *Land Is a Big Deal*. He has since pivoted into the property-assessment industry, co-founding the startup **ValueBase** (2022) and the nonprofit **Center for Land Economics**, where he serves as "Co-Founder & Chief Everything-Doer." He blogs at *Fortress of Doors*.

## Georgism: the core thesis he advances

Doucet's central project is reviving and empirically testing Henry George's 19th-century argument. George's puzzle, from *Progress and Poverty* (1879), is the "paradox of progress": why does poverty deepen even as technology and capital advance? George's answer is that the three factors of production — land, labor, and capital — earn rent, wages, and interest respectively, and that as a society grows richer, **landowners capture the productivity gains as rising rent**, leaving wages and interest squeezed. Land, in George's framing, is not wealth: wealth is labor applied to nature, whereas land is nature itself, fixed in supply and unproduced. Money, stocks, and bonds are merely claims on wealth.

The mechanism is the **Law of Rent**: rent on any parcel equals the difference between what it can produce and what the best available "marginal" (rent-free) land can produce. Doucet's illustrative version: if Lot A yields 100 units and the marginal Lot C yields 10, the landlord can extract 90 in rent without contributing anything productive. As communities build infrastructure and grow, this surplus accrues automatically to whoever holds the deeds.

The Georgist remedy is the **land value tax**: tax the unimproved value of land at a high rate, but leave buildings, labor, and capital untaxed. Doucet argues this is uniquely efficient because land's supply is fixed — taxing it creates no "deadweight loss" the way taxing income, sales, or buildings does (those discourage the taxed activity; you cannot discourage the existence of land). It also kills land speculation, since holding idle land becomes costly. George paired this with a "Citizen's Dividend" (a precursor to UBI). Doucet repeatedly frames Georgism as a "third way" that scrambles the capitalism-vs-socialism axis by reintroducing land as a distinct factor — a point he summarizes as land being the "missing piece" that "flips the entire script" on the labor-vs-capital framing.

Doucet leans on George's biography for color: George ran for New York City mayor in 1886 (losing to Tammany Hall, reportedly out-polling a young Theodore Roosevelt); his 1897 death drew roughly 100,000–200,000 mourners; and the board game that became *Monopoly* originated as Elizabeth Magie's Georgist teaching tool, "The Landlord's Game." He also notes that economists across the spectrum — Milton Friedman (who called LVT the "least bad tax") and Paul Krugman among them — have conceded the theoretical point, even while Marx dismissed George.

## The empirical series: testing three objections

Rather than argue Georgism on principle, Doucet structured his ACX series around the three strongest practical objections, treating each as a testable hypothesis.

**Part I — "Is land really a big deal?"** Here he answers Krugman's dismissal that land is economically trivial today. His figures: most of the value of urban real estate is the *land*, not the structure (a San Francisco spot-check put land at ~76% of property value, consistent with American Enterprise Institute data showing land share approaching 100% in expensive coastal cities). He estimates total US land value at **$24–44 trillion** (the $24T figure from a Federal Reserve-based method at a 5% capitalization rate; the $44T from analyst Jeffrey J. Smith), implying annual land rents of roughly **$1.2–3.5 trillion** — enough, he argues, to fund any single one of Defense, Social Security, or Medicare+Medicaid outright. He cites OECD data putting land at ~40% of US household wealth (~50% in the UK and France), McKinsey research that real estate is ~two-thirds of global real assets (over half of that being land), and Jordà-Schularick-Taylor data showing real estate is the majority of bank lending in developed economies (~60% of UK bank loans by 2017). On concentration: per Fed data, the top 1% own 14.7% of US real estate value, the top 10% own 44.8%, the top 50% own 88.5%. His rhetorical contrast: confiscating all US billionaires' wealth raises slightly less than selling all federal land and minerals once — whereas land rents could be collected *every year* — and land, unlike capital, "can't flee the country."

He introduces two key Georgist concepts. **ATCOR** ("All Taxes Come Out of Rent," from economist Mason Gaffney): cutting taxes on labor and capital causes land values to rise by a corresponding amount, because workers and firms can now afford higher rent — so an LVT could partly fund itself dynamically. And the **Henry George Theorem** (formalized by Joseph Stiglitz, 1977): under certain conditions, spending on (non-rival, non-excludable) public goods is fully recaptured by land rents, so a 100% LVT would be exactly sufficient to fund those public goods.

**Part II — "Can landlords pass LVT on to tenants?"** This is the incidence question. Doucet's argument: because land is fixed in supply, LVT cannot be passed to tenants the way a gas or cigarette tax is. The intuition behind passable taxes is that taxing variable-supply goods drives marginal producers out, shrinking supply and raising prices — but land can't be withdrawn from existence. Landlords already charge the maximum the market bears, so they cannot raise rents further without losing tenants; the tax instead capitalizes into a *lower land purchase price* (he stresses the stock/flow distinction: the tax hits the asset price, not the monthly rent flow). His strongest evidence is a 2007 study by Høj, Jørgensen, and Schou exploiting a Danish municipal-boundary reorganization that semi-randomly changed LVT rates across ~250 areas — a natural experiment showing "full capitalization" into land prices. He cites roughly eight further studies (Oates 1969, Buettner 2003, Plummer 2010, Borge & Rattsø 2014, Choi 2015, among others) finding the same pattern. He acknowledges one genuine complication: if LVT funds better local services, land demand can rise, partly offsetting the price drop — which he concedes is "a solid argument," resolving it by noting a 100% LVT would recapture those gains too.

**Part III — "Can unimproved land be assessed separately from buildings?"** The implementation objection. Doucet surveys the three classical appraisal methods (sales-comparison/market, cost, and income approaches) and a wave of post-2008 statistical mass-appraisal techniques: multiple regression (~85% accuracy in a Turkish study with ~100 price factors), kernel regression (0.704 correlation with expert assessments in Berlin), adaptive weights smoothing, semiparametric regression (0.845 correlation in Berlin), and an "innovative land valuation model" tested in the Philippines. His core defense against the accuracy worry is that **tolerance matters more than perfection**: because real Georgists advocate roughly a 75–85% LVT rather than 100%, there's a built-in cushion — over-assess land by 15% and tax at 85%, and you've still only collected ~97.75% of true value. He cites British Columbia (one assessor per ~7,250 residents, annual computerized revaluations since 1975) to rebut the "army of assessors" fear, and Australia's sub-1% assessment-objection rates. His verdict: not "a slam dunk," but the claim that separating land from improvements is *impossible* is "plainly ruled out." He also flags that US assessment quality is often poor (he repeatedly cites Pennsylvania ranking near the bottom of states on assessment accuracy) — which is precisely the problem his company aims at.

## From games to land: the career pivot and assessment work

Doucet's move into property assessment is the practical extension of Part III. In 2022 he co-founded **ValueBase** with Will Jarvis (based in Durham, NC), a startup building automated valuation models / computer-assisted mass-appraisal (CAMA) tools to make municipal land assessment "more accurate, efficient, and transparent" using open-source and open-data methods — directly addressing the implementation gap he identified. Reporting indicates ValueBase raised on the order of $7.9M. He subsequently helped found the nonprofit **Center for Land Economics** (with executive director Greg Miller), which runs land-tax impact studies for jurisdictions and co-runs the *Progress & Poverty* Substack; its board and advisor roster includes figures like Redfin economist Daryl Fairweather, Joshua Vincent, and Rory Sutherland.

This pivot is itself a notable intellectual move: Doucet's reputation rests on arguing Georgism is *practical*, and he has staked his career on building the assessment infrastructure that critics say doesn't exist. It also creates a candid tension he is transparent about — he is now a commercial vendor of the very assessment technology whose feasibility he argues for in his writing.

## Game-industry and platform economics

A parallel and substantial body of Doucet's work analyzes digital distribution. Writing for *Game Developer* (formerly Gamasutra), he produced widely cited data-driven pieces such as "So You Want To Compete With Steam" (2018) and Steam "discoverability" analyses, and founded the metrics site **GameDataCrunch**. His through-line is empirical study of platform economics and developer livelihoods — how Steam's recommendation and discovery systems, the proliferation of releases, and platform fee structures (the ~30% "platform cut" debated in the Epic v. Apple era) shape which developers survive. Notably, his land obsession bleeds into this domain: he co-authored "Land value tax in online games and virtual worlds: A how-to guide" (with designer Daniel Cook, 2022) and "Land speculators will kill your game's growth" (2021), applying Georgist rent theory to virtual economies where developers control the "land." His games writing is characteristically facts-first rather than polemical.

## Debates, named opponents, and tensions

**The Caplan–Gochenour distortion critique.** The most cited academic objection comes from Bryan Caplan and Zachary Gochenour ("A Search-Theoretic Critique of Georgism," 2012). They argue a 100% LVT is *not* neutral: it destroys the incentive to *search* for valuable land uses and natural resources, because any profit from discovering a good location or deposit immediately raises that land's assessed value and is taxed away — so socially valuable search activity is under-supplied. They add a time-inconsistency/regime-uncertainty problem: even gradual Georgist policy undermines credible commitment to property rights. The Georgist rebuttal (Fred Foldvary) holds that Caplan and Gochenour smuggle produced capital into their definition of "land"; if land is strictly unproduced nature, the distortion claim doesn't go through. Doucet's own posture is the pragmatic one — advocate ~75–85%, not 100%, blunting the sharpest theoretical edge.

**The assessment objection (Hayek).** Friedrich Hayek conceded LVT would be powerful *if* land could be valued fairly, but doubted assessment could be done without arbitrary, unfair outcomes. A related line argues site values are partly *created* by neighbors' improvements, so taxing "site value" is really taxing productive potential. This is exactly the objection Doucet's Part III and his company exist to answer empirically.

**The "land is small" dismissal (Krugman).** Krugman represents the mainstream view that land is a minor share of a modern service economy, making the single-tax idea quaint. Doucet's Part I is a direct, numbers-heavy rebuttal.

**Tensions within his own commitments.** (1) **Theory vs. practice:** he champions the elegant 100%-LVT theory (ATCOR, the Henry George Theorem) while advocating a sub-100% rate in practice — leaning on the cushion to handle assessment error, which means conceding he can't fully realize the frictionless ideal. (2) **Single-tax purism vs. revenue realism:** his own Part I numbers show land rents could cover a large slice but generally *not all* of combined federal-state-local spending under conservative estimates (his ranges run from ~14–26% up to ~29–49% of all tax receipts), so the historic "single tax" branding overstates what LVT alone funds. (3) **Vendor and advocate:** he simultaneously argues assessment is feasible and sells assessment software, an alignment of interests he is open about but which critics can flag. (4) **Capital-friendly framing vs. distributional critique:** he markets Georgism as pro-growth and acceptable to capital owners (untaxing buildings and investment), while George's original moral case was a sharp attack on rentier wealth concentration — Doucet bridges these but the emphasis differs from George's polemical roots.

**Influences and interlocutors.** His direct intellectual lineage is Henry George, with heavy use of 20th-century Georgist economist **Mason Gaffney** (ATCOR) and **Joseph Stiglitz** (Henry George Theorem). His amplifiers and endorsers include **Scott Alexander** (whose contest launched him), **Vitalik Buterin** (who praised *Land Is a Big Deal* as "among the clearest and most compelling writing in support of land value taxes that I've seen" and has written his own Georgism-adjacent pieces), and **Noah Smith** (a sympathetic popularizer who has written on "how to sell Georgism to the middle class"). His foils are Krugman, Caplan/Gochenour, and Hayek. His collaborators in the assessment/policy world include Will Jarvis and Greg Miller.
