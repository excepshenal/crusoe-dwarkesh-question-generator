# Kenneth T. Jackson — Reference Dossier

## Biography and Career

Kenneth Terry Jackson was born July 27, 1939, in Memphis, Tennessee. He earned a B.A. from Memphis State University (now the University of Memphis) in 1961, then an M.A. (1963) and Ph.D. (1966) from the University of Chicago. He served roughly three years in the United States Air Force before entering academic life.

Jackson joined Columbia University as an assistant professor in 1968, was promoted to associate professor in 1971 and full professor in 1976, named Andrew W. Mellon Professor in 1987, and took the Jacques Barzun Professorship of History and the Social Sciences in 1990. He chaired Columbia's Department of History, held the Barzun chair for decades, and retired in 2020 as Jacques Barzun Professor Emeritus. As of mid-2026 he is reported still living (he was 86 as of 2025); no death notice for the Columbia historian appears in the public record reviewed here.

His professional leadership spans the major bodies of his field: president of the Urban History Association (mid-1990s), the Society of American Historians (late 1990s), and the Organization of American Historians (2000–2001). He served as director/president of the New-York Historical Society (commonly dated 2001–2004), where he helped revive the institution and anchored it as a partner in his publishing projects. He also led the New York Academy of History. Honors include the Bancroft Prize and the Francis Parkman Prize (both for *Crabgrass Frontier*), the Mark Van Doren Award (1989), Columbia's Great Teacher Award (1999), New York State Scholar of the Year (2001), and Columbia's Alexander Hamilton Medal (2016). He is a member of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences.

Jackson is widely identified as "Mr. New York." His best-known teaching vehicle is his Columbia course "History of the City of New York," which he taught for roughly four decades and whose signature event is an all-night bicycle ride through the city — students meet near the Central Park sundial around 11 p.m., set off after midnight, and wind through Central Park, Times Square, Madison Square, Gramercy Park, the West Village, Battery Park City, over the Brooklyn Bridge, ending in Brooklyn Heights at dawn, with Jackson narrating through a megaphone. He created the ride because daytime walking tours left the streets too crowded; it became a "bucket list" course so popular it now requires an NYPD permit. The ride embodies his core pedagogical conviction: that history must be learned in place, on the ground, in the built environment itself.

## Major Works

**The Ku Klux Klan in the City, 1915–1930 (Oxford, 1967).** Jackson's first book, drawn from his dissertation, was revisionist. Against the prevailing image of the 1920s Klan as a Southern, rural, small-town phenomenon, Jackson argued the second Klan drew vigorous strength from cities — Denver, Chicago, Detroit, Portland, and others, North, West, and South. He located its appeal among native-born white Protestants who felt "frightened, dislocated, and uprooted" by rapid urban change, immigration, and shifting ethnic composition. The book examined urban Klansmen's socioeconomic status and reframed the Klan as a response to modern urban life rather than a backwater holdover.

**Crabgrass Frontier: The Suburbanization of the United States (Oxford, 1985).** His landmark work; winner of both the Bancroft Prize and the Francis Parkman Prize. It remains the foundational synthesis of American suburban history (details below).

**The Encyclopedia of New York City (Yale University Press / New-York Historical Society, 1995; 2nd ed. 2010).** Jackson conceived and edited this single-volume reference, widely called "indispensable." The first edition ran to roughly 1,350 pages with more than 4,000 entries by over 650 contributors; the second edition (2010) was about 35 percent larger, with more than 5,000 entries. The project tied his scholarship to his New-York Historical Society work and to his civic mission of making the city legible to its inhabitants.

**Empire City: New York Through the Centuries (Columbia University Press, 2002),** co-edited with David S. Dunbar, a documentary anthology of New York across four centuries.

**Robert Moses and the Modern City: The Transformation of New York (W. W. Norton, 2007),** co-edited with art historian Hilary Ballon, the catalogue volume for a three-part 2007 New York exhibition and the central text of the Moses reassessment (details below).

## Crabgrass Frontier: Central Arguments

Jackson's thesis is that American suburbanization was historically distinctive, driven by two foundational forces — **cheap transportation** and **cheap housing** — and then decisively accelerated, in its mid-20th-century mass form, by **federal policy**.

On transportation, he traces a sequence from steam ferries and railroads to horsecars, then electric streetcars, then the automobile, each enabling commuting across longer distances. He notes the streetcar's centrality: by 1890 American street railways carried over two billion passengers a year, "more than twice that of the rest of the world combined," and quotes Frank Sprague's 1904 claim that "the electric railway has become the most potent factor in our modern life."

On housing, he stresses construction innovation, especially the balloon-frame method (Chicago, 1830s), which made detached wood houses fast and cheap. He argues that "for the first time in the history of the world, middle class families in the late nineteenth century could reasonably expect to buy a detached home," at real shelter costs below Europe's.

His most influential claim concerns federal intervention. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC, 1933) and especially the Federal Housing Administration (FHA, 1934) revolutionized mortgage finance — long-term, low-down-payment, amortized loans — but did so through neighborhood "Residential Security" grading that downgraded older, denser, racially mixed, and minority districts. Jackson, who worked with HOLC maps he located in the National Archives, concluded that "the result, if not the intent, of the public housing program of the United States was to segregate the races, to concentrate the disadvantaged in inner cities." This is the redlining argument that made the book canonical: FHA and VA underwriting channeled the great postwar mortgage subsidy overwhelmingly toward new, homogeneous, white suburbs, often enforced by racially restrictive covenants, while starving central cities of capital.

Jackson also frames suburbanization as deeply **cultural**. Americans, he argues, inherited from England a "historic distaste" for cities and a preference for "a detached dwelling to a row house, rural life to city life, and owning to renting." The suburb thus became, in his framing, the chief symbol of the American way of life — a fusion of the rural ideal and the metropolitan economy. He calls the American suburb "a remarkable and probably lasting achievement," explicitly refusing to treat it purely as pathology.

His **comparative** argument is that the United States was unusual: affluent and middle-class Americans live far from work, in homes they own, at low density, to a degree not matched in Europe or Japan. He observes that since William Levitt built outside Paris in 1965, "the European landscape has become littered with all the trappings of suburban America," and at points suggests suburban form reflects a near-universal aspiration unlocked by technology and affluence — a claim in some tension with his emphasis on American distinctiveness and on policy as cause.

## Suburbanization Causation: Reasoning and Counterarguments

Jackson's multi-causal account — cheap land, transportation technology, cheap construction, FHA/HOLC underwriting and redlining, the home-mortgage-interest deduction, federal highway spending, and a deep cultural preference for detached owner-occupied homes — became the field's baseline. His distinctive emphasis is that government action was decisive: in his words, "the result, if not the intent, of Washington programs has been to encourage decentralization," and he argued that "economic causes have been even more important than skin color" insofar as policy made suburban homeownership the only economically feasible path for many white families.

The strongest counterarguments come from later urban and suburban historians who built on and challenged him. **Matthew Lassiter** has argued that *Crabgrass Frontier* both overstates a single national pattern and, paradoxically, "downplays the impact of race in federal policy" while undercounting suburban diversity. **Matthew Garcia** faulted Jackson for ignoring working-class, Latino, and agricultural suburbs (e.g., California citrus suburbs built on Mexican labor), which do not fit the white-middle-class model. **Diane Harris** criticized the book's thin engagement with architecture and domestic design. **Nikhil Rao** noted the framework travels poorly outside the U.S., where land scarcity and centralization (as in South Asia) invert his assumptions. A separate strand of scholarship (associated with revisionist work on the HOLC maps) has questioned the causal weight Jackson placed on HOLC redlining specifically — arguing the maps may have reflected rather than primarily caused disinvestment, and that FHA underwriting standards and private practice mattered more — though this debate refines rather than overturns his federal-policy thesis.

## The Robert Moses Reassessment

Jackson is the most prominent academic voice in the rehabilitation — or, in his framing, the rebalancing — of Robert Moses's reputation against Robert Caro's *The Power Broker: Robert Moses and the Fall of New York* (1974). With Hilary Ballon, Jackson co-edited *Robert Moses and the Modern City* (2007), the first major scholarly treatment since Caro and the catalogue for a coordinated three-venue New York exhibition.

Jackson's argument is deliberately partial, not a full exoneration. He grants Moses's authoritarianism and human costs: Moses "did not listen to critics or to residents about to lose their homes" and "did not build what we would have wanted." But Jackson contends Caro inflated Moses's singular causal role — Moses was "simply swimming with the tide of history," doing what planners in other cities did, often under the same federal urban-renewal and highway programs. Jackson's affirmative case: Moses was "a public servant in the best sense of the term"; without him New York "would have lacked the wherewithal" to modernize; and his parks, parkways, bridges, beaches (Jones Beach), and public housing laid "the foundation for the city's revival after 1975." Jackson and Ballon further argued that the era of "citizen participation" — symbolized by the 1985 defeat of the Westway project — had produced urban paralysis, implicitly suggesting that some Moses-like capacity to build is needed again. Jackson has also noted, as a meta-point, that Caro's book is "so massive and intimidating that other historians have been discouraged from writing about Robert Moses."

**The strongest counterarguments, with named opponents:**

- **Robert Caro** is the foundational antagonist. His portrait casts Moses as a reformer corrupted by power — a man whose "susceptibility to the addiction of power was unusually strong" and who came to seek "power for its own sake, as an end in itself" — and who, via the unaccountable Triborough Bridge Authority, drove highways through working-class neighborhoods, displaced hundreds of thousands, starved mass transit, and entrenched automobile dependency. The defense that "other cities did the same" cuts against the revisionists too: Caro's distinctive claim is precisely Moses's unmatched *power to execute* these plans ruthlessly and at scale (e.g., the Cross-Bronx Expressway).
- **Thomas Bender** directly rebutted Jackson's central historical claim. Bender argued New York's post-1975 revival came not from Moses's legacy but from the "massive rehabilitation of the subway system" under Richard Ravitch and from zoning reforms enabling loft conversions — "both mass transit and those old buildings were, of course, objects of Moses' scorn." Bender also rejected the urban-paralysis thesis by pointing to successful contemporary projects.
- A complementary revisionist line (e.g., historians invoking **Richard Wade** and **Marshall Berman**) argues that highway and renewal programs were a "total elite consensus" and national federal policy, which simultaneously diminishes Moses's blameworthiness (helping Jackson) and his heroic indispensability (cutting against Jackson) — since cities without a Moses "assumed the same physical and spatial configuration."

## Advocacy for Cities and Place-Based History

Across his career Jackson combined his suburban scholarship with active advocacy for cities and for dense, walkable urban life, and for the teaching of history through direct encounter with place. His all-night bike ride, his New-York Historical Society leadership, and the *Encyclopedia* all express a conviction that civic memory and physical environment are inseparable, and that students and citizens understand the metropolis only by moving through it. He has positioned himself as a partial ally of urbanists who value density while remaining, in his historical writing, a non-moralizing analyst of why Americans chose the suburb.

## Internal Tensions (Stated as Facts)

- *Crabgrass Frontier* simultaneously argues that American suburbanization was historically *exceptional* (driven by distinctive U.S. policy and culture) and that suburban form is becoming a near-*universal* outcome of technology and affluence abroad — two claims that pull in opposite directions on causation.
- The book treats federal policy and racial redlining as central engines of inequitable suburbanization, yet also calls the American suburb "a remarkable and probably lasting achievement," holding critique and admiration together.
- Jackson the suburban historian documents how decentralization hollowed out central cities; Jackson the public historian is "Mr. New York," celebrant and institutional steward of dense urbanism.
- In the Moses debate he concedes Moses's anti-democratic methods and human costs while defending his net contribution and lamenting the participatory politics that constrains large public works — endorsing both the value of citizen input (implicitly) and the need for unilateral building capacity.
- He attributes mass suburbanization chiefly to federal policy ("the result, if not the intent") yet also asserts a pre-existing, centuries-old Anglo-American cultural preference for detached homes and anti-urbanism — leaving the relative weight of structure versus culture unresolved within his own account.
