# Sam Bankman-Fried: A Blind Reference Dossier

## Biography and Background

Sam Bankman-Fried (commonly "SBF") was born March 5, 1992, in Stanford, California. Both parents are professors at Stanford Law School: Joseph Bankman, a scholar of tax law and clinical psychology, and Barbara Fried, a legal scholar known for work on distributive justice and consequentialist ethics. He graduated from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 2014 with a bachelor's degree in physics and a minor in mathematics. In the summer of 2013 he interned at the proprietary trading firm Jane Street Capital, trading international ETFs, and returned full-time after graduation. He founded the quantitative trading firm Alameda Research in 2017 (named after his hometown of Alameda, California) and co-founded the cryptocurrency derivatives exchange FTX in 2019 with Gary Wang.

## The "Kimchi Premium" Origin Story

The founding narrative of Alameda centers on a cross-border Bitcoin arbitrage. In late 2017, Bankman-Fried observed that Bitcoin sometimes traded at large premiums on foreign exchanges relative to U.S. venues — at times he cited spreads up to ~60% across exchanges, though the most-cited figure is roughly 50%. The most famous instance was South Korea's "Kimchi premium," where intense local demand pushed Bitcoin prices well above U.S. levels (one widely repeated illustration: ~$10,000 in the U.S. versus ~$15,000 in Korea). A second leg ran through Japan. Bankman-Fried said the firm at times moved on the order of $25 million per day and could make as much as roughly $1 million per day. He attributed his edge less to spotting the trade than to executing the operational plumbing — securing exchange and bank approvals and physically moving large sums across borders daily — which most competitors could not solve.

## Effective Altruism, "Earning to Give," and Risk-Neutral Utilitarianism

Bankman-Fried was a prominent figure in the effective altruism (EA) movement, which he encountered through a talk by philosopher William MacAskill while at MIT. He adopted the "earning to give" strategy: pursue maximal income in order to donate it. He described himself as a utilitarian and stated an intention to keep roughly 1% of his earnings (or a floor near $100,000/year) and give the rest to charity, citing influences including GiveWell and longtermist causes. In February 2022 the FTX Foundation launched the FTX Future Fund, which planned to distribute $100 million in 2022 and reportedly disbursed over $190 million to causes including AI safety, biosecurity, and pandemic preparedness, as well as MacAskill's Centre for Effective Altruism.

His stated decision framework was risk-neutral expected-value maximization. In a March 2022 interview with economist Tyler Cowen, Cowen posed a hypothetical game: "51 percent, you double the Earth out somewhere else; 49 percent, it all disappears. Would you play that game? And would you keep on playing that, double or nothing?" Bankman-Fried did not reject repeated play; he reframed the St. Petersburg paradox as potentially compounding into "an enormously valuable existence" rather than inevitable destruction, accepting the literal expected-value logic even at planetary stakes.

The reasoning: a bet with positive expected value should be taken regardless of variance, scaled to any size. The strongest counterargument, advanced by writers including Sarah Constantin and discussed extensively on the EA Forum, is that infinite double-or-nothing iteration converges to ruin with probability one, and that genuine utility is not linear in money or world-states — making true risk-neutrality self-defeating. Critics including the EA Forum's own retrospectives and a Vox "How effective altruism let SBF happen" piece argued that his extreme corporate risk-taking was a direct expression of this philosophy, a connection EA leaders were initially reluctant to acknowledge. The internal tension: a charitable mission premised on doing the most good coexisted with a willingness to accept catastrophic-tail bets that could destroy the very capital base meant to fund that good.

## FTX's Rise

FTX raised $400 million in a Series C round in January 2022 reaching a $32 billion valuation, up from $25 billion in October 2021. The company built an aggressive consumer brand: it paid over $6 million for a 30-second 2022 Super Bowl ad starring Larry David ("Don't Miss Out"), bought naming rights to the Miami Heat's arena (FTX Arena), and secured endorsements from Tom Brady, Gisele Bündchen, Stephen Curry, and others. Bankman-Fried became a major political donor, giving about $40 million in the 2022 cycle — making him the second-largest donor to Democrats behind George Soros. The bulk (~$27 million) funded the "Protect Our Future PAC," focused on pandemic preparedness. He later claimed on a call with YouTuber Tiffany Fong that he had given comparable amounts to Republicans through undisclosed channels: "All my Republican donations were dark." FEC records did not corroborate equal partisan giving, and he was subsequently charged with campaign-finance violations.

## The Collapse (November 2022)

On November 2, 2022, CoinDesk published Alameda Research's balance sheet, reporting that roughly 40% of Alameda's ~$14.6 billion in assets consisted of FTX's own FTT token (the largest single holding), raising concerns the two firms' finances were dangerously entangled. On November 6, Binance CEO Changpeng "CZ" Zhao announced via tweet that Binance would liquidate its FTT holdings. That triggered roughly $5 billion in withdrawal requests within about 72 hours — a bank run — exposing an approximately $8 billion shortfall in customer funds. On November 11, 2022, FTX, Alameda Research, and over 100 affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and Bankman-Fried resigned as CEO. The FTX Future Fund team, including MacAskill, resigned the same week, citing fears the company "may have engaged in deception or dishonesty."

## The Vox / Kelsey Piper Interview (November 2022)

Days after the bankruptcy, Vox's Kelsey Piper published Twitter DMs exchanged with Bankman-Fried. Asked whether FTX's regulatory engagement had been substantive, he replied "fuck regulators" and said they "make everything worse." Asked whether he stood by his earlier statements about ethics, he wrote "all the dumb shit I said" and "it's not true, not really," describing prior ethical posturing as "this dumb game we woke westerners play where we say all the right shibboleths and so everyone likes us." He added "I feel bad for those who got fucked by it." Bankman-Fried later said he had believed he was speaking privately to Piper "as a friend" and that some remarks were "thoughtless or overly strong." The exchange became central evidence of a gap between his public advocacy and private views.

## Trial and Conviction

Bankman-Fried was arrested in the Bahamas in December 2022 and extradited to the United States. He faced seven counts of fraud, conspiracy, and money laundering. After a roughly month-long trial in the Southern District of New York before Judge Lewis Kaplan, a jury convicted him on all seven counts on November 2, 2023. On March 28, 2024, Kaplan sentenced him to 25 years in federal prison and ordered forfeiture of approximately $11 billion. An appeals court later upheld the conviction.

## His Stances and Defense

Bankman-Fried's core public claim, articulated in his January 12, 2023 Substack post "FTX Pre-Mortem Overview," was that FTX was fundamentally solvent and that no funds were stolen. He wrote: "I didn't steal funds, and I certainly didn't stash billions away. Nearly all of my assets were and still are utilizable to backstop FTX customers." He asserted FTX International retained roughly $8 billion in assets at the time of the management transfer, that FTX US was fully solvent (with ~$350 million net cash beyond customer balances), and that the failure was a liquidity crisis turned into insolvency by a bank run — driven by Alameda's under-hedged margin position during a market crash — rather than theft. He claimed that given "a few weeks" to raise liquidity, customers could have been made "substantially whole," and that if FTX International rebooted there was "a real possibility" of doing so.

On regulation, his pre-collapse positions were detailed in December 2021 testimony to the House Financial Services Committee and February 2022 Senate Agriculture Committee materials (his "Policy Goals for the Present Crypto Ecosystem"). He advocated a unified federal rulebook applying the same principles — customer protection, market integrity, anti-financial-crime, system safety — across spot and derivatives markets, and favored the CFTC (viewed as lighter-touch than the SEC) taking the lead role. He cultivated a "thought leader" media presence, testifying before Congress, publishing policy writing, and giving extensive interviews.

At trial, testifying in his own defense, Bankman-Fried said he made "a number of small mistakes and a number of larger mistakes," identifying the biggest as the absence of a dedicated risk-management team and chief risk officer, which produced "significant oversights." Asked directly whether he defrauded anyone or took customer funds, he answered "No." He attributed failures to mismanagement rather than intentional fraud and pointed responsibility toward subordinates.

## Counterarguments and Critics

John J. Ray III, appointed FTX's restructuring CEO and previously chairman of Enron's creditor-recovery entity, wrote in a court filing that in over 40 years of insolvency work he had "never" seen "such a complete failure of corporate controls and such a complete absence of trustworthy financial information." He described FTX as run by "a very small group of inexperienced, unsophisticated and potentially compromised individuals" and emphasized the lack of basic record-keeping, which he said distinguished FTX even from Enron.

Caroline Ellison, former Alameda CEO and Bankman-Fried's ex-girlfriend, testified as the prosecution's star witness. She said Bankman-Fried "directed me to commit these crimes," that he set up the system allowing Alameda to borrow from FTX customer funds without their knowledge, and that Alameda ultimately took around $14 billion. She also testified that Bankman-Fried asked that his luxury company car be traded for a Toyota Corolla because it "would play better in the media" — undercutting the authenticity of his frugal image. Co-founders Gary Wang (FTX's CTO) and Nishad Singh (director of engineering) also pleaded guilty and testified to the misuse of customer funds and the special code privileges granted to Alameda. Prosecutors used the documented gap between Bankman-Fried's public statements and private conduct to argue intent.

The EA movement faced sustained criticism over its association with him; commentators including a Vox post-mortem and Facebook co-founder Dustin Moskovitz's circle debated whether EA's expected-value culture enabled or excused his risk-taking. On lifestyle, critics contrasted his publicized frugality (the Toyota Corolla, beanbag sleeping, multiple roommates) against his residence in a roughly $30 million-plus penthouse at the Albany resort complex in Nassau, Bahamas — a five-bedroom unit shared with associates.

## Internal Tensions (stated as facts)

His utilitarian "ends justify the means" framing stood in direct conflict with a fraud conviction: the philosophy that any positive-expected-value act is justified collided with rules he was found to have broken. His "altruism" branding coexisted with the diversion of customer deposits to Alameda, political donations, and venture investments. His public advocacy for crypto regulation coexisted with his private "fuck regulators" remark to Vox. His self-presentation as a disciplined risk-manager coexisted with his courtroom admission that FTX had no dedicated risk function, and with a stated personal willingness to accept world-destroying tail risk for positive expected value. His Substack insistence that FTX was merely illiquid and never insolvent stood against John Ray III's finding of an "absence of trustworthy financial information" and the jury's conclusion that customer funds were stolen.
