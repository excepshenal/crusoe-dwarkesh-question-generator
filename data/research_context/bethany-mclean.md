# Bethany McLean — Reference Dossier

## Biography and Career Timeline

Bethany McLean was born December 12, 1970, in Hibbing, Minnesota, and graduated from Hibbing High School in 1988. She attended Williams College, earning a B.A. in 1992 with a double concentration in English and mathematics. The mathematics background is frequently cited as formative to her later facility with financial statements.

After college, McLean spent three years as an investment banking analyst at Goldman Sachs. She left finance for journalism, joining Fortune magazine in 1995 as a reporter. She rose through the masthead at Fortune, working as a writer, columnist (including the "Bulldog" column), and eventually editor-at-large. In 2008 she joined Vanity Fair as a contributing editor, a role she has held alongside other work. She has contributed a "Moneybox" column to Slate (beginning around 2010), and over time has written for or contributed to The New York Times, The Washington Post (as a columnist), The Atlantic, Yahoo Finance, and other outlets.

McLean is co-host of "Capitalisn't," a podcast produced by the Stigler Center at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business, alongside economist Luigi Zingales; the podcast examines where capitalism functions and where it fails. She earlier launched a podcast called "Making a Killing" on Luminary in 2019. She has served as an advisor and contributing editor at Hunterbrook Media, an outlet that pairs investigative journalism with trading. She lives in Chicago. She was married to Chris Wilford (2000–2006) and later to Sean M. Berkowitz (married 2008), a former federal prosecutor who led the Enron Task Force; she has two children.

## Enron and "The Smartest Guys in the Room"

McLean's signature work began with a Fortune article published in the March 5, 2001 issue titled "Is Enron Overpriced?" At the time Enron was Wall Street's "It Stock," having returned roughly 89% the prior year, with revenues that had more than doubled to over $100 billion and reported earnings up about 25%. The stock traded at roughly 55 times trailing earnings, and Enron itself argued the shares were worth around $126 — about 50% above the prevailing price.

McLean's central question was deceptively simple: "How exactly does Enron make its money?" She argued that the company's financial statements were nearly impenetrable, that nobody — including the analysts cheering the stock — could clearly explain how it generated profits. She marshaled specific concerns: return on equity around 13% and return on invested capital near 7% (comparable to risk-free Treasuries), net debt that had climbed to roughly $13 billion, a debt-to-capital ratio rising toward 50% from 39%, and operating cash flow of only about $100 million in the first nine months of 2000 versus roughly $1.2 billion in 1999. She flagged that discretionary asset sales appeared to be booked as recurring revenue and that the broadband unit was being assigned a $20–40 billion valuation despite roughly $408 million in revenue and $60 million in losses.

The article quoted key figures who would become central to the scandal: CEO Jeff Skilling described Enron as a "logistics company" that was "very simple to model"; CFO Andrew Fastow insisted "We are not a trading company"; Goldman Sachs analyst David Fleischer praised the firm's "extraordinary franchises"; and S&P's Todd Shipman said of the business model, "If you figure it out, let me know." McLean has recounted that Skilling initially called the line of inquiry "unethical" and that Fastow flew to New York to dissuade her, an episode she treats as illustrative of how aggressively the company managed its narrative.

Enron filed for bankruptcy in December 2001. McLean and Fortune colleague Peter Elkind expanded the reporting into "The Smartest Guys in the Room: The Amazing Rise and Scandalous Fall of Enron" (2003). The book's central thesis is that Enron's business was substantially an illusion that Wall Street accepted because nearly everyone stood to profit from believing it. The authors center the role of accounting — particularly mark-to-market accounting, which let Enron book the projected future profits of long-term deals as current income, and off-balance-sheet special-purpose entities (the "Raptors," LJM partnerships) that hid debt and losses. The book was adapted into the 2005 Oscar-nominated Alex Gibney documentary of the same name, which McLean and Elkind co-narrated.

A recurring tension McLean herself emphasizes about Enron is that she was not the only skeptic but was among the first in mainstream business media to publish the doubt; short-sellers such as Jim Chanos had been circulating concerns, and McLean has credited that early skeptical network as a source. The strongest counterpoint to crediting her article as the trigger is that the collapse was ultimately precipitated by Enron's own restatements and the unraveling of Fastow's partnerships in late 2001, not by press coverage — a sequencing McLean has acknowledged. Her enduring framing is less "I caught them" than "the information was public and almost nobody asked."

## The 2008 Financial Crisis and "All the Devils Are Here"

With Joe Nocera, then a New York Times columnist, McLean wrote "All the Devils Are Here: The Hidden History of the Financial Crisis" (2010). The title is drawn from "The Tempest." The book's organizing argument is that the crisis "wasn't about finance at all; it was about human nature," and that it was not an accident — that the major players largely understood the systemic picture and continued risky practices anyway.

The authors trace a long arc beginning in the 1980s with the invention of mortgage-backed securities at Salomon Brothers and the creation of the securitization machine, through the growth of subprime lenders, the role of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the credit-rating agencies, AIG's financial-products unit, and investment banks. A distinctive feature of their account is the assignment of "bipartisan" responsibility: a mixture of government policy (including affordable-housing goals and deregulation) and private-sector "laissez-faire" excess. On Fannie and Freddie specifically, the book documents that by 2008 the two guaranteed or owned roughly $5.3 trillion in mortgages, but argues they came to subprime relatively late, largely imitating the riskiest private originators rather than leading the bubble. A recurring image, noted by reviewers, is "unjustifiably self-assured men" making enormous bets with other people's money while poorly understanding the risks.

The book was widely praised (The Huffington Post called it the best business book of 2010; the New York Times called it among the most legible overviews). The principal counterargument comes from the political right and from analysts such as Peter Wallison (a dissenting member of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission), who argue that McLean and Nocera understate the causal role of government housing policy and the GSEs in inflating the bubble. A 2011 Cato Institute review, while calling it "one of the best books yet on the recent U.S. financial crisis," reflects the tension on the right between admiring the reporting and resisting the conclusion that private actors bore the larger share of blame. From the left, some critics felt the "everyone is to blame" framing diffused accountability and softened the case for prosecuting individuals — an internal tension between McLean's systemic, multi-causal storytelling and the public appetite for villains. McLean has repeatedly defended the complexity, arguing that "everyone wants to find the one villain" but that the honest account is messier.

## Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and "Shaky Ground"

McLean's "Shaky Ground: The Strange Saga of the U.S. Mortgage Giants" (2015), published by Columbia Global Reports as a short-form book, returned to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac seven years after the crisis. A central provocation is that the financial crisis arguably began not on September 15, 2008 (Lehman's bankruptcy) but nine days earlier, on September 6, 2008, when the U.S. Treasury placed Fannie and Freddie into conservatorship.

The book examines the post-crisis "limbo" in which the two companies remained in government conservatorship while the Treasury, under a 2012 "net worth sweep," took essentially all of their profits to reduce the federal deficit, leaving them with minimal capital reserves. McLean chronicles the resulting litigation — roughly twenty lawsuits — brought by hedge-fund investors (including figures associated with Perry Capital and Fairholme) who had bought the companies' stock and argued the government had unlawfully expropriated shareholder value. A signature observation is the irony she identifies in American policy: that the United States effectively "socializes" mortgage finance through government backing while "privatizing" much of health care — the reverse of most peer countries. The book's broader claim is that housing finance reform remained one of the largest unresolved problems left by the crisis, with no political consensus on how to wind down or replace the GSEs. A central tension she highlights is structural: the implicit (and during conservatorship, explicit) government guarantee underpins the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage that Americans expect, even as policymakers profess to want the government out of housing.

## Fracking and "Saudi America"

"Saudi America: The Truth About Fracking and How It's Changing the World" (2018), also a short Columbia Global Reports book of roughly 130 pages, examines the shale boom centered on the Permian Basin in Texas that made the United States the world's top producer of oil and natural gas. McLean's core financial argument is that fracking is "a business built on attracting ever-more gigantic amounts of capital investment, while promises of huge returns have yet to bear out" — that many shale producers spent more cash than they generated and survived on cheap debt and continual equity raises rather than free cash flow.

The narrative is anchored by Aubrey McClendon, the flamboyant co-founder of Chesapeake Energy and an early shale-gas pioneer, whose habit of overpaying for drilling rights and financing a lavish lifestyle, McLean argues, outran even his exceptional fundraising abilities; McClendon died in a single-car crash in 2016, one day after being indicted, an episode she treats as emblematic of the industry's boom-and-bust character. She draws on data including David Einhorn's shale-skeptic analysis and Chesapeake and E&P company financials. A key macro warning is that the model depends on a low-interest-rate environment; if rates rose, the cheap capital sustaining unprofitable drilling could dry up. She also questions the geopolitical narrative of "energy independence" and the "drill, baby, drill" rhetoric, arguing it overstates the durability and economics of the boom.

"Saudi America" drew direct industry rebuttal. The energy law firm Haynes and Boone and analysts (including the investor Simon Lack at SL-Advisors, who argued "the shale revolution is real") contended that McLean relied on outdated datasets — Chesapeake numbers from 2002–2012, Einhorn's 2006–2014 figures, E&P data from 2012–2017 — reflecting a pre-maturity industry and a period of historically low oil prices and higher break-even costs. Critics cited IEA projections that the shale industry would turn free-cash-flow positive in 2018 and Wood Mackenzie estimates that larger U.S. shale companies needed only about $53 a barrel to generate free cash, and emphasized that shale's short-cycle economics (wells under ~$10 million repaying capital within roughly two years) were a strength, not a weakness. The internal tension in McLean's position is timing: while the larger shale players did improve cash generation in subsequent years (partly vindicating critics), the broader pattern of capital destruction across the sector and later bankruptcies (including Chesapeake's 2020 filing) lent support to her skepticism. Her framing was financial sustainability rather than geological capacity — she did not dispute that the oil was there, only whether producing it reliably made money.

## COVID-19 and "The Big Fail"

"The Big Fail: What the Pandemic Revealed About Who America Protects and Who It Leaves Behind" (2023), again co-authored with Joe Nocera, argues that the U.S. pandemic response was a profound policy failure that protected the affluent and well-educated while harming small businesses and vulnerable populations. The authors characterize the broad lockdowns as among the costliest peacetime public-policy mistakes, arguing they lacked a strong prior evidence base and that leaders failed to revisit the original "flatten the curve" rationale once hospital-overwhelm fears passed.

The book documents distributional harms with specifics: roughly 6,000 permanent restaurant closures in New York City and about 20,000 in California, around 2.5 million restaurant-worker job losses, and youth unemployment near 17%. It contrasts school policy across states — noting that many private schools and schools in Republican-led states reopened while public schools in many Democratic-led states stayed closed, contributing to steep declines in student test scores concentrated in high-poverty areas. The authors frame the partisan extremes through Andrew Cuomo's New York lockdowns and Ron DeSantis's decision to reopen Florida (associated with scientist Jay Bhattacharya). They also criticize the CDC for reliance on worst-case computer models and note epidemiologist D.A. Henderson's 2006 warning that aggressive quarantine measures could be "devastating socially and economically." The book additionally examines the financialization of health care — including private equity in hospitals and the fragility of supply chains — as a pre-existing vulnerability.

Notably, the book is not uniformly anti-intervention: it praises Operation Warp Speed as a demonstration of government-industry capacity to accelerate vaccine development, a stance Nocera has noted irritates conservatives who otherwise applaud the lockdown criticism. Reviewers such as John Tierney in City Journal credited the authors as veteran, non-partisan journalists who criticize Republicans and corporate executives as well as Democrats. The strongest counterargument, voiced by many public-health figures and writers, is that the book benefits from hindsight — that decisions made under acute uncertainty in early 2020, before vaccines and with limited data, look worse in retrospect than they were at the time, and that lockdowns plausibly saved lives during the initial surge. This represents an internal tension in McLean's body of work: her Enron-era insistence that the evidence was knowable in real time sits uneasily against a pandemic critique that relies substantially on information that accumulated after the key decisions were made.

## Recurring Intellectual Themes

Across her work, several through-lines recur. The first is skepticism of consensus narratives: McLean argues that "skepticism about business tends to travel in a very small circle" because nearly everyone — analysts, bankers, employees, investors — is "biased toward belief" since most people profit when a stock rises. She frames her core method as relentless reporting without shortcuts: "you just have to call everybody, talk to everybody, be open-minded, and ask as many questions as you can."

A second theme is her resistance to the simple villain. She repeatedly argues that the public "wants to find the one villain" but that catastrophes are produced by many mutually reinforcing actors and incentives. Related to this is her view of "good people" and the role of ordinary human nature in fraud — that the business world is "incredibly emotional," not cold and rational, and that disasters often involve self-deception and groupthink as much as outright criminality.

A third theme is the visionary-fraudster spectrum. McLean argues that the visionary and the fraudster, seemingly at opposite ends, "actually meet in the circle," and that "the only thing that separates them is that the visionary gets lucky and it all works out and the fraudster gets caught." She has applied this lens beyond her books to figures including Elizabeth Holmes (Theranos) and Sam Bankman-Fried (FTX), and to debates about figures such as Elon Musk, treating the line between transformative ambition and fraud as contingent on outcomes.

A fourth, more technical theme is accounting and disclosure as the locus of deception. From Enron's mark-to-market and off-balance-sheet entities, to the opacity of mortgage securitization, to shale producers' cash-flow versus reported-earnings gap, McLean returns to the gap between what financial statements report and what is economically real, and to the failure of gatekeepers — auditors, rating agencies, analysts, regulators — to police that gap. An internal tension across her career is her dual identity: a former Goldman Sachs analyst whose financial fluency is her chief journalistic asset, yet whose work is fundamentally a critique of the finance industry she trained in. Critics from the industry have at times used her shorter, argument-driven books ("Saudi America," "Shaky Ground") to charge that her skepticism can harden into a thesis that data later complicates, while defenders note that her central warnings — about Enron, about pre-2008 mortgage risk, about shale's capital appetite — repeatedly identified fragilities that consensus opinion missed.
