# Scott Alexander — Reference Dossier

Scott Alexander is the pen name of Scott Alexander Siskind (born 1984), a San Francisco Bay Area psychiatrist and one of the most widely read writers in the "rationalist" intellectual community. After studying philosophy as an undergraduate, he earned his medical degree from University College Cork (Ireland) in 2012 and completed a psychiatry residency in Michigan; he specializes in treatment-resistant depression and has interests in chronotherapy, behavioral genetics, and the ontology of psychiatric disorders. He now runs a low-overhead telepsychiatry practice, Lorien Psychiatry. Writing first on the community forum LessWrong (under the handle "Yvain") and then on his own blog Slate Star Codex (launched February 12, 2013), he built a large readership across science, medicine, philosophy, politics, statistics, and futurism. He deleted Slate Star Codex on June 23, 2020 after a *New York Times* reporter (Cade Metz) indicated the paper would print his legal surname; he relaunched on Substack as Astral Codex Ten (ACX) on January 21, 2021. The *Times* article, "Silicon Valley's Safe Space," ran February 13, 2021. His corpus is enormous — a PDF of the SSC archive runs over nine thousand pages — and a large share of it is long literature reviews and book reviews.

## Method and Epistemic Style

Alexander's signature mode is the exhaustive, heavily hedged survey of a contested empirical question — posts in the "Much More Than You Wanted To Know" format (e.g., on SSRIs, marijuana, the minimum wage, face masks) that aggregate studies, weigh effect sizes, and arrive at a probabilistic rather than categorical verdict. His stated writing principles, set out in "Nonfiction Writing Advice" (2016), include pre-empting the reader's objections ("Anticipate and defuse counterarguments — you sound a lot more credible if you're the one who brings the possible counterarguments up yourself"), favoring concrete examples, and using "microhumor." He treats calibration as a discipline: every year he publishes dated, probability-tagged predictions and grades them against outcomes, on the principle that one's 70%-confidence claims should come true about 70% of the time. He launched and popularized the ACX/Metaculus prediction contest and is a vocal advocate of prediction markets as forecasting tools that, with skin in the game, tend to outperform pundits.

A recurring meta-theme is epistemic humility about one's own reasoning. In "Epistemic Learned Helplessness" he argues that for most people, on most topics outside their expertise, *refusing* to be moved by a clever argument is the rational default — because persuasive-sounding arguments can be constructed for false conclusions (he cites being temporarily convinced by Velikovsky's pseudo-history), and a layperson cannot reliably tell a sound argument from a sophistical one. This sits in productive tension with the rationalist ideal of "updating" on evidence: he simultaneously champions belief revision and warns that excessive openness to argument is itself a failure mode.

## Moloch, Coordination Failure, and Transhumanism

His most influential essay, "Meditations on Moloch" (2014), uses Allen Ginsberg's "Howl" to personify "Moloch" as the impersonal optimization process that destroys human values through competition. The mechanism is the **multipolar trap**: "in some competition optimizing for X, the opportunity arises to throw some other value under the bus for improved X. Those who take it prosper. Those who don't take it die out." He runs through roughly a dozen examples — prisoner's dilemmas, dollar auctions, a fish-farming pollution case, Malthusian population traps, competitive capitalism, arms races, regulatory races to the bottom, cancer as cellular defection. Four things currently restrain Moloch, he argues: physical limits, an unusual abundance of "excess resources" (the modern "whalefall"), the fact that some systems happen to optimize for human satisfaction (capitalism for customers, democracy for voters), and explicit coordination (governments, norms, institutions).

He rejects two rival responses. Against Nick Land's "Gnon" (nature's law, which reactionaries say we should submit to), he answers that the same nature that rewards work also kills everyone, so submission buys nothing. Against Warg Franklin's "walled garden" — a self-contained rational civilization that opts out of the competition — he objects that no garden is safe from foreign armies, memes, diseases, or competition. His own answer is explicitly transhumanist: because Moloch lives in the competitive substrate, the only escape is to lift civilization above that substrate via a value-aligned superintelligence, which he names "Elua," the god of "art and science and philosophy and love." He closes, "I am a transhumanist because I do not have enough hubris not to try to kill God." The essay's strongest counterpressure comes from critics who note its abstraction risks naturalizing real harms (treating contingent political choices as inevitable thermodynamic doom) and that the proposed solution — a benevolent singleton AI — is itself a coordination problem of staggering difficulty, arguably another Moloch. There is also a tension internal to his thought: he is a committed pluralist and anti-authoritarian elsewhere, yet "lifting value above competition" implies a single global optimizer, which is in some sense the centralized control he distrusts.

## AI Risk and Forecasting

Alexander is a long-standing popularizer of AI-safety concerns. His "Superintelligence FAQ" (2016) is a widely cited lay introduction to the alignment problem; he takes as given that AI can be both smarter than humans and dangerous, and is more interested in risk taxonomies and alignment theory than in re-litigating whether risk exists. In "Why Not Slow AI Progress?" he engages the dilemma that slowing Western labs may simply hand the lead to less safety-conscious actors. He co-authored and championed the "AI 2027" scenario, a concrete narrative forecast in which misaligned AI could move against human interests as early as 2030, and has written on AI as "profoundly abnormal technology" whose adversarial potential breaks ordinary assumptions about how technologies are governed. The strongest opposing voices he engages are AI-risk skeptics who argue that scaling does not imply agency or hostile goals, and accelerationists who argue the expected benefits of faster AI dominate speculative tail risks; critics also note that his probabilistic, scenario-based forecasts are hard to falsify. He situates himself adjacent to but not identical with effective altruism (EA): he endorses giving roughly 10% of income to effective charities and points readers to Giving What We Can, while also writing internal critiques of EA's culture and blind spots.

## Categories, Definitions, and the Trans Debate

"The Categories Were Made For Man, Not Man For The Categories" (2014) argues that categories are tools justified by usefulness rather than facts about reality: whether a whale is a "fish" depends on whether you're a biologist tracking evolutionary descent or King Solomon sorting sea creatures from land animals, and neither is strictly "wrong." He deploys this anti-essentialism to defend using transgender people's preferred categories on pragmatic, harm-reduction grounds — accommodation costs little and reduces suffering. The essay drew sharp replies from two directions: gender-critical writers said it dissolves any objective basis for sex categories, while some trans and rationalist critics (e.g., the "categories were made for man *to make predictions*" rejoinder) argued he overstated category flexibility and that some categorizations really are more accurate than others. The episode highlights a tension: his pragmatism about definitions coexists with his realism about empirical facts elsewhere, and critics press on where the line falls.

## Tribalism, Outrage, and Polarization

"I Can Tolerate Anything Except The Outgroup" (2014) argues that the "tolerance" people pride themselves on usually means tolerance of distant groups, not of the near, similar group they actually loathe — for an American "Blue Tribe" liberal, the genuine outgroup is not al-Qaeda but the "Red Tribe." He opens by noting the Nazis' outgroup was not the very-different Japanese but the near-identical German Jews, and contends real tolerance means enduring people you genuinely find distasteful — including, uncomfortably, one's political opposites. "The Toxoplasma of Rage" (2014) explains why the most divisive, ambiguous cases (rather than clear injustices) go viral: like the toxoplasma parasite, controversies spread *because* they provoke disagreement — activists amplify weak, contestable stories to signal zeal, opponents amplify the same stories to mock the activists, and the outrage cycle selects for maximally divisive content. The short story "Sort By Controversial" coins "Shiri's scissor" (the "scissor statement") — a proposition engineered to split any population into two camps that find each other incomprehensibly evil. These pieces are frequently cited across the political spectrum; the recurring critique is that his even-handed framing can flatten genuine asymmetries of power or harm into a symmetric "both tribes do it" picture.

## Cost Disease, Institutions, and Slow Decline

"Considerations On Cost Disease" (2017) catalogs how, in real (inflation-adjusted) terms over roughly fifty years, U.S. K–12 spending per pupil more than doubled, college tuition rose roughly tenfold, healthcare costs quintupled, and subway construction costs ballooned — all without commensurate improvement in outcomes (e.g., flat test scores). He canvasses candidate explanations (Baumol's cost disease, regulation, risk aversion, administrative bloat, rising expectations) without confidently settling on one, and argues the phenomenon reframes political fights over "free college" or universal healthcare as quarrels over how to *distribute* losses from a broken cost structure. The piece is widely credited with seeding a broader "progress studies" and abundance discourse. "Who By Very Slow Decay" (2013), drawn from his clinical experience, is a stark account of how modern medicine prolongs dying, and is among his most-praised non-analytical essays.

## Engagement With the Reactionary Right and the Doxxing Saga

"The Anti-Reactionary FAQ" (2013) is a ~30,000-word point-by-point rebuttal of neoreaction (the "Dark Enlightenment"), including Curtis Yarvin's positions, arguing against claims of civilizational decline and natural hierarchy. Alexander's deliberate strategy of arguing *against* such views by first stating them charitably and at length became a flashpoint: supporters saw it as a model of steelmanning, while critics argued that platforming and seriously engaging far-right ideas lent them legitimacy "by association." (He later appended a partial retraction, saying section 1 was weaker than it should be.) This controversy intensified in 2021 when a 2014 private email of his was leaked in which he expressed sympathy for "human biodiversity" (HBD) ideas and recommended writers such as Steve Sailer; defenders frame this as private, exploratory hedging and note his public anti-racist arguments, while critics read it as evidence of genuinely held views he soft-pedaled in public. This is the central live tension in assessments of him: between the persona of a scrupulously fair, charity-first reasoner and the charge that the same charity was selectively extended rightward.

The 2020 *New York Times* episode is the other defining public event. Alexander said he wrote pseudonymously because he is a practicing psychiatrist (revealing his identity could compromise patients and his ability to treat them) and because he had received death threats and harassment. When the *Times* declined to omit his surname, he deleted the blog in protest. The affair became a proxy battle over journalistic norms and Silicon Valley's relationship with the press, covered by Reason, the New Statesman, National Review, and — most prominently — Gideon Lewis-Kraus's New Yorker piece, "Slate Star Codex and Silicon Valley's War Against the Media."

## Influences and Interlocutors

His intellectual lineage runs through the LessWrong rationalist tradition (Eliezer Yudkowsky), Bayesian epistemics, behavioral economics and cognitive-bias literature, and the AI-risk work of Nick Bostrom (whose *Superintelligence* anchors "Meditations on Moloch" alongside Ginsberg and Nick Land). He is a hub figure for effective altruism, prediction markets/forecasting (Metaculus, Robin Hanson's work on signaling and futarchy), and the "progress studies" movement. Frequent interlocutors and critics include Bryan Caplan (who replied to "Untitled" on shy male nerds), Robin Hanson, Zvi Mowshowitz, and a wide field of left and right commentators who treat his essays as reference points. His running themes — multipolar traps, calibration, charitable interpretation, category pragmatism, the gulf between tribes, and institutional cost disease — recur as a stable conceptual toolkit across more than a decade of writing.
