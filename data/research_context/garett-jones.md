# Garett Jones — Blind Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from Garett Jones's public record: his three books, his academic papers, his Mercatus/GMU profiles, and published reviews. It handles the IQ, democracy, and migration material as scholarly subject matter, presenting his claims alongside the strongest documented objections.

## Biography and Career

Garett Jones (born April 6, 1970) is an American economist. He is an associate professor of economics at George Mason University and the BB&T Professor for the Study of Capitalism at GMU's Mercatus Center. He sits in the economics department associated with George Mason's tradition in public choice — the school founded by James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock — and his work draws heavily on the public-choice and Austrian-adjacent intellectual currents prominent there.

His training is unusually interdisciplinary. He earned a B.A. in history (with a sociology minor) from Brigham Young University in 1992, an M.P.A. in public affairs from Cornell University in 1993, an M.A. in political science from the University of California, Berkeley in 1994, and finally a Ph.D. in economics from the University of California, San Diego in 2000. He joined the George Mason faculty in 2007 after a series of visiting and junior academic positions. He served as a staffer in the U.S. Senate, an experience he repeatedly cites as the origin of his interest in how electoral proximity changes legislator behavior. He has served on the editorial board of the *Journal of Neuroscience, Psychology, and Economics* and was a guest blogger at EconLog (2012–2013).

His primary fields are macroeconomics and monetary economics, the microfoundations of economic growth, and the relationship between cognitive skill and productivity. He has published three trade books with Stanford University Press — *Hive Mind* (2015), *10% Less Democracy* (2020), and *The Culture Transplant* (2022) — which together form what he and critics describe as a loose trilogy on the non-obvious foundations of national prosperity. He also edited *Banking Crises: Perspectives from the New Palgrave Dictionary* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2016).

## Short-Term Macro and the Monetary Work

Before the trade books, Jones's published research was substantially in monetary macroeconomics, often co-authored with R.W. Hafer. Representative papers include "On Money and Output: Is Money Redundant?" (with Hafer and Joseph Haslag, *Journal of Monetary Economics*, 2007), which examined whether money supply adds predictive content beyond interest rates, and "Dynamic IS Curves With and Without Money: An International Comparison" (with Hafer, *Journal of International Money and Finance*, 2008). In the wake of the 2008 financial crisis he proposed "speed bankruptcy" — a fast, pre-planned firewall for converting failing firms' debt to equity — in "Speed Bankruptcy: A Firewall to Future Crises" (*Journal of Applied Corporate Finance*, 2010). This short-term macro and monetary-policy expertise grounds his later argument for insulated central banks: he treats the technocratic, independent central bank as the clearest worked example of democracy deliberately constrained for good outcomes.

## The "Garett Jones Worker"

A concept associated with Jones in macroeconomic commentary is the "Garett Jones worker" — his observation, advanced during the slow post-2008 recovery, that a large and growing share of employees in modern economies do not produce a measurable current-period widget but instead build organizational capital: writing software, designing processes, training others, and building intangible assets whose payoff is deferred. Jones argued that this changes how recessions and layoffs should be interpreted, because cutting such workers reduces the future capital stock rather than current output, and because their "production" does not show up cleanly in short-run productivity statistics. The idea connects his labor-market thinking to his broader claim that the most economically consequential human capital is cognitive and organizational rather than manual.

## Hive Mind (2015)

*Hive Mind: How Your Nation's IQ Matters So Much More Than Your Own* (Stanford University Press, November 2015) builds on Jones's academic papers on IQ and productivity. Its organizing puzzle is what Jones calls **the paradox of IQ**: at the individual level, a person's IQ score is only a modest predictor of that person's income, whereas at the national level, a country's average test score is a very strong predictor of national prosperity. Jones reports that the nations with the highest average measured IQ are roughly **eight times wealthier** than those with the lowest, a cross-country relationship far stronger than the within-country individual relationship.

The quantitative core comes from his paper "IQ in the Production Function: Evidence from Immigrant Earnings" (*Economic Inquiry*, 2010) and "IQ and National Productivity." At the individual level he finds that **one IQ point predicts roughly 1% higher wages**. At the national level the effect is far larger — his earlier finding (with co-author/psychologist work) was that IQ mattered roughly **six times more for countries than for individuals**, i.e., the national return is several times the private return. He estimates that large, persistent differences in national average IQ acting through the private marginal product of labor can account for **about one-sixth of global income inequality**, before accounting for spillovers.

The mechanism for the gap between private and social returns is **positive spillovers / externalities of intelligence**. Jones argues that the social return to cognitive skill exceeds the private return because higher-scoring people are, on average, more patient (lower time preference, hence higher savings and investment), more cooperative, better at sustaining beneficial norms, more supportive of market-friendly and growth-friendly policies, and better at adopting and diffusing technology. He summarizes the channels roughly as: higher savings rates, more cooperation, more market-oriented policy support, and faster technology diffusion. Supporting papers include "Will the Intelligent Inherit the Earth? IQ and Time Preference in the Global Economy" and "Cognitive Ability and Technology Diffusion: An Empirical Test" (*Economic Systems*, 2012).

The **O-ring model** is central to the externality story. Borrowing Michael Kremer's 1993 "O-Ring Theory of Economic Development" (named for the Challenger disaster's failed component), Jones contrasts **O-ring sectors** — where production is a chain of tasks and a single error destroys the whole product, so a low-skill worker can have an effectively *negative* marginal product — with **"foolproof" sectors**, where mistakes are cheaply caught and three low-skill workers can substitute for one high-skill worker. In O-ring sectors, high-skill workers are complements, so being surrounded by high-skill colleagues raises everyone's productivity, generating the spillover. He develops this formally in "The O-Ring Sector and the Foolproof Sector: An Explanation for Skill Externalities" (*Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*, 2013).

His cooperation evidence comes from "Are Smarter Groups More Cooperative? Evidence from Prisoner's Dilemma Experiments, 1959–2003" (*Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization*, 2008) and follow-on experimental work with Omar Al-Ubaydli and Jaap Weel. A meta-analysis of repeated prisoner's-dilemma experiments found that groups composed of higher-scoring students cooperated substantially more. In their own experiments, a pair's average cognitive ability (Raven's test) robustly predicted cooperation, with higher scorers more likely to sustain positive reciprocity; the reported pattern was that a pair's IQ was about **five times more important than an individual's IQ** for producing cooperation. He also documented related effects in "Patience, Cognitive Skill, and Coordination in the Repeated Stag Hunt" and used the famous "Human Capital in the Creation of Social Capital: Evidence from Diplomatic Parking Tickets" study (with John Nye) as illustration that culturally transmitted norms travel with people.

**Counterarguments and tensions.** The book's empirical foundation rests partly on the national-IQ datasets assembled by Richard Lynn and Tatu Vanhanen, which have been heavily criticized. Jelte Wicherts, Denny Borsboom, and Conor Dolan argued the Lynn–Vanhanen data rely on unsystematic study selection and unrepresentative samples; Wicherts and colleagues showed that including studies Lynn excluded raised the estimated sub-Saharan African average from about 67 to about 82. Ken Richardson and others contest whether cross-national score differences reflect a stable trait at all rather than schooling, nutrition, health, test familiarity, and the Flynn effect (the secular rise in scores over the 20th century, which Jones acknowledges and which complicates any innate interpretation). Jones's stated position is agnostic on the causes of score differences — he argues the *predictive* relationship and the spillover mechanism hold regardless of whether the differences are genetic or environmental, and he emphasizes that scores are malleable. Critics counter that if scores are largely environmental and policy-responsive, the framing of "your nation's IQ" overstates fixity and risks misreading correlation as something closer to destiny.

## 10% Less Democracy (2020)

*10% Less Democracy: Why You Should Trust Elites a Little More and the Masses a Little Less* (Stanford University Press, February 2020) argues that the world's richest, most stable democracies would govern slightly better with a modest reduction — on the order of 10% — in direct democratic accountability, ceding a bit more authority to insulated experts and longer-horizon institutions.

The central analytic device is an **inverted-U (Laffer-style) relationship** between democracy and good governance. Jones argues that very low levels of democracy produce predation and bad outcomes, that increasing democracy improves outcomes over a wide range, but that beyond some peak additional democratic responsiveness *reduces* policy quality by amplifying voter myopia and short-termism. His claim is that the wealthy democracies sit at or slightly past that peak, so the marginal adjustment should be toward *less* democracy, carefully chosen — not toward authoritarianism.

His **strongest worked example is central-bank independence**. The cross-country evidence that more independent central banks deliver lower and more stable inflation without sacrificing growth is, in his telling, the cleanest natural experiment in deliberately removing a policy lever from direct electoral control. He extends the logic to **judicial independence** (appointed judges, with long or life tenure, outperform frequently elected judges on measures like consistency and resistance to local pressure) and to **longer terms in office**. Drawing on his Senate experience and on empirical work showing U.S. senators vote differently as their re-election approaches, he argues longer terms buy policymakers room to take politically costly but socially beneficial actions.

He extends the argument toward **"regulation" of governments by financial markets** — letting bondholders and creditors exert discipline on fiscal policy, and even floating the provocative idea of giving creditors or bondholders a formal voice. And he discusses **epistocracy** — rule weighted toward the knowledgeable, e.g., extra votes or influence for the more informed — as the most radical end of the spectrum, treating it as a live idea worth examining rather than a concrete proposal. He uses **Singapore** as an example of a high-functioning state operating at something like "50% less democracy," while acknowledging the measurement difficulty in placing it.

**Counterarguments and tensions.** Bryan Caplan — a GMU colleague and frequent interlocutor — praised the book's spirit but pressed that the democracy-and-growth empirics are "muddled," that Jones offers few clear cases where longer terms produced persistently bad policy (an asymmetry that weakens the inverted-U), and that the claim that some corruption/transactional politics is functionally necessary is the book's weakest part. Reviewer Robert Murphy (Independent Review) noted that recent meta-analyses, citing Colagrossi et al. (2020), find *clearly positive* effects of democracy on growth, cutting against the premise, and that Jones's chosen dimensions of "democracy" may not map onto the institutional variation that actually matters. A standing internal tension is that Jones's preferred technocratic insulation sits uneasily with the classical-liberal and public-choice skepticism of unaccountable administrative power that animates much of his own intellectual home at GMU: the same public-choice logic that warns voters are myopic also warns that insulated elites pursue their own interests. The Singapore example exposes the measurement problem — labeling it "50% less democracy" relies on contestable democracy indices.

## The Culture Transplant (2022)

*The Culture Transplant: How Migrants Make the Economies They Move to a Lot Like the Ones They Left* (Stanford University Press, November 2022) argues that migrants carry culturally transmitted economic attitudes — toward saving, trust, cooperation, and the role of government — that persist across multiple generations and partially reshape destination economies. Its core empirical claim is that **full assimilation within a generation or two is a myth**: measurable home-country traits decay slowly, persisting for decades and plausibly centuries.

The book sits on the **"deep roots" literature** in economic growth. Jones leans on Louis Putterman and David Weil's construction of a **migration matrix** estimating, for each modern country, where its population's ancestors lived circa 1500, distinguishing voluntary from coerced (e.g., enslaved) migration. The key deep-roots result Jones builds on is that modern national prosperity is predicted far better by the **history of a nation's people** (where the ancestors came from) than by the **history of the land** — adjusting for migration sharply improves the fit. He also draws on Oded Galor's long-run growth work.

His preferred summary measure is the **SAT score** — a weighted index of **S**tate history (how long ancestors lived under organized states), **A**gricultural history (how long under settled farming), and **T**echnology history (technological sophistication around 1500) — with the variant he favors, **SAT\***, weighting **T** most heavily. The migration-adjusted SAT\* score (ancestry-weighted) tracks present-day prosperity closely. Reviewer Alex Nowrasteh records specific values from the book: the **U.S. SAT\* of 0.73**, with **40 countries (about 2.8 billion people) scoring higher** — including China, Brazil, Vietnam, and Russia; Argentina at 0.81; Singapore at 0.75; and a global weighted average around 0.65. The "Spengler" thread refers to Joseph Spengler's older argument and to evidence that the *composition* of migration flows, not just their size, shapes long-run development.

Jones marshals persistence evidence: inherited-trust findings (notably Yann Algan and Pierre Cahuc's 2010 work tracing the trust attitudes of Americans back to their ancestors' countries of origin), studies of savings behavior persisting across immigrant generations, and the diplomatic-parking-ticket finding that corruption norms travel with people. The policy implication he draws is cautious and asymmetric: because migrants durably import the cultural traits of their origin countries, destination countries — and especially already-prosperous, high-trust ones — have a legitimate long-run interest in *who* they admit, since large flows from lower-SAT origins could, over generations, erode the institutional and cultural substrate that made the destination prosperous. He frames this as a reason for selectivity rather than closure, and explicitly notes that on standard measures large high-trust-to-high-trust flows would be benign.

**Counterarguments and tensions.** The sharpest published critic is Alex Nowrasteh of the Cato Institute, himself an open-immigration economist. Nowrasteh's two-part review levels several objections: (1) the book does little original empirical work and leans on a thin slice of the literature; (2) the inherited-trust results rest on small samples and a single anomalous World Values Survey wave (1999–2004), with Müller et al. (2012) and Eder (2018) finding the effects statistically insignificant under alternative specifications, and survey-measured "trust" correlating only weakly (around 0.34) with actual trusting behavior; (3) Jones never specifies a decay rate or a bounded number of generations over which traits persist, leaving the persistence claim hard to falsify; and (4) institutions, not transplanted culture, may do the causal work — Nowrasteh invokes the "doctrine of first effective settlement" and presents a synthetic-control analysis of Singapore's post-1981 immigration liberalization finding no significant divergence in GDP per capita, total factor productivity, or economic freedom despite very high foreign-born and ethnic-Chinese shares. He also cites Briggeman's 2022 replication reporting that Putterman–Weil's deep-roots results weaken and lose significance once major outliers (China, India, the United States) are accounted for. Bryan Caplan likewise argues that even granting cultural persistence, the gains from migration to migrants and to global output dwarf the modeled cultural externalities. A recognized internal tension is that the deep-roots data Jones relies on inherit the same data-quality and causal-inference disputes that surround the national-IQ datasets used in *Hive Mind*, and that "culture" in the book is measured largely through proxies (trust surveys, ancestry indices) whose construct validity critics contest.

## Recurring Themes Across the Trilogy

A consistent through-line is that the most economically decisive variables are *non-obvious and slow-moving* — average cognitive skill, the durability of culture, and institutional insulation — rather than the levers that dominate public debate. A second is the gap between **private and social returns**: in *Hive Mind* the social return to skill exceeds the private return; in *The Culture Transplant* an individual's migration can impose or confer externalities on the destination's long-run institutions; in *10% Less Democracy* the social value of insulating decisions exceeds what myopic voters would individually choose. A third is methodological: Jones repeatedly positions himself as "standing athwart" expert and popular consensus, a posture critics credit as intellectually useful even where they reject his conclusions. The unifying empirical vulnerability his critics identify is reliance on contested cross-country datasets (Lynn–Vanhanen IQ scores, Putterman–Weil ancestry matrices, World Values Survey trust measures) whose measurement and causal interpretation remain disputed.
