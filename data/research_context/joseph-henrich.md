# Joseph Henrich — Reference Dossier

## Biography and Career

Joseph Patrick Henrich (born 1968) is an American evolutionary anthropologist whose work fuses evolutionary theory, anthropology, psychology, and economics. He is unusual among anthropologists in having a dual undergraduate background: in 1991 he earned both a B.A. in anthropology and a B.S. in aerospace engineering from the University of Notre Dame. Between 1991 and 1993 he worked as a test and evaluation systems engineer at General Electric Aerospace in Springfield, Virginia, before returning to academia. He completed an M.A. (1995) and a Ph.D. (1999) in anthropology at UCLA, where he studied under cultural-evolution theorist Robert Boyd.

His academic appointments trace a path through several disciplines. He was on the faculty of the Department of Anthropology at Emory University from 2002 to 2007. From 2007 to 2015 he held the Canada Research Chair in Culture, Cognition and Coevolution at the University of British Columbia, where—notably for an anthropologist—he held a joint professorship across the departments of psychology and economics. In 2015 he was named Professor and Chair of the Department of Human Evolutionary Biology at Harvard University; he is currently the Ruth Moore Professor of Biological Anthropology there.

Honors include the 2003 Presidential Early Career Award for Scientists and Engineers (PECASE) and the 2022 Hayek Prize (awarded by the Manhattan Institute for *The WEIRDest People in the World*). His early ethnographic fieldwork was conducted among the Machiguenga horticulturalists of the Peruvian Amazon and the Mapuche of Chile; he later helped coordinate cross-cultural experimental fieldwork across more than a dozen small-scale societies on five continents.

His major books are: *Foundations of Human Sociality* (2004, edited volume); *Why Humans Cooperate* (2007); *Experimenting with Social Norms* (2014, edited); *The Secret of Our Success* (2015/2016, Princeton University Press); and *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020, Farrar, Straus and Giroux).

## Theoretical Foundations: Dual Inheritance and Cultural Evolution

Henrich's entire program rests on **dual inheritance theory** (also called gene-culture coevolution), the framework formalized by population geneticists Luigi Luca Cavalli-Sforza and Marcus Feldman (1981) and by Henrich's mentors Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson (*Culture and the Evolutionary Process*, 1985). The core claim is that humans inherit information through two parallel, interacting systems: genes transmitted biologically, and culture transmitted socially. Culture, on this view, is not a thin veneer over a fixed human nature; it is a second inheritance system subject to its own selection-like dynamics, and the two systems coevolve.

Henrich's distinctive contribution is the argument that culture became the *dominant* driver of human evolution. He contends that natural selection favored increasingly sophisticated social learning—the capacity to faithfully copy others—because copying accumulated cultural know-how outperforms individual trial-and-error. This created selection pressure for larger brains, longer childhoods, and a suite of learning biases. Genes adapted to culture, in other words, not merely the reverse. He calls humans a "cultural species" whose biology has been domesticated by its own cultural products.

## The Secret of Our Success (2015): The Collective Brain

The book's central thesis is that human dominance derives not from individual raw intelligence but from **cumulative cultural evolution**—the gradual accumulation of know-how, tools, and practices across generations, no single individual of which could reinvent from scratch. Henrich's organizing metaphor is the **"collective brain"**: innovation is a function of the size and interconnectedness of a population and the fidelity of its social learning, not primarily of individual genius. Larger, better-connected groups generate and retain more cultural complexity; isolated or shrinking groups can lose technologies (he cites Tasmania's apparent technological simplification after isolation from mainland Australia).

A recurring argument is that much cultural knowledge is **"causally opaque"**: practices work, and are faithfully copied, even though practitioners do not understand *why* they work. His flagship example is Amazonian manioc (cassava) processing. Bitter manioc contains cyanogenic compounds; the Tukanoan peoples of the Colombian Amazon use a multi-step, multi-day protocol—scraping, grating, washing to separate fiber, starch and liquid, then leaving the products to sit for two days before baking—that detoxifies it. No individual derived this from chemistry; chronic low-dose cyanide poisoning manifests only after years, so trial-and-error learning could not have produced the full protocol. The lesson: tradition encodes adaptive information that exceeds individual comprehension.

Henrich's most cited illustrations are the **lost European explorers**. He documents cases of well-equipped, intelligent European expeditions that starved or died in environments where local foragers thrived, because the Europeans lacked locally accumulated cultural know-how. The Franklin Expedition (1845) perished in the Arctic. The Burke and Wills expedition (1860) died near Cooper Creek in the Australian interior: they were given *nardoo* (an aquatic fern) by local Aboriginal people but processed it incorrectly, and the improperly prepared nardoo—high in the enzyme thiaminase—contributed to their deaths by depleting thiamine. The point is that intelligence without the relevant cultural package is insufficient.

From these foundations Henrich derives further claims: that **prestige-biased and conformist transmission** are evolved learning strategies; that **rituals, taboos, and religions** often persist because they carry adaptive functions (e.g., food taboos protecting pregnant women, divination randomizing hunting to avoid prey depletion); and that **gene-culture coevolution** explains traits like adult lactase persistence (dairying cultures) and reduced gut size enabled by cooking. The book closes by arguing that the same collective-brain dynamics underlie the modern explosion of innovation.

## Prestige vs. Dominance

In a widely cited 2001 paper with Francisco Gil-White ("The Evolution of Prestige," *Evolution and Human Behavior*), Henrich distinguishes **two evolutionarily distinct routes to high status**. **Dominance** is status obtained through force, threat, and intimidation—the ancestral primate route, shared with other apes. **Prestige** is status freely conferred by others on individuals judged skilled or knowledgeable. The theory holds that prestige evolved as a byproduct of social learning: because it pays to learn from the most competent models, learners offer deference (attention, gifts, favors) to gain proximity and learning access to skilled individuals, who in turn tolerate and reward them. Prestige hierarchies are thus a side effect of cultural transmission and are predicted to differ behaviorally from dominance hierarchies (e.g., prestigious individuals attract approach and imitation, dominant ones attract avoidance and fear). This framework has become standard in evolutionary social science, though later empirical work (e.g., Royal Society Open Science, 2019) has found prestige and dominance can both occur in natural groups without tracking task-specific knowledge as cleanly as predicted.

## Cross-Cultural Economic Experiments and the Ultimatum Game

Henrich's empirical reputation was built on importing behavioral-economics experiments into the field. In his dissertation work he ran the **Ultimatum Game** among the Machiguenga of the Peruvian Amazon. In this game a "proposer" splits a sum and a "responder" can accept (both get the split) or reject (both get nothing). Standard game theory predicts minimal offers; Western (e.g., UCLA student) samples typically offer close to 50% and reject low offers. The Machiguenga behaved very differently: mean offers were around 26%, low offers were common, and responders almost always accepted even offers below 20%—they found low offers unremarkable rather than insulting.

This result motivated a large collaborative project, published as *Foundations of Human Sociality* (2004), spanning roughly 15 small-scale societies. The findings: there is **no universal "Homo economicus"** and no single human bargaining norm. Behavior varied widely and tracked features of daily life. At one extreme, the **Lamalera** whale hunters of Indonesia—who depend on large-scale cooperative hunting—made mean offers *above* 50% (hyper-fair, "gift" behavior). At another, groups like the Machiguenga, with little need for cooperation beyond the family, made low offers. The strongest predictors of fairness offers were a society's **degree of market integration** and **payoffs to cooperation**: more market-integrated societies made fairer offers. Henrich reads this as evidence that fairness norms are culturally evolved and context-dependent rather than innate universals.

## WEIRD Psychology

In 2010, with Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan, Henrich published "The Weirdest People in the World?" in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. The paper coined the acronym **WEIRD**—Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic—and made two claims. The first, methodological and widely accepted: behavioral scientists routinely generalize about "human nature" from samples drawn overwhelmingly from WEIRD populations (one frequently cited figure: a large majority of psychology subjects came from such societies, often American undergraduates, a tiny and unrepresentative slice of humanity). The second, stronger and more contested: WEIRD people are not merely one population among many but are **frequent psychological outliers**—unusual across domains including visual perception (e.g., susceptibility to the Müller-Lyer illusion), fairness and cooperation, spatial reasoning, categorization, moral reasoning, analytic vs. holistic thinking, individualism, and self-concept. The paper is among the most-cited in the social sciences and reshaped debates about replicability and sampling.

## The WEIRDest People in the World (2020) and the Church Thesis

Henrich's 2020 book attempts to explain *why* WEIRD psychology arose, and links it to long-run economic and institutional development. The argument: WEIRD psychology (individualism, analytic thinking, guilt over shame, impersonal trust and fairness toward strangers, willingness to follow abstract rules) is historically peculiar and emerged largely as an **unintended byproduct** of the medieval Western Church's family policies.

The proposed mechanism is the **"Marriage and Family Program" (MFP)**. Beginning in late antiquity and intensifying from roughly the time of Pope Gregory I (c. 597) onward, the Western (Catholic) Church progressively banned cousin marriage (extending prohibitions to remote degrees), polygamy, levirate marriage, and adoption, promoted monogamous nuclear families, bilateral (rather than patrilineal) descent, neolocal residence, and individual consent and inheritance. Henrich argues this dissolved Europe's **intensive kin-based institutions**—clans, lineages, cousin-marriage networks—replacing them with relatively independent nuclear families. Loosened kinship, he claims, pushed people into voluntary associations (guilds, monasteries, universities, charter towns) and cultivated the individualistic, impersonally prosocial, rule-following psychology that later underpinned impersonal markets, the rule of law, representative governance, and the Industrial Revolution.

The book's quantitative backbone is the 2019 *Science* paper "The Church, Intensive Kinship, and Global Psychological Variation" with Jonathan Schulz, Duman Bahrami-Rad, and Jonathan Beauchamp. Key constructs and findings: a **Kinship Intensity Index (KII)** built from variables such as cousin-marriage preference, polygamy, clan organization, co-residence, and endogamy; a measure of **duration of medieval Western-Church exposure** (operationalized partly by centuries of exposure and by proximity—e.g., whether a region was within ~50 km of a bishopric); and roughly **24 psychological/behavioral outcomes** (individualism, conformity, impersonal trust/fairness, generosity to strangers, etc.). The headline correlations: longer Church exposure predicts lower kinship intensity; lower kinship intensity predicts more WEIRD psychology. As a vivid illustration, regions and countries with lower cousin-marriage rates show more impersonal prosociality—e.g., Italian provinces with lower cousin-marriage rates have higher voluntary blood donation.

## Religion, Monogamy, and Scale

A connected strand of Henrich's work argues that **religions and norms that increased within-group cooperation were favored by intergroup cultural competition**. "Big gods"—moralizing, punishing, omniscient deities—are argued to expand the circle of cooperation and enable large-scale societies (work he pursued alongside Norenzayan and others). Relatedly, in a 2012 paper he argued that **normative monogamy spread because it reduces the pool of unmarried, low-status men and thereby lowers male-male competition, crime, and within-group conflict**, giving monogamous societies a competitive advantage despite running against the reproductive interests of high-status men. These arguments rely heavily on **cultural group selection**, a mechanism that remains contested.

## Major Criticisms and Counterarguments

**On the WEIRD framework.** The methodological claim is broadly accepted; the stronger claim that WEIRD people are intrinsic "outliers" is contested. Critics argue the apparent outlier status is partly an artifact: because psychology's instruments, constructs, and validation samples were all developed on WEIRD populations, WEIRD subjects will inevitably look like the baseline against which everyone else appears deviant—"weirdness is in the eye of the beholder." Others note the acronym lumps together highly heterogeneous societies.

**On the Church/MFP causal claim.** This is the most heavily attacked part of his work. (1) **Proxy reliability:** critics (e.g., Asier Garcia) argue the "centuries of Church exposure" and "50-km-bishopric" measures are unreliable—for instance, parts of Spain that were continuously Christian for centuries appear with implausibly low exposure values—and that chaining two proxy-mediated regressions cannot establish causation. (2) **Geographic scope:** the analysis arguably excludes or underweights several regions where capitalism actually flourished early (Netherlands, Belgium, parts of Germany, Britain), undercutting claims about capitalism's origins. (3) **Counter-evidence on cousin marriage:** Nicholas Guyatt (*The Guardian*) and others note cousin marriage actually *rose* in parts of 17th–18th-century Europe, and that elites were frequently exempted from the rules via dispensations, complicating a clean dose-response story. (4) **Reverse/alternative causation:** *The Economist* and others ask whether the Church drove social change or merely ratified changes already underway as cities and commerce grew; Pauline Grosjean (UNSW) points to evidence (e.g., sharply falling marriage ages among 19th-century European migrants to resource-rich colonies) that cuts against a pure Church-prohibition explanation. (5) **Internal tension on power:** economist Blair Fix argues Henrich's narrative shifts language from "command" and "ritualized power" when describing kin-based and pre-modern societies to "free exchange" and "competition" when describing capitalism, obscuring that modern corporate hierarchies are *larger* in scale than the hierarchies they supposedly replaced. (6) **Eurocentrism charge:** some reviewers (e.g., Hilton Root, George Mason) note the thesis is itself "WEIRD" in framing and risks treating the Western trajectory as the benchmark of normality and progress.

**On cultural group selection and dual inheritance generally.** The broader framework has long-standing critics. Dan Sperber and Olivier Morin advance an **"epidemiology of representations"/cultural attraction** alternative, arguing that high-fidelity imitation is *not* the main engine of cultural stability—cultural forms persist because they are repeatedly reconstructed toward cognitive "attractors," not faithfully copied—which challenges Henrich's emphasis on copying fidelity. Cultural group selection as a mechanism is disputed by many evolutionary biologists who doubt that selection on groups is strong enough to override within-group selection. Earlier critics of culture-as-evolution include Richard Lewontin, Niles Eldredge, and Stuart Kauffman.

## Internal Tensions in the Work

Several tensions run through the corpus as stated facts about the body of work. First, Henrich simultaneously argues that humans are not especially intelligent as individuals (the collective brain does the work) and that this very capacity for high-fidelity social learning is a species-defining cognitive adaptation—locating extraordinary individual cognitive machinery in the service of an anti-individualist thesis. Second, the "causally opaque tradition is adaptive" theme sits in tension with his celebration of WEIRD analytic, rule-based, innovation-driving cognition: traditions are wise precisely because people do *not* understand or question them, yet modern prosperity is attributed to a psychology that does question and abstract. Third, the cultural-evolution framework treats culture as a quasi-autonomous selection system, while the WEIRD/Church thesis grounds a sweeping cultural-psychological outcome in a specific set of top-down institutional edicts—blending impersonal evolutionary dynamics with a contingent, agentive historical cause. Fourth, his work leans heavily on cultural group selection to explain cooperation, monogamy, and religion, even though that mechanism is among the most contested in his field.
