# William (Will) MacAskill — Reference Dossier

*A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from primary and secondary sources. No interview framing; statements of fact and attributed positions only.*

## Biography

William MacAskill was born **William David Crouch on 24 March 1987 in Glasgow, Scotland**, and grew up there, attending Hutchesons' Grammar School. He reports that at age 15, after learning how many people were dying of AIDS, he resolved to earn money and give much of it away; reading Peter Singer's 1972 essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" around age 18 further shaped his philosophical direction.

He earned a **BA in philosophy at Jesus College, Cambridge (2008)**, a **BPhil at St Edmund Hall, Oxford (2010)**, and a **DPhil at St Anne's College, Oxford (2014)** with a thesis titled "Normative Uncertainty," supervised by **John Broome and Krister Bykvist** (with a year as a visiting student at Princeton). He took a junior research fellowship at Emmanuel College, Cambridge, then was elected to an associate professorship at Oxford (with a fellowship at Lincoln College). At the time of appointment he was reported to be the **youngest associate professor of philosophy in the world**. He chaired the advisory board of the **Global Priorities Institute** at Oxford and directed the **Forethought Foundation for Global Priorities Research**; more recently he is associated with the research organization **Forethought**.

He changed his surname from Crouch to **MacAskill** (his then-wife Amanda Askell's maternal grandmother's maiden name) on marriage; he and Askell later divorced. He is vegetarian on animal-welfare grounds.

**Organizations co-founded.** In **2009** he co-founded **Giving What We Can** with **Toby Ord**, encouraging members to pledge at least **10% of income** to the charities they believe most effective. Inspired by Ord's personal commitment to give away everything he earned above roughly **£20,000/year**, MacAskill made a comparable pledge to cap his own consumption and donate the surplus. In **2011** he co-founded **80,000 Hours** with **Benjamin Todd** (named for the approximate number of hours in a career), providing impact-oriented career advice, and the **Centre for Effective Altruism** as an umbrella body over Giving What We Can and 80,000 Hours. He is widely regarded as one of the originators of the **effective altruism (EA)** movement.

## Major Works

### *Doing Good Better* (2015)

A popular primer on effective altruism, which MacAskill defines as **"the project of using evidence and reason to find out how to do the most good, and on this basis trying to do the most good."** The book organizes giving and career decisions around a set of questions roughly mapped to a **scale / tractability / neglectedness** framework: How many people are affected and how much? What is the probability of success and how good is success? How much does an additional contribution add given what others already do (neglectedness)? What would happen otherwise (counterfactual reasoning)?

Central claims and figures:
- **The ~100x multiplier.** Because of the vast income gap between rich-country residents and the global poor, MacAskill argues a dollar of income (or a donation) generates roughly **100 times** more welfare benefit when directed to the world's poorest than when consumed by a typical wealthy-country resident — a consequence of the diminishing marginal utility of money.
- **QALYs / cost-effectiveness.** He uses metrics such as the **quality-adjusted life year** to compare interventions, arguing that the best charities can be hundreds or thousands of times more cost-effective than typical ones, so charity *selection* dominates the decision to give.
- **The drowning child.** He draws on Singer's thought experiment (a bystander obligated to rescue a drowning child at trivial cost to themselves) to argue for a strong duty to aid distant strangers.
- **Earning to give.** The argument that some people can do more good by taking a high-earning career and donating heavily than by direct charitable work. MacAskill later **publicly de-emphasized** earning to give as a flagship strategy, especially after the FTX collapse.
- **Replaceability.** In assessing a career's impact, one should ask whether one's role would otherwise be filled by someone comparably effective; impact is the counterfactual difference made, not the gross good done.
- Contrarian applied conclusions: e.g., that **fair-trade** purchasing and **boycotting sweatshops** can do little or even harm the global poor.

### *What We Owe the Future* (2022)

A book-length argument for **longtermism**, which MacAskill defines as the view that **positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time.** It rests on three premises: (1) **future people matter morally** as much as present people; (2) the future could contain **vastly more people** than the present; and (3) **our actions today can affect** how their lives go.

- **Scale of the future.** MacAskill emphasizes that if humanity survives anywhere near as long as a typical mammalian species, or until Earth becomes uninhabitable (~1 billion years), future people could number in the range of **10^16 or more** — dwarfing the roughly 100+ billion humans who have ever lived. The companion academic paper offers a concrete lower bound (see below).
- **Trajectory changes vs. catastrophe prevention.** Two routes to improving the future: **averting permanent catastrophes** (ensuring survival) and **changing civilization's trajectory** toward higher-value states.
- **Significance / persistence / contingency.** He proposes assessing the long-run value of an outcome by its **significance** (how much value it adds on average), **persistence** (how long it lasts), and **contingency** (whether it would have happened anyway).
- **Plasticity and value lock-in.** He argues moral and cultural values are **malleable but can become rigid** ("early plasticity, later rigidity"), and warns of **value lock-in** — an event (e.g., the development of artificial general intelligence, or a single dominant ideology) that freezes one value system in place for an extremely long time. He frames AGI as a potential lock-in mechanism.
- **Contingency of values — the abolition of slavery.** He uses the **abolition of chattel slavery** as a case study, arguing it was **not inevitable** but depended on the moral activism and sacrifice of abolitionists; absent that contingency, he argues slavery could have persisted far longer. This supports his broader thesis that present moral effort can durably shape which values win out, and motivates **moral circle expansion**.
- **Existential and catastrophic risk.** He highlights **engineered pandemics** and **misaligned AGI** as leading extinction-level threats, and discusses civilizational collapse scenarios (nuclear winter, extreme climate change, fossil-fuel depletion); he concludes civilization is fairly **resilient and would likely recover** from many collapses, which makes permanent, non-recoverable outcomes the more distinctive concern.
- **Population ethics.** He adopts (with qualifications) a broadly **total-view** stance on which adding happy lives is good, leading to the conclusion that premature human extinction would be an enormous moral loss because of the lost future good lives.

### *Moral Uncertainty* (2020, with Krister Bykvist and Toby Ord)

An academic monograph (Oxford University Press) on how an agent should act when **uncertain about which moral theory is correct**. It develops and defends **maximizing expected choiceworthiness (MEC)** — analogous to expected-utility maximization but over moral theories weighted by one's credence in them. It addresses the central technical obstacle of **intertheoretic value comparisons** (how to compare "how much" one theory cares about an option versus another) and argues against the simpler "**My Favourite Theory**" approach (just act on the single most probable theory). The book grew out of MacAskill's doctoral work on normative uncertainty.

### "The Case for Strong Longtermism" (2019/2021, with Hilary Greaves)

A **Global Priorities Institute working paper** (GPI Working Paper No. 5-2021, June 2021 version) making the rigorous philosophical case for **strong longtermism**. It distinguishes:
- **Axiological strong longtermism (ASL):** in the most important decision situations, the **best** option is mainly determined by its far-future effects.
- **Deontic strong longtermism (DSL):** in those situations, far-future effects are the main determinant of **what we ought to do**.

The core argument rests on the **vastness of expected future population**: e.g., if there is even a **0.1% probability** humanity survives until Earth becomes uninhabitable (~1 billion years) at **at least 10 billion lives per century**, the **expected future population is at least 100 trillion**. The authors argue ASL is **robust** across plausible variations in axiology (risk aversion, priority to the worse off, and population ethics). This paper supplies the formal backbone for the more popular arguments in *What We Owe the Future*.

## Core Ideas in Depth

**The EA framework.** MacAskill frames EA as combining impartial beneficence with the analytic tools of **expected-value reasoning, counterfactual impact, and cause prioritization** along scale/tractability/neglectedness. He stresses **side constraints** in his definition (do the most good "without violating any side-constraints"), distinguishing EA from naive act-utilitarianism.

**Cluelessness and expected value.** Within EA philosophy, MacAskill and collaborators (notably Greaves) take seriously the problem of **cluelessness** — that the long-run, indirect consequences of our actions are largely unknowable. This is both a motivation for and a challenge to longtermism: it pushes toward acting on **expected value** under deep uncertainty while raising the worry that we cannot reliably estimate far-future effects.

**Weak vs. strong longtermism.** *Weak* longtermism holds the long-term future is *one* important consideration; *strong* longtermism holds it is *the* dominant consideration in many key decisions. MacAskill's popular book argues primarily for the weaker, more accessible claim; the Greaves–MacAskill paper defends the strong version.

**Existential risk and AI.** MacAskill treats **reducing existential risk** as a central longtermist priority and increasingly emphasizes **transformative AI / AGI** as the key lever, both as an extinction risk and as a potential **value lock-in** mechanism. His more recent work (e.g., "Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion," 2025, and the **"Better Futures"** essay series via Forethought, 2025) argues that AGI could compress a "century" of change into a decade and that altruists should focus not only on **survival** but on whether the future is **good** ("making good futures even better"), since he argues humanity is plausibly closer to securing survival than to securing flourishing.

**Population ethics and its puzzles.** Longtermism's force depends on population-ethics commitments. The **total view** implies adding happy lives is good but generates the **repugnant conclusion** (a sufficiently large population of barely-worth-living lives can be "better" than a smaller, very happy one). A **person-affecting / neutrality** intuition (that we have no duty to create happy people) would blunt longtermism. The **non-identity problem** notes that our present choices change *which* people exist, complicating claims that future people are "harmed" by our actions. MacAskill engages these directly, defending a position friendly to the total view while acknowledging the counterintuitive results.

**The giving pledge.** Beyond Giving What We Can's 10% pledge, MacAskill committed to a personal cap on his own income/consumption and to donating the surplus to effective causes, framing this as a long-term commitment rather than one-off charity.

## Positions, Counterarguments, and Named Critics

**Longtermism vs. near-term priorities — Peter Singer.** **Peter Singer**, whose work inspired MacAskill, has emphasized **present and near-term** suffering — global poverty and animal welfare — and has voiced reservations about the longtermist turn, worrying it can divert attention and resources from tractable present harms toward speculative far-future benefits. This marks an internal fault line in EA between **near-termist** (global health, animal welfare) and **longtermist** wings.

**The "TESCREAL" critique — Émile P. Torres and Timnit Gebru.** **Émile P. Torres** (philosopher) and **Timnit Gebru** (computer scientist) coined **TESCREAL** (Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, Longtermism), most fully in their 2024 *First Monday* paper "The TESCREAL bundle." They argue these ideologies form an interlinked "bundle" with roots in **20th-century eugenics**, and that longtermism functions as a **quasi-religious worldview** that can justify de-prioritizing present injustices (poverty, climate harms to the global South) in favor of a speculative, technologically maximalist future, with what they characterize as authoritarian and elite-serving implications. Torres specifically argues longtermism can rationalize **dangerous consequentialist** trade-offs against present people.

**Fanaticism and Pascal's mugging.** Critics argue that multiplying tiny probabilities by astronomically large payoffs (e.g., 10^16 future lives) leads to **fanaticism** — being driven by minuscule chances of vast value — analogous to **Pascal's mugging**. Greaves and MacAskill explicitly address this in the strong-longtermism paper, arguing the case does not depend on vanishingly small probabilities, but the objection remains a standing line of philosophical attack.

**Moral mathematics — Kieran Setiya.** Philosopher **Kieran Setiya**, reviewing *What We Owe the Future* in *Boston Review* ("The New Moral Mathematics"), called it intelligent and instructive but argued that "a moral arithmetic is only as good as its axioms." He pressed on **neutrality vs. the repugnant conclusion**, arguing both carry counterintuitive costs and that longtermists must grapple with neutrality as seriously as with repugnance — challenging the total-view foundations on which the numerical case rests.

**Broader philosophical critics.** Critics including **Alice Crary** (who has called longtermism a "toxic ideology") and reviewers in outlets such as the *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* and the *Wall Street Journal* (Barton Swaim) have variously charged longtermism with resting on contestable utilitarian axioms, with offering "highfalutin truisms" dressed as rigor, or with insufficient attention to power and distribution.

**The FTX / Sam Bankman-Fried association.** MacAskill had a years-long association with **Sam Bankman-Fried (SBF)**, the FTX founder, who publicly credited EA/earning-to-give as motivation. MacAskill sat on the team of the **FTX Future Fund**, which in 2022 directed roughly **$160 million** to EA and longtermist causes, including a reported **~$33 million to organizations connected to MacAskill**. He also (in 2022) helped broker an **email introduction between SBF and Elon Musk** regarding a possible Twitter stake. A 2023 *TIME* investigation reported that EA leaders, including MacAskill, had been **warned about SBF's conduct years earlier** — including around a **2018 dispute at Alameda Research** in which complaints about SBF were raised — allegations MacAskill was said at the time to have treated as difficult-to-adjudicate "he said–she said" disputes.

After FTX's **November 2022 collapse and bankruptcy**, MacAskill responded publicly on Twitter/X. He wrote that **if** there had been "deception and misuse of funds," he was "**outraged**," torn between "**utter rage at Sam**" and "**sadness and self-hatred for falling for this deception**." He acknowledged that others "inside and outside the community" had **worried about the misuse of EA ideas** in ways that could cause harm, that he had treated those worries as "speculative and unlikely," and that he **"was probably wrong,"** pledging to reflect on "what should change." He stressed that fraud cannot be justified on EA grounds and that ends do not justify deceptive or harmful means.

## Internal Tensions (as facts)

- **Means vs. ends.** MacAskill's EA pairs welfare-maximizing aims with explicit **side constraints**; critics note that the consequentialist core can be read as licensing the very risk-taking the side constraints are meant to forbid — a tension thrown into relief by the FTX episode.
- **Near-term vs. long-term allocation.** The movement he co-founded began centered on **global poverty and animal welfare** (Singer's priorities); his own work has shifted weight toward **longtermism and AI**, producing an unresolved tension about how to split attention and funding.
- **Cluelessness vs. confident long-run claims.** Longtermism relies on **expected-value estimates of the far future** even as MacAskill grants the deep problem of **cluelessness** about long-run consequences.
- **Expected-value rigor vs. fanaticism.** The same expected-value machinery that grounds his arguments is what critics say exposes them to fanaticism/Pascal's-mugging objections.
- **Earning to give, before and after FTX.** Having popularized **earning to give**, MacAskill subsequently **distanced himself** from it as a headline strategy, and post-FTX has emphasized integrity, governance, and institutional reform.
- **Survival vs. flourishing.** His recent **"Better Futures"** work argues that existing longtermism over-weights *survival* and under-weights making the future *good*, partially revising the emphasis of his own earlier popular framing.

## Recent Direction

Since the FTX collapse MacAskill has stepped back from some governance roles (e.g., trusteeship of the EA umbrella organization) and refocused on **global priorities research, cause prioritization, and the implications of transformative AI** through **Forethought**. His 2025 outputs include "**Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion**" and the "**Better Futures**" essay series, which argue that AGI may dramatically accelerate change and that altruists should prioritize steering toward a *flourishing* long-term future (concepts he labels with terms such as "viatopia"), not merely averting extinction.
