# Bryan Caplan — Reference Dossier

## Biography and Career

Bryan Douglas Caplan was born April 8, 1971, in Northridge, California, and grew up attending public schools in the Los Angeles area. He encountered Ayn Rand's Objectivism in the eleventh grade, then moved through the work of John Hospers and into Austrian economics via Murray Rothbard, an intellectual trajectory he describes in his "Intellectual Autobiography." He earned a B.A. in economics (with a philosophy minor) from the University of California, Berkeley in 1993, writing a senior thesis titled "The Economics of Non-State Legal Systems," and a Ph.D. in economics from Princeton University in 1997, where his advisers included Ben Bernanke.

He has spent his entire academic career at George Mason University, joining as an assistant professor in 1997, becoming associate professor in 2003, and full professor in 2010. He is affiliated with GMU's Center for Study of Public Choice and is a senior research fellow at the Mercatus Center. He blogged for many years at EconLog (the Library of Economics and Liberty's group blog) and now writes at his own Substack, "Bet On It." His named intellectual influences include Ludwig von Mises, James M. Buchanan, the philosopher Michael Huemer, the psychiatrist Thomas Szasz, and the forecasting researcher Philip Tetlock. He identifies as an economic libertarian and an anarcho-capitalist.

## "The Myth of the Rational Voter" and Rational Irrationality

Caplan's first major book, *The Myth of the Rational Voter: Why Democracies Choose Bad Policies* (Princeton University Press, 2007), argues that democracies systematically adopt bad economic policies not because voters are merely ignorant, but because they are *irrational* in a specific, predictable way. His central theoretical contribution is "rational irrationality": because a single vote almost never changes an electoral outcome, the personal cost of holding a comforting-but-false belief at the ballot box is effectively zero. Voters therefore indulge emotionally satisfying beliefs the way they would consume any other cheap good. This contrasts with the older "rational ignorance" framework (Anthony Downs), which holds only that voters do not bother to inform themselves; Caplan's claim is the stronger one that voters actively prefer error.

The empirical backbone is the Survey of Americans and Economists on the Economy (SAEE), which let Caplan compare what the general public believes about the economy with what professional economists believe, controlling for income, job security, ideology, and other factors. He identifies four *systematic* biases — systematic meaning the errors do not cancel out across voters but push in a consistent direction:

- **Anti-market bias**: a tendency to underestimate the benefits of the price system and market mechanisms (e.g., suspicion of profit, of "middlemen," and of price increases).
- **Anti-foreign bias**: a tendency to underestimate the economic benefits of dealing with foreigners, manifesting as protectionism and hostility to trade and immigration.
- **Make-work bias**: a tendency to undervalue the conservation of labor — treating jobs rather than production as the goal, and viewing labor-saving change as a threat.
- **Pessimistic bias**: a tendency to overestimate the severity of economic problems and to underestimate the past, present, and future performance of the economy.

Caplan's policy conclusion is skeptical of democracy as an aggregator of preferences and friendly to removing decisions from majoritarian control (favoring markets, and giving more weight to expert economic consensus). The strongest counterargument, raised by critics including Austrian-tradition reviewers at the Mises Institute and by public-choice colleagues, is that economists themselves may be the biased party — that the "economist benchmark" Caplan uses to define voter error simply encodes economists' own ideological priors, so the gap between public and expert opinion is not proof of public irrationality. A second tension: Caplan's prescription (less democracy, more deference to experts) sits uneasily with his broader libertarian distrust of concentrated authority.

## "The Case Against Education" and the Signaling Model

*The Case Against Education: Why the Education System Is a Waste of Time and Money* (Princeton University Press, 2018) advances Caplan's most quantified claim: that roughly **80 percent** of the private financial return to education is *signaling* rather than human-capital formation. On this view, school does not mainly make students more productive; it certifies pre-existing traits employers want — intelligence, conscientiousness, and conformity. Caplan summarizes the human-capital-to-signaling split as roughly **20/80**.

His evidence includes:

- **Sheepskin effects**: the disproportionate income gain attached to *completing* a credential (the final diploma year) versus completing intermediate years, which Caplan treats as his strongest single piece of evidence for signaling — if employers paid for skills accumulated year by year, the final year should not carry an outsized premium.
- The gap between the high *private* return to schooling and the much lower estimated *social/national* return: studies that try to link a country's education to its growth find far weaker effects than individual wage regressions imply.
- **Forgetting**: students rapidly lose much of the specific content taught, and most adults retain little of subjects like advanced math, foreign languages, or history.
- The mismatch between curricula and job tasks: most students study material with no direct vocational use.

Caplan's policy recommendations follow from this: cut government subsidies to education ("educational austerity"), and expand vocational education and apprenticeship as more honest skill-building paths. He acknowledges the signaling model implies a collective-action problem — for the individual, more credentials still pay, even though society would be better off with less credentialism (the "arms race" or "rat race" dynamic).

His principal named opponent is education economist **Eric Hanushek**, with whom he debated publicly (Caplan's "Is Education Worth It?" opening statement, 2018). Hanushek's position is that the *quality* of education — measured by gains in cognitive skills, not years of schooling — is a genuine and large driver of individual earnings and national economic growth, implying a much larger human-capital share than Caplan's 20 percent. Critics also argue Caplan's 80 percent figure is inflated by his interpretive choices and that learning may confer general cognitive and behavioral gains he discounts. An internal tension: Caplan attacks education as wasteful signaling while remaining a tenured professor whose own livelihood depends on the credentialing system, a point he openly concedes.

## Open Borders

*Open Borders: The Science and Ethics of Immigration* (First Second, 2019), a nonfiction graphic novel illustrated by Zach Weinersmith, makes Caplan's case for radically liberalized immigration. The economic core is the "trillion-dollar bills on the sidewalk" argument (a phrase associated with economist Michael Clemens): because workers are dramatically more productive in rich, well-institutioned countries than in poor ones, letting people move would unlock enormous gains. Caplan's headline claim is that open borders would roughly **double world GDP**; he cites a literature range of gains from roughly **+67% to +147%**, implying that migration restrictions hold global output near half of its free-migration potential.

To address objections, Caplan emphasizes **keyhole solutions** — narrowly targeted policies that neutralize specific harms while still permitting migration, rather than blanket exclusion. Examples include admitting immigrants on terms that bar them from welfare for a period, charging surtaxes or admission fees, or imposing waiting periods for citizenship and voting. The logic: if the worry is fiscal burden or political effects, restrict *those* rather than restrict *entry*.

His most prominent economic opponent is Harvard's **George Borjas**, a leading immigration restrictionist. Borjas grants that open borders could yield large global gains (he has discussed figures on the order of $40 trillion under high wage-ratio assumptions, ~60% of world GDP) but argues two things eliminate them: enormous migration costs (he cites internal-U.S. relocation cost estimates of roughly $312,000 per person) and the risk that mass migration degrades the very institutions that make rich countries productive. In his EconLog response ("Trillion Dollar Bills on the Sidewalk: The Borjas Critique," July 16, 2014), Caplan rebuts that using rich-country relocation costs to estimate what desperate third-world migrants would pay is "ludicrous," that marginal migrants have far lower reservation prices (some would accept wage cuts to escape danger), and that diaspora networks make psychic costs fall as migration scales. The unresolved tension Borjas presses is institutional: Caplan's optimism requires that the host country's institutions survive large inflows, which his quantitative argument largely assumes rather than demonstrates.

## Pro-Natalism and "Selfish Reasons to Have More Kids"

*Selfish Reasons to Have More Kids: Why Being a Great Parent Is Less Work and More Fun Than You Think* (Basic Books, 2011) argues that prospective parents should have *more* children than they plan to, and can do so at lower cost than they fear. The empirical foundation is behavioral genetics — specifically twin and adoption studies, including comparisons of identical twins reared apart and of adoptees versus biological children. Caplan's reading of this literature is that for adult outcomes such as income, educational attainment, health, longevity, intelligence, happiness, and values, *nature* dominates *nurture*: parenting has small long-run effects on how children "turn out" as adults (at least within the range of normal middle- and upper-class American parenting).

The practical conclusion is that intensive, high-effort "concerted cultivation" parenting buys little durable benefit, so parents should relax, parent less anxiously, and enjoy the experience more — which lowers the perceived cost of each additional child and justifies having more. Caplan also frames children as a long-run investment in one's own future happiness (grandchildren, a larger family network) and frames falling birth rates as a social problem. A standard counterargument is that behavioral-genetic estimates are drawn largely from a relatively homogeneous range of Western environments and may understate parental influence outside that range, and that parents value the *childhood experience itself*, not only adult outcomes. A tension within Caplan's framework: he urges more children partly on pro-social and pro-civilizational grounds while titling the book around *selfish* reasons.

## "Build, Baby, Build" and Housing Deregulation

*Build, Baby, Build: The Science and Ethics of Housing Regulation* (Cato Institute, 2024), again illustrated as a graphic nonfiction work (with Ady Branzei), applies Caplan's method to housing. The thesis is that government regulation — zoning, height and density limits, minimum lot sizes, permitting delays, environmental review, and related rules — "manufactures scarcity," and that sweeping deregulation is the cure. Caplan aligns the book with the YIMBY ("Yes In My Backyard") movement.

On magnitudes, Caplan presents a range. He cites work (associated with Wharton's Joseph Gyourko and collaborators) suggesting deregulation could plausibly cut housing prices by roughly **50 percent** in the most constrained markets, while also discussing more conservative estimates — on the order of an **11 percent** reduction in average U.S. house prices with corresponding living-standard gains — depending on assumptions and geography. Beyond affordability, he argues deregulation would raise growth (by letting workers move to high-productivity cities), increase fertility (cheaper housing makes family formation easier, linking the book to his natalism), and reduce homelessness. The book also makes an explicitly *ethical* case that restricting what owners may build on their own land is a rights violation, not merely an inefficiency. Counterarguments from critics include concerns about local externalities, infrastructure strain, and whether the largest price-reduction estimates hold once general-equilibrium and amenity effects are included.

## Betting Record

Caplan treats public betting as a discipline device — a way to force pundits to "put up or shut up" and to demonstrate calibrated judgment. He maintains a public "Complete Bet Wiki" listing every wager. His central boast is an *unbroken winning record* on resolved bets. The wiki's reported tally grew over time: roughly **20-0** as of 2020, and he has publicly stated a record on the order of **23-0** (23 wins, 0 losses) on resolved bets in the 2021–2022 period, declaring himself "still undefeated." Bets have spanned many years and were still accumulating, with several unresolved at any given time.

Documented wins include a bet that no country would leave the European Union by the end of 2019 (resolved in his favor January 1, 2020, despite the Brexit referendum, because the UK's formal exit had not completed by the deadline), and a series of correctly-called recession, unemployment, and election predictions (he has written posts such as "I Win All My Election Bets"). He has publicly noted at least one wager he expected to *lose* — a long-dated climate-related bet — which he treats as evidence that his record reflects selective, well-priced bets rather than universal infallibility. Caplan's own gloss is that the skill lies less in raw prediction than in choosing which bets to make: declining bad terms and accepting only wagers where he judges the odds strongly in his favor.

## Labor Economics and Disagreements with the Mainstream

*Labor Econ Versus the World: Essays on the World's Greatest Market* (2022) collects roughly fifteen years of Caplan's EconLog posts on labor markets (edited by Jack Pfefferkorn). Its through-line is that "pro-worker" interventions usually backfire and that workers' high living standards come from high productivity plus competition among employers, not from protective legislation. Specific positions:

- **Minimum wage**: opposes it, arguing it causes unemployment and other harms to the least-skilled workers it purports to help.
- **Immigration restriction**: argues it does not protect domestic workers from poverty and is globally destructive (consistent with *Open Borders*).
- **Education subsidies**: opposes them on signaling grounds (consistent with *The Case Against Education*).
- **Keynesianism and "make-work"**: skeptical of demand-side job-creation rationales.

Tyler Cowen, a GMU colleague and frequent interlocutor, registered his disagreement, arguing that much labor-market inequity originates "upstream" of labor markets in culture rather than in market or policy distortions — a critique Caplan engages directly. Caplan's general methodological stance is that mainstream labor economists soft-pedal market-friendly findings to remain socially respectable, a charge that itself draws the counter that Caplan's certainty outruns the empirical literature on contested questions like minimum-wage employment effects.

## Methodological and Philosophical Views

Caplan's normative framework is what he calls **common-sense ethics** combined with **mild deontology**, drawing heavily on philosopher Michael Huemer's ethical intuitionism: ordinary moral intuitions (it is wrong to kill, steal, or coerce) should be taken as strong default evidence, and grand theories that overturn them face a high burden of proof. He applies this to politics in *How Evil Are Politicians?: Essays on Demagoguery* (2022), arguing that politicians' conduct, judged by everyday moral standards, is genuinely blameworthy rather than merely "naive."

The same intuitionist method grounds his **pacifism**. In "The Common-Sense Case for Pacifism" (2010), he argues that (1) the immediate costs of war are clearly horrific, (2) the long-run benefits are highly uncertain, and (3) under mild deontology a war is justified only if its expected long-run good *substantially* exceeds its near-certain harms — he proposes a threshold analogous to refusing to murder one innocent person to save five, requiring a lives-saved-to-lives-lost ratio well above 5:1. Because that bar is rarely met with confidence beforehand, he concludes most wars are unjustified.

His **anarcho-capitalism** holds that even core state functions (law, defense, courts) could in principle be provided by competitive private institutions; his "Anarchist Theory FAQ" argues that anarcho-capitalists have a stronger claim to the historical anarchist tradition than left-anarchists. He also identifies as a strong proponent of free will/rationality in some domains while building his most famous book (*Myth of the Rational Voter*) on systematic human irrationality — a juxtaposition critics note: Caplan argues voters are predictably irrational yet maintains an optimistic confidence in markets and in his own contrarian judgment. He has separately written *Don't Be a Feminist: Essays on Genuine Justice* (2022), arguing against what he defines as feminism (the view that society treats women less fairly than men) on empirical grounds, a stance that placed him among his most controversial public positions.
