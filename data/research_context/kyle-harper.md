# Kyle Harper — Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from Kyle Harper's published work and the surrounding scholarly record. Harper is a historian of the ancient world at the University of Oklahoma, best known for fusing the methods of classical history with paleoclimatology and genomics.

## Biography and Career

Kyle Harper was born December 29, 1979, in Norman, Oklahoma. He earned a bachelor's degree in letters from the University of Oklahoma (OU) in 2001, where in his final year he was named one of Oklahoma's two finalists for a Rhodes Scholarship. He then went to Harvard University for graduate study, completing an MA in 2003 and a PhD in 2007. He returned to OU as an assistant professor immediately upon finishing his doctorate.

In 2009 he was named founding director of OU's Institute for the American Constitutional Heritage. He was promoted to full professor of classics and letters in 2014, the same year he became provost of the Norman campus, a role he held from 2014 to 2020. He stepped down from the provostship in mid-2020 to return to teaching and research; local press coverage at the time described his tenure as "long-embattled," reflecting controversy over budget and administrative matters during his term, but the move returned him to a full faculty role. He holds the G.T. and Libby Blankenship Chair in the History of Liberty, and his current titles include Professor of Classics and Letters, Senior Advisor to the President, and Provost Emeritus.

Harper's honors include a Guggenheim Fellowship and an Andrew Carnegie Fellowship; he has been a Junior Fellow at Dumbarton Oaks and a Visiting Scholar at Stanford. He has published four scholarly monographs across two distinct phases of his career: first as a social and cultural historian of late Roman slavery and sexuality, then as an environmental and biological historian of disease and climate. His later books appear in two prestigious Princeton series — The Princeton History of the Ancient World (*The Fate of Rome*) and The Princeton Economic History of the Western World (*Plagues upon the Earth*).

## The Four Books at a Glance

- **Slavery in the Late Roman World, AD 275–425** (Cambridge University Press, 2011) — a 600-page monograph on the persistence of mass slavery in late antiquity.
- **From Shame to Sin: The Christian Transformation of Sexual Morality in Late Antiquity** (Harvard University Press, 2013) — on how Christianity reframed ancient sexual ethics.
- **The Fate of Rome: Climate, Disease, and the End of an Empire** (Princeton University Press, 2017) — his breakout interdisciplinary account of Rome's decline.
- **Plagues upon the Earth: Disease and the Course of Human History** (Princeton University Press, 2021) — a deep-time global history of infectious disease.

## "Slavery in the Late Roman World" (2011)

Harper's first book argues that the late Roman Empire — through the "long fourth century," roughly Diocletian to the end of Honorius's reign — remained a genuine slave society, not a world already sliding toward medieval serfdom. He estimates the slave population at roughly five million out of a total imperial population of about fifty million, i.e., on the order of ten percent, concentrated in the hands of a rising aristocracy and propertied "bourgeoisie." A central and contested claim is that medieval serfdom did not evolve out of late Roman agricultural slavery but emerged as a separate later innovation, so that a deep institutional divide runs through late antiquity separating the Roman slave system from its early-medieval successors. The book was widely reviewed as a major contribution to Roman social and economic history, and its emphasis on quantification and tabulated data (it includes twenty tables) anticipated the data-heavy method of his later work.

## "From Shame to Sin" (2013)

Harper's second book traces what he calls a revolution in sexual morality accompanying the rise of Christianity. His thesis is that ancient sexuality shifted from a socially policed culture of "shame" — in which honor, gender, and the slave/free distinction governed acceptable sexual behavior — to a theological culture of "sin," organized around individual free will, the soul, and cosmic order. In the early Roman Empire, sexual norms protected social hierarchy: the bodies of the enslaved and the low-status were sexually available, and morality tracked status. Beginning in the fourth century, Harper argues, sexuality was detached from the social realm and reframed as a matter of the individual will and its shadow, sin. He identifies two phenomena as especially symptomatic of the change: the blanket proscription of all same-sex love and the flat condemnation of prostitution, both of which cut against the grain of classical Roman practice. The book won the American Philological Association's Goodwin Award of Merit and is treated as a landmark in the history of sexuality.

## "The Fate of Rome" (2017)

### Central Thesis

*The Fate of Rome* argues that the decline and fall of the Roman Empire was driven not only by emperors, armies, and barbarians but by "volcanic eruptions, solar cycles, climate instability, and devastating viruses and bacteria." Harper's compressed formulation — quoted by reviewers — is that "germs are far deadlier than Germans": disease and climate, more than barbarian invasion, drove the catastrophic depopulation behind imperial collapse. The book spans roughly 500 years, from Rome's second-century peak to about 650 CE, when the empire was reduced to a Byzantine rump.

### The Climate Framework

Harper periodizes the climate of the Roman era into three phases, reconstructed from paleoclimate proxies (ice cores, tree rings, cave deposits, and lake sediments):

1. **The Roman Climate Optimum (RCO)**, roughly 200 BC to AD 150 — a warm, wet, stable regime that, in his account, underwrote agricultural surplus, population growth, and imperial expansion.
2. **The Roman Transitional Period** (or Late Roman Transition), roughly AD 150–450 — climatic destabilization coinciding with the third-century crisis.
3. **The Late Antique Little Ice Age (LALIA)**, roughly AD 450–700, with the sharpest cooling 536–660 CE — triggered by a cluster of major volcanic eruptions, most dramatically the **536 CE event**, which Harper describes as producing a "volcanic winter."

He frames Rome's expansion as enabled by a "great conveyor belt of connectivity" — roads, harbors, dense cities — that supported a population he places near 75 million at its height.

### The Pandemics

Harper places three "pandemic" episodes at the center of the story, arguing that the same connectivity and urbanization that made Rome rich also made it a superb amplifier of disease:

- **The Antonine Plague (c. 165–180 AD)** — which he, following Galen's description of fever, diarrhea, and a black pustular rash, treats as most likely smallpox. Harper's estimate is on the order of ten percent empire-wide mortality, in the range of roughly six to seven million deaths (estimates in the broader literature run from about 1 million up to 7–10 million).
- **The Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–262 AD)** — which he associates with drought and possibly a viral hemorrhagic fever, occurring amid the third-century crisis.
- **The Justinianic Plague (beginning 541–543 AD)** — caused by *Yersinia pestis*, the same bacterium behind the later Black Death. Harper treats this as the "first pandemic" of bubonic plague, recurring in waves for roughly two centuries and, in his strongest claim, carrying off something like half the population. He writes that "everything about our knowledge of the Justinianic Plague is consistent with the conclusion that the mortality also carried off an unfathomable half of the population."

A key methodological resource for the book is the maturation of ancient-DNA (aDNA) science: paleogenetic studies that recovered *Y. pestis* genomes from sixth-century skeletal remains gave Harper genomic confirmation that the Justinianic Plague was indeed bubonic plague, settling a long-running etiological dispute. Harper has noted that historians have become "unintentional beneficiaries" of contemporary climate science, since the same proxy archives that document modern warming also illuminate the ancient climate.

## "Plagues upon the Earth" (2021)

### Central Thesis

*Plagues upon the Earth* widens the lens from Rome to all of human history. Its core claim is reciprocal: "human history shapes disease ecology and pathogen evolution; disease ecology and pathogen evolution in turn shape the course of human history." Or, in Harper's compressed phrasing, "our germs are a product of our history, and our history has been decisively patterned by the battle with infectious disease." He stresses that before about 1900, even in the most developed societies, most people died of infectious disease — making the history of pathogens inseparable from demography, ecology, and economy.

### Pathogen Evolution and the Human Disease Pool

Harper argues that humanity's uniquely dangerous pool of pathogens is rooted in our evolutionary deep past and has been amplified by the very things that made us successful: enormous, sedentary populations; cities; sustained contact with domesticated animals; and trade networks linking distant populations. These conditions, he argues, selected for "highly specialized" and "really nasty" human-adapted microparasites. The framework explicitly integrates biology with social science, tracing disease through the entwined histories of agriculture, slavery, colonialism, and capitalism.

### The "Great Escape" and Its Paradox

Harper's narrative culminates in humanity's "great escape" from infectious disease — the roughly modern collapse in mortality that made contemporary life possible. He emphasizes a paradox several reviewers highlight: the public-health revolution that drove the escape was in significant part built on the *miasma* (sanitarian, anti-"filth") paradigm rather than a clean application of germ theory, and the same technological and economic progress that suppressed old diseases has "actually accelerated the emergence of new, dangerous diseases — cholera, influenza, polio, AIDS, COVID-19." Writing in the shadow of COVID-19, Harper presents that pandemic as broadly predictable rather than anomalous, arguing that "a lot of pressure has been building" and that a novel respiratory virus emergence was "really likely." The book thus resists triumphalism: the escape is real but precarious and self-undermining.

## The Scholarly Controversies

### Environmental Determinism (Haldon and colleagues)

The sharpest sustained critique of *The Fate of Rome* is a three-part response published in *History Compass* in 2018 by **John Haldon, Hugh Elton, Sabine Huebner, Adam Izdebski, Lee Mordechai, and Timothy Newfield**, titled "Plagues, climate change, and the end of an empire: A response to Kyle Harper's *The Fate of Rome*" (Part 1: Climate; Part 2: Plagues and a crisis of empire; Part 3: Disease, agency, and collapse). Their core charge is that Harper's account is **environmentally deterministic**: the environment "appears everywhere" while human agency — society, culture, institutions, choices — is "mostly absent." They argue that he constructs a persuasive narrative on rhetorical flourish more than evidence, that he overreads contested proxy data, and that internal contradictions and misinterpretations of evidence run through the book.

A related, more technical line of criticism — voiced by Newfield in the *Bryn Mawr Classical Review* and by the Haldon group — is that paleoclimate proxies are too sparse and too geographically local for antiquity to support confident "global" or empire-wide climate generalizations; that Harper tends to present supporting scholarship in the body while relegating contradictory findings to footnotes; and that he sometimes adopts the weakest available scholarly proposals when they suit the thesis. The LALIA, critics note, is portrayed as uniformly catastrophic despite proxy evidence of warm European winters in the relevant period.

### The Justinianic Plague: Maximalists vs. Minimalists

Harper's near-half-the-population mortality claim for the Justinianic Plague places him firmly in the **"maximalist"** camp. The leading **"minimalist"** challenge comes from **Lee Mordechai and Merle Eisenberg**, whose 2019 work — including the *PNAS* article "The Justinianic Plague: an inconsequential pandemic?" (Mordechai, Eisenberg, et al.) and the *Past & Present* article "Rejecting Catastrophe: The Case of the Justinianic Plague" — argues that the surviving evidence does not support catastrophic, empire-wide mortality. Examining datasets such as legislation, coinage, papyri, inscriptions, building activity, and pollen records, they find that none shows a clear, independent signal tracking plague outbreaks, and they contend the plague should be regarded as comparatively "inconsequential" because it did not produce the collapse of states or empires. The maximalist position they oppose holds that the plague killed between a quarter and half of the Eastern Roman population — figures sometimes rendered as 15 to 100 million deaths across its recurrences.

The debate intersects with the interpretation of aDNA evidence. The recovery of *Y. pestis* genomes confirms what organism caused the disease, but minimalists argue that genomic confirmation of the pathogen says little about its demographic scale — presence is not prevalence — whereas maximalists, including Harper, read the genomic, textual, and climatic evidence together as mutually reinforcing. This is the central evidentiary fault line: the same molecular data is taken by one camp as corroboration of a civilization-altering pandemic and by the other as compatible with a serious but geographically uneven and demographically limited event.

### Reception of "Plagues upon the Earth"

The disease-history synthesis was broadly praised for ambition and scope but drew the predictable cautions about a single-factor "big history" frame. Reviews such as the one in the *Los Angeles Review of Books* ("Perfecting Diseases' Pasts") engaged its grand framework critically, and reviewers in public-health and history venues questioned whether so sweeping a thesis can avoid flattening regional and contingent variation — a continuation of the agency-versus-environment tension raised against *The Fate of Rome*.

## Internal Tensions in the Work (Stated as Facts)

- **Multicausality in principle, mono-causal emphasis in practice.** Harper repeatedly affirms that Rome's fall and disease history are multicausal, yet the architecture and rhetoric of both *The Fate of Rome* and *Plagues upon the Earth* foreground climate and pathogens as prime movers, which is the basis for the "determinism" critique.
- **Reliance on contested proxy and genomic data.** Harper's climate periodization rests on paleoclimate proxies whose density and geographic representativeness for antiquity are disputed, and his plague mortality figures rest on aDNA that confirms pathogen identity but not demographic scale. His confident high-mortality estimates therefore depend on interpretive leaps beyond what the proxies alone establish.
- **Connectivity as both cause of greatness and cause of ruin.** The same Roman "conveyor belt of connectivity" that Harper credits for prosperity is the mechanism he credits for catastrophic disease spread — a deliberate dialectic, but one that makes the model's predictions sensitive to how he weights integration's benefits against its biological costs.
- **The "great escape" as triumph and trap.** In *Plagues upon the Earth*, modern progress both defeats old pathogens and breeds new ones, so Harper's narrative of human victory over disease is simultaneously a narrative of mounting, self-generated risk — a tension he embraces rather than resolves.
- **Two scholarly identities.** Harper's early books (slavery, sexuality) are archive-driven social and cultural history; his later books are data-driven natural-science-inflected history. The shift in method and evidence base is a notable discontinuity in a single body of work.
