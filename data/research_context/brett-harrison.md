# Brett Harrison — Reference Dossier

## Short Bio

Brett Harrison is an American software engineer and financial-technology executive, born May 11, 1988, in New York City. He is the founder and CEO of Architect Financial Technologies, a brokerage and trading-infrastructure firm he started in January 2023. He earned a B.A. and M.S. in computer science (with a focus on artificial intelligence) from Harvard University, where he completed the famously demanding Math 55 sequence; he previously attended the Research Science Institute at MIT and graduated from Half Hollow Hills High School West in 2006. Harrison spent roughly seven to eight years at the quantitative trading firm Jane Street Capital, a stint at Headlands Technologies, and nearly two years at Citadel Securities before serving as the first president of FTX US from May 2021 until his resignation on September 27, 2022 — roughly six weeks before FTX's broader collapse in November 2022. He was never charged with any wrongdoing in connection with FTX. He is married to Hannah Harrison (2017) and lives in the Chicago area.

A factual note on the prompt's framing: the 2024 book titled *Easy Money: Cryptocurrency, Casino Capitalism, and the Golden Age of Fraud* was written by actor Ben McKenzie and journalist Jacob Silverman, not by Brett Harrison. As of the public record reflected here, Harrison's principal first-person account of FTX is not a published memoir but his lengthy January 2023 thread on X and the interviews, public statements, and op-eds he has given since. This dossier treats those primary self-accounts as the equivalent body of material and is explicit about provenance throughout.

## Career Path: Jane Street, Headlands, Citadel Securities

Harrison joined Jane Street Capital in 2010, beginning as a trader on the American depositary receipts (ADR) desk before moving into engineering leadership, where he led the development of algorithmic trading systems across the firm. It was at Jane Street that Harrison first worked alongside Sam Bankman-Fried (SBF), who was a junior trader there; this prior professional relationship is what later brought Harrison to FTX. After Jane Street, Harrison was a senior software developer at Headlands Technologies, a quantitative trading firm.

He then moved to Citadel Securities, the market-making arm of Ken Griffin's franchise, where over roughly two years he rose from head of ETF technology to head of semi-systematic trading technology. In that role he oversaw a team of approximately 100 engineers responsible for trading platforms spanning global options, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), over-the-counter (OTC) instruments, and ADRs. This sequence — Jane Street to Headlands to Citadel Securities — gave Harrison a background squarely in traditional, highly regulated, institutional electronic market-making and trading infrastructure, a contrast he has repeatedly drawn against the looser operating culture he later described at FTX.

## FTX US: Role and Tenure

Harrison became the first president of FTX US in May 2021, recruited by SBF. His public characterization of his mandate was to build out and professionalize the U.S.-regulated business of the crypto exchange: he established regulated business operations, opened a Chicago headquarters, and in 2022 oversaw the launch of FTX's U.S. retail stock-brokerage and trading platform (FTX Stocks). He was, by his own account and that of contemporaneous reporting, instrumental in building the U.S. operation and in staffing it with experienced professionals drawn from U.S. finance firms, law firms, and regulated exchanges.

A defining incident of his tenure was the FDIC episode of August 2022. On July 20, 2022, Harrison posted on the account "@Brett_FTX" that "direct deposits from employers to FTX US are stored in individually FDIC-insured bank accounts in the users' names" and that "stocks are held in FDIC-insured and SIPC-insured brokerage accounts." On August 18-19, 2022, the FDIC issued cease-and-desist letters to FTX US and four other crypto firms, alleging false or misleading representations about deposit insurance in violation of section 18(a)(4) of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act. Harrison deleted the tweet and stated that he had not meant to suggest that crypto assets were FDIC-insured, only that USD deposits from employers were held at insured banks: "We really didn't mean to mislead anyone, and we didn't suggest that FTX US itself, or that crypto/non-fiat assets, benefit from FDIC insurance."

## His Account of FTX, Sam Bankman-Fried, and Why He Resigned

Harrison's most detailed first-person account came in a 49-post thread on X published January 14-15, 2023 (the thread begins "1/49 Many have asked questions about my time at FTX US and why I left when I did"). The core of his narrative is that his relationship with SBF soured roughly six months into his tenure, when he began "advocating strongly for establishing separation and independence for the executive, legal, and developer teams of FTX US" from FTX International — and SBF disagreed. Harrison wrote that he saw in that early conflict SBF's "total insecurity and intransigence when his decisions were questioned, his spitefulness, and the volatility of his temperament." He accused SBF of "gaslighting and manipulation."

Harrison's specific factual claims include:

- In April 2022 — about 11 months into the role — he filed "a written formal complaint about what I saw to be the largest organizational problems inhibiting FTX's future success," and said he would resign if the problems were not addressed. He framed the problems as the lack of appropriate delegation of authority, the absence of a formal management structure, and missing key hires.
- He escalated concerns to SBF and to Nishad Singh (FTX's director of engineering). According to the FTX bankruptcy debtors' later reporting, after he raised these issues his bonus was "drastically reduced."
- Company lawyers instructed him to apologize to SBF; he refused. He says he was threatened on SBF's behalf with being fired and told that "Sam would destroy my professional reputation" if he did not retract the complaint and deliver an apology.
- He described managerial responsibility and financial controls being concentrated among Bahamas-based executives at FTX.com and Alameda, and said the expertise of FTX US's professional hires was rendered "irrelevant and valueless."

Harrison announced his resignation on September 27, 2022, via X, framing it at the time as a planned transition into an advisory role: he wrote that "over the next few months I'll be transferring my responsibilities and moving into an advisory role at the company" and expressed "deep gratitude for my experiences at FTX in the last year and a half." The benign public framing of September 2022 stands in tension with the far harsher account he gave in January 2023; he has explained the gap as a function of separation agreements, legal caution, and the fact that the fraud had not yet been exposed when he left.

## The Post-Collapse Disclaimer and the Knowledge Dispute

After FTX collapsed in November 2022, Harrison's central public claim — repeated in his January 2023 thread and subsequent interviews — was that he and other FTX US executives were not part of, and had no knowledge of, the criminal scheme. His formulation: "It's clear from what has been made public that the scheme was held closely by Sam and his inner circle at FTX.com and Alameda, which I was not a part of, nor were other executives at FTX US. I understand now why they carefully concealed their criminal activity from us. We have extensive professional networks, our own lines of communication with US regulators and our own authority to speak to US media. If any one of us had suspected let alone learned the truth, we would have reported them immediately." He has emphasized that he was "isolated from decision-making" by SBF and was kept away from FTX.com and Alameda's finances.

The "inner circle" he names as the locus of the fraud comprises SBF, Caroline Ellison (Alameda CEO), Nishad Singh, and Gary Wang — the figures who later pleaded guilty (Ellison, Singh, Wang) or were convicted (SBF, on seven counts in November 2023). Supporting context Harrison and others have cited from the FTX debtors' reports includes: a June 2022 episode in which employees fabricated an Alameda portfolio summary after being told to "come up with some numbers? Idk"; SBF's own admission that "Alameda is unauditable... We sometimes find $50m of assets lying around"; the summary firing of a legal-department employee who raised concerns about Alameda's lack of corporate controls; private keys and seed phrases stored in plaintext; and a roughly $432 million hack at the time of bankruptcy.

The strongest counterargument to Harrison's "I didn't know" position has been advanced by critics who argue that senior FTX US executives should have known more or asked harder questions given the visible red flags. Skeptics — including commentators in crypto media and some legal observers — have pointed to the FDIC insurance misstatement, the commingled corporate structure, the absence of an independent board, and SBF's well-documented erratic management as warning signs that an experienced markets executive arguably should not have tolerated for seventeen months. Harrison's own thread is, in this reading, both an exoneration and an indictment: by his account he raised serious governance alarms internally as early as April 2022, which his critics note also means he continued to serve as the public face of FTX US for months after concluding the company was badly governed and after being threatened by its leadership.

## Internal Tensions in His Account (Stated as Facts)

- Tension between raising red flags and remaining in post: Harrison claims he formally threatened to resign in April 2022 over "organizational problems" and concluded SBF was insecure, intransigent, and spiteful — yet he stayed as president until late September 2022 and continued to recruit professionals into the firm during that period.
- Tension between his governance complaints and his stated ignorance of fraud: Harrison's narrative simultaneously holds that he identified deep structural defects (no delegation, no formal management, controls concentrated in the Bahamas, expertise rendered "valueless") and that he had no inkling of the underlying fraud. The governance failures he describes are the same structural conditions — commingling, opacity, concentration of control at FTX.com/Alameda — that enabled the fraud.
- Tension between the September 2022 and January 2023 framings: his resignation tweet expressed "deep gratitude" and described an orderly transition into an advisory role; four months later he described threats, gaslighting, a reduced bonus, and a coerced apology demand. He attributes the shift to legal constraints lifting after the collapse.
- Tension between profiting from crypto and condemning its conduct: Harrison has been sharply critical of the fraud and "casino" dynamics surrounding crypto while simultaneously building a new venture, Architect, that markets crypto-derived market structure (perpetual futures) to institutions — a juxtaposition critics have flagged as he raised capital from crypto-aligned investors including Coinbase Ventures and Circle Ventures.
- Tension over the FDIC statement: he characterized the insurance tweet as an innocent miscommunication, while the FDIC formally alleged false or misleading representations about deposit insurance — a regulated-markets compliance lapse for an executive whose stated value-add was traditional-finance rigor.

## Views on Crypto, Market Structure, Trading Technology, and Regulation

Harrison's public posture on crypto has been described as "glass half full," even after the FTX collapse. His recurring arguments:

- Regulation as an accelerant, not an obstacle: he argues that greater SEC and CFTC oversight paradoxically increases institutional appetite — "as we've seen when thinking about how the SEC wants to regulate crypto more, that's actually making institutions more excited about participating," because it addresses concerns about market health and counterparty integrity.
- The end of "free money": he has framed the 2022 downturn as the close of "the decade of free money in the U.S.," expecting markets to stabilize around a higher interest-rate environment rather than collapse permanently.
- Long-run digital-asset adoption: he has predicted that "a significant amount of the movement of money supply and payments are going to move to digital assets over the next 10 years," citing payment-efficiency gains.
- Bear markets as build periods: he has argued downturns are the optimal time to build infrastructure rather than to exit, contrasting the venture-capital depth of the 2022 cycle favorably against the 2018 bear market.
- Technology and market structure as the durable value: his consistent thesis is that the lasting contribution of crypto is not the tokens themselves but the market-structure and software innovations — continuous (24/7) trading, capital-efficient margining, and non-expiring "perpetual" derivatives — which he believes can be ported into traditional, regulated markets via good software and proper compliance.

His framing implicitly draws on his traditional-finance background at Jane Street and Citadel Securities: he positions himself as someone who can bring institutional-grade engineering and regulatory discipline to ideas that originated in crypto, an explicit repudiation of the operational sloppiness he attributes to FTX.

## Architect Financial Technologies: Product and Thesis

Harrison founded Architect Financial Technologies in January 2023, closing an initial seed round (reported variously around $5 million, with a subsequent $12 million round) with backing that included Coinbase Ventures and Circle Ventures, and later Anthony Scaramucci's SALT Fund. He has acknowledged that the FTX association initially made fundraising difficult.

Architect's central product is AX, an exchange offering perpetual futures — non-expiring, continuously settled derivatives contracts pioneered in crypto markets — but written on traditional asset classes rather than on cryptocurrencies. AX's stated coverage spans foreign currencies, interest rates, single stocks, stock indexes, metals, energy, and other commodities; notably, AX does not offer crypto perpetuals. Harrison's thesis, in his words: "We think the biggest and most important markets are derivatives of traditional asset classes. This is such a large market that we think is ripe for disruption using the new structure." The "new structure" is the crypto-native combination of perpetual contracts, continuous trading, and capital-efficient margining applied to mainstream assets.

The firm's regulatory trajectory reflects Harrison's emphasis on operating inside regulated perimeters: Architect became a CFTC-regulated derivatives broker in September 2023 and an SEC-regulated broker-dealer in October 2024. Because perpetual futures on traditional assets lack U.S. regulatory approval, AX itself operates through a Bermuda-regulated subsidiary and serves only non-U.S. institutional customers. In late 2025 Architect raised a $35 million Series A (announced around December 23, 2025), led by Miami International Holdings and Tioga Capital, at a reported post-money valuation of about $187 million; reporting also noted Architect acquiring a U.S. futures exchange in 2026.

In January 2026 Architect announced an expansion of AX into AI-compute markets: in partnership with compute-index provider Ornn Data LLC, AX said it would "imminently" introduce what it called the financial industry's first exchange-traded perpetual futures tracking the prices of compute inputs — daily GPU rental rates used in AI training and inference, and DRAM (memory) prices — pending regulatory approval. Harrison's stated rationale: "As capital expenditures for AI and AI-adjacent companies continue exponential growth, there is an urgent cross-industry need to establish standardized derivatives contracts and centralized order books for compute." This positions Architect at the intersection of his two recurring convictions — that crypto's market-structure innovations are portable into regulated finance, and that AI-driven demand is creating new, financializable underlying markets.
