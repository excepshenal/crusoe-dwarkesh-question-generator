# Ada Palmer — Reference Dossier

Ada Palmer (born June 9, 1981, Washington, D.C.; raised in Annapolis, Maryland) is an American cultural and intellectual historian, science-fiction novelist, composer, and free-expression advocate. She earned a B.A. in history from Bryn Mawr College (2001) and an M.A. (2003) and Ph.D. (2009) in history from Harvard. After teaching at Texas A&M (2009–2014) she joined the University of Chicago, where she became associate professor of history (2018–present). Her scholarship centers on the long-durée history of ideas — the Italian Renaissance, the Humanist reception of classical philosophy, the history of heresy/atheism/science, and the history of censorship. She is the author of the academic monograph *Reading Lucretius in the Renaissance* (2014), the trade history *Inventing the Renaissance: The Myth of a Golden Age* (2025), and the four-volume *Terra Ignota* science-fiction series (2016–2021). She writes the long-running blog *Ex Urbe*, composes and performs folk/Renaissance a cappella music with the group Sassafrass, and is a disability-rights advocate who lives with Crohn's disease and PCOS (diagnosed 2004). She is, in her own framing, a historian who studies how ideas spread, get suppressed, and get mythologized.

## Renaissance history and the "golden age" thesis

Palmer's signature historical argument is that "the Renaissance" as commonly understood — a glorious rebirth out of a benighted "Dark Age," producing the modern world — is a constructed myth rather than a neutral description of the period. Her 2025 trade book *Inventing the Renaissance: The Myth of a Golden Age* (University of Chicago Press; a *New Yorker* Best Book of 2025) is a work of historiography: it studies how the idea of a Renaissance has been built, weaponized, demolished, and rebuilt across six centuries.

Her core claims: First, the "golden age / dark age" frame was a *rhetorical invention*, not an observation. She traces it to Petrarch, who, living through the Black Death of 1348, felt marooned in "an age of ash and shadow" and proposed that recovering the books that educated the Roman elite could summon a new golden age. The construction was originally aspirational — a self-improvement program of imitating ancient Rome — and only later hardened into a descriptive historical period. As she puts it, to celebrate a golden age you must first invent a dark age; "rebirth implies death."

Second, Palmer argues the period was not a serene flowering but "altogether desperate" — an era troubled by centuries of war, plague, and political violence, in which Europeans looked backward to a lost Rome precisely because their present felt unstable. She marshals demographic evidence that mortality was *worse*, not better, during the Renaissance (children, adults, and men all died at higher rates), and notes that people resist these numbers because they contradict the inherited story.

Third, she insists the innovations credited to the Renaissance largely "began gradually during the Middle Ages." Medieval intellectuals read the classics, debated big questions, and deployed a sophisticated method (scholasticism). The sharp medieval/Renaissance rupture is, on her account, propaganda — first humanist, then repeatedly recycled.

Fourth, she argues there is no single "Renaissance X-factor." Her book examines the two most influential modern definitions and treats both as period-bound projections: Jacob Burckhardt (19th-century) located the Renaissance's essence in the birth of individualism and self-fashioning; Hans Baron (20th-century) located it in the first stirrings of modern liberal-republican government in the Italian city-states. Palmer reads each as a historian answering *his own* era's anxieties. Later actors — Cold War capitalists, colonial apologists — repurposed the golden-age narrative to legitimize present power.

**The reasoning beneath it:** Palmer's method is to track the actual circulation and reception of ideas (who read what, when, and how) rather than to assume that the presence of a "modern-seeming" idea means modernity had arrived. The mechanism she identifies is mythmaking-as-legitimation: regimes and movements invent a glorious past to authorize their present projects.

**Strongest counterargument:** Defenders of a robust Renaissance concept (in the lineage of Burckhardt and Baron, and popular historians) argue that *something* genuinely discontinuous did happen — the recovery of Greek, the print revolution, perspective, the new philology, civic humanism — and that demolishing the period label risks flattening real change into an undifferentiated continuum, a "nothing-ever-changes" reductionism. Critics note that her "it was already happening in the Middle Ages" move can prove too much. Palmer's reply is that change was real but *gradual, local, and contingent*, and that the periodizing label imports a teleology the evidence does not support.

**Internal tension:** Palmer wants both to debunk the golden-age myth *and* to affirm that progress is real and direction exists (see below). She must therefore distinguish "the Renaissance happened as a sudden golden rebirth" (false) from "cumulative human effort across the medieval and early-modern centuries improved things" (true) — a fine line that some reviewers find she walks gracefully and others find under-specified.

## Lucretius, the spread of dangerous ideas, and method

Palmer's first book, *Reading Lucretius in the Renaissance* (Harvard University Press / I Tatti Studies, 2014), tracks the reception of Lucretius's Epicurean poem *De Rerum Natura* from its 1417 rediscovery (by Poggio Bracciolini) to 1600. Its thesis: after rediscovery, Lucretius "threatened to supply radicals and atheists with the one weapon unbelief had lacked in the Middle Ages: good answers." Atomism offered a sophisticated, internally coherent system that explained nature without divine participation — a genuine intellectual rival to Christian cosmology.

Her surprising historical finding is *who* carried these ideas and *how*. The people who rescued and transmitted Lucretius were not radical scientists but poets and philologists who loved him as classical *literature*. Lesser-known, largely orthodox readers absorbed and passed along Epicurean material without intending heresy, gradually normalizing heterodox content inside mainstream scholarship — and thereby widening Europe's receptivity to "radical science," helping set the stage for the Scientific Revolution.

Methodologically the book is notable for a quantitative study of *marginalia*: Palmer cataloged the annotations readers left in manuscripts and printed editions to reconstruct how reading practices shifted over the sixteenth century. This data-driven reception history is the empirical backbone of her later, more sweeping claims about how ideas actually move through cultures (slowly, sideways, via unexpected vectors), and it produced one of her most-cited specific findings: that Machiavelli's interest in radical Epicurean materialism was *exceptional* for his generation — more characteristic of thinkers seventy years his junior than of his contemporaries. She uses this as a cautionary tale against "proto-modern" projection.

## Progress, contingency, and the critique of Whig history

In her widely read 2017 essay "On Progress and Historical Change" (published on *Ex Urbe* and in *KNOW: A Journal on the Formation of Knowledge*), Palmer stakes out a middle position between determinism and naive triumphalism. She attacks **Whig history** — the view that history is an inevitable march toward greater liberty and enlightenment culminating in modern liberal democracy — as teleological and presentist: it tempts historians to anoint certain figures as "forward-moving" and erase the period's real complexity.

Her positive thesis: progress is "not inevitable, but it is happening." She credits **Francis Bacon** with "inventing progress" in the early 1600s — the deliberate proposal that humans using systematic reason could, "little by little, make human life just a little easier, just a little better." Change occurred before Bacon, but the *project* of progress as conscious collective effort did not exist. The mechanism she proposes blends structure and agency: "Great Forces" (economics, class, demography) create the preconditions, but individual choices determine specific outcomes. Her metaphor — drawn from her papal-election classroom simulation — is that "the dam was about to break, but every person there got to dig a channel to try to direct the waters once they flowed." Her summary line: progress "is not transparent, but it is visible. It is not safe, but it is beneficial. It is not linear, but it is directional. It is not controllable, but it is us."

She is emphatic that progress is morally ambivalent: "the same Enlightenment impulse to make a better, more rational world... BOTH caused the brutalities of the Belgian Congo AND gave me the vote." And she rejects the idea that catastrophe breeds renewal: "destruction doesn't cause growth, it just sets it back; growth only comes from people working hard over a long time." This directly motivated *Inventing the Renaissance*, which she began in 2020 in response to journalists repeatedly asking whether COVID, like the Black Death supposedly did for the Renaissance, would trigger a boom. Her answer: the premise is false.

**Strongest counterargument:** A determinist or strong-Whig opponent (or a "great forces" economic historian) would argue that aggregate material trends — life expectancy, literacy, violence reduction — *do* describe a robust directional arc that individual contingency only perturbs at the margins; emphasizing contingency can become an excuse for fatalism or for ignoring structural drivers. **Internal tension:** Palmer simultaneously insists progress is "directional" and that it is "not inevitable" and radically contingent — a stance she manages by locating directionality in the *desire* of many humans to reduce suffering rather than in any law of history, but which leaves open how a non-inevitable trend can be reliably directional.

## Censorship and free expression

Palmer's active book project and her public advocacy concern the *real* motives and mechanics of censors from antiquity to the internet, with deep expertise in the Inquisition, early-modern book regulation (the *Index of Prohibited Books*, expurgation), and 20th-century media censorship. She teaches a Rare Book School course on the subject and delivered the 2023 Nuveen Lecture, "Why We Censor: from the Inquisition to the Internet."

Her central, counterintuitive claims: censors rarely try to *destroy* information. They label it, police access to it, and — above all — cultivate fear. Her formulation: "The vast majority of censorship is self-censorship, but the vast majority of self-censorship is intentionally cultivated by an outside power." The Inquisition's *expurgation* practice (forcing readers to cross out passages and write orthodox corrections in the margins) was, she argues, less about erasing content than about turning every act of reading into a reminder of authority — she likens it to modern blockpages, which function pedagogically to mark the boundary of the forbidden.

Her headline historical example reframes the Galileo affair: judged by the goal of silencing Galileo, the public trial *failed* — it made him famous. But it *succeeded* indirectly by frightening **Descartes** into withdrawing a completed radical treatise and rewriting it into something far more orthodox and Catholic, which then shaped French intellectual life. The visible "failure" masks the invisible success: the works never written. She also argues that pervasive censorship corrupts the historical record itself — once suppression is known to exist, every surviving document becomes suspect (possibly coerced, doctored, or written in fear), so later readers can dismiss anything as false.

On the present, Palmer warns that centralized digital platforms with automatic updates are uniquely dangerous: where banned print books survived in scattered copies, a platform can in principle erase or alter content near-simultaneously across millions of devices — a censorship capacity earlier authorities never had. Her admired figure is **Diderot**, who self-censored his atheistic writing to protect the *Encyclopédie*'s larger educational mission; her stated ideal is "a world where nobody ever has to do that again."

**Strongest counterargument:** Free-speech absolutists and some content-moderation skeptics would resist any framing that treats moderation as continuous with Inquisitorial censorship, while others argue that some suppression of harmful material is legitimate; Palmer's emphasis on *self*-censorship can also be read as understating cases where states did simply destroy books and people. **Internal tension:** she is a strong free-expression advocate who nonetheless treats Diderot's strategic self-censorship as admirable prudence — leaving an unresolved gap between "self-censorship is the censor's chief weapon" and "self-censorship can be the wise, mission-preserving choice."

## Fiction: the Terra Ignota series

Palmer's four-volume *Terra Ignota* quartet — *Too Like the Lightning* (2016), *Seven Surrenders* (2017), *The Will to Battle* (2017), and *Perhaps the Stars* (2021) — is a philosophical science-fiction work set in 2454. It depicts a post-scarcity, near-utopian Earth of roughly three centuries' peace, organized not by geographic nations but by seven voluntary, value-based "Hives" (Humanist, Cousins, Masonic, Gordian, European, Mitsubishi, Utopian). People live in chosen families called bash'es, work ~20-hour weeks on synthesized abundance, and circle the globe in under five hours via a flying-car network (operating since the prototype *Mukta*, 2073). Public practice of religion is banned after past religious wars; private spiritual counsel is provided by "sensayers." Gendered language is taboo. "Set-sets" — humans neurologically rewired to interface with data — are opposed by "Nurturists." The peace, it emerges, rests on hidden, manipulative, sometimes murderous machinery, and the central dramatic question is whether to preserve order at the cost of truth.

The series is openly an Enlightenment novel: it is narrated by Mycroft Canner, a brilliant convict-narrator who writes in an 18th-century memoir voice, directly addressing the reader and conjuring dialogues with the dead — a conceit modeled on Diderot's *Jacques the Fatalist*. Palmer draws explicitly on Voltaire, Hobbes, and the Marquis de Sade, and threads real intellectual history through the plot (providence, theodicy, the social contract, the constructedness of gender and morality). *Too Like the Lightning* won the 2017 Compton Crook Award and was a Hugo Award finalist for Best Novel; the series was a 2022 Hugo finalist for Best Series. Critics praised the ambition and worldbuilding while debating its dense accessibility and its provocative treatment of gender (the narrator deploys gendered pronouns in a society that forbids them, as a deliberate, unsettling editorial intrusion).

## Music, blog, and public-facing work

Palmer composes and performs mostly a cappella music blending folk and Renaissance styles with the group Sassafrass; her largest project is *Sundown: Whispers of Ragnarok*, a Norse-mythology song cycle narrating the cosmos from creation to Ragnarok, centered on Baldur's death and the conflict between Odin and Loki. Her blog *Ex Urbe* is a major venue for her public history (long essays on Machiavelli, Florence, Viking metaphysics, progress, and the Renaissance myth) and is where much of *Inventing the Renaissance* originated, including the foundational 2020 essay "Black Death, COVID, and Why We Keep Telling the Myth of a Renaissance Golden Age and Bad Middle Ages."

## Influences and interlocutors

Her intellectual lineage runs through Renaissance humanism (Petrarch, Poggio, Machiavelli, Montaigne, Pomponio Leto) and Enlightenment thought (Bacon on progress; Diderot and Voltaire on free thought and atheism). Within Renaissance historiography her chief named interlocutors are **Jacob Burckhardt** and **Hans Baron**, whose period-defining theses she critically dissects. On progress she positions herself against the **Whig** historiographical tradition (Herbert Butterfield's named target). Her reception-history method draws on the history-of-the-book tradition. As a science-fiction writer she names the philosophes — especially **Diderot** — as formal and thematic models.
