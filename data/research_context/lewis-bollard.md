# Lewis Bollard — Blind Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from primary sources (Open Philanthropy / Coefficient Giving grant writeups, Bollard's Farm Animal Welfare newsletter, his EA Forum posts and AMA, the 80,000 Hours interviews, and EA Global talks). It excludes any Dwarkesh Patel / Lunar Society podcast material.

## Biography and Career

Lewis Bollard leads farm animal welfare grantmaking at Open Philanthropy, where his title is Program Director (earlier, Program Officer/Senior Program Officer) for Farm Animal Welfare. He holds a BA in Social Studies from Harvard University and a law degree from Yale Law School. Before philanthropy, he worked as an associate consultant at Bain & Company, then moved into animal advocacy at the Humane Society of the United States (HSUS), where he served as a litigation fellow and as a policy advisor in its Farm Animal Protection Campaign. He joined Open Philanthropy in 2015 to build out its newly created farm animal welfare program.

Bollard is the author of a monthly newsletter (originally circulated as internal "Open Philanthropy farm animal welfare updates" / "Lewis's Notes," now the public Substack "farmanimalwelfare," recently branded under Coefficient Giving — the new name for Open Philanthropy's grantmaking entity). The newsletter has tens of thousands of subscribers and covers corporate campaigns, legislation, and alternative proteins. He is a recurring guest on the 80,000 Hours podcast (episodes on big wins against factory farming, ending factory farming, and "the 7 most promising ways to end factory farming"), has spoken on the TED main stage, and publishes regular grant writeups. 80,000 Hours has described him as plausibly "the single best person in the world to interview to get an overview of all the methods that might be effective for putting an end to factory farming."

## Scale of the Problem — Key Numbers

Bollard frames factory farming as "the most important moral crisis that we never talk about," anchoring it in population figures. At any given moment roughly 100 billion mammals, birds, and fish are confined in factory-farm conditions. He breaks land animals into ~23 billion chickens alive at any time (roughly 15 billion broilers raised for meat, ~8 billion laying hens) plus ~6 billion land mammals (pigs, cows, rabbits). Farmed fish he places in a wide 35–140 billion range alive at any point, explicitly flagging this as "incredibly imprecise because no one is keeping track." Wild-caught fish reach 1–3 trillion killed per year. Fish and shrimp, he notes, are the most numerous animals humans kill industrially; there are roughly three times as many farmed fish and shrimp alive at any one moment as all land animals killed in an entire year. For insects he cites order-of-magnitude figures (e.g., ~10 quintillion insects globally) but treats farmed-insect numbers as still emerging.

On comparative scale he emphasizes chickens and fish dominate the suffering math: "more than 10 times as many chickens" as pigs, and potentially "more than 100 times as many farmed fish" as pigs. He notes a chicken's brain is roughly one-hundredth the size of a pig's but argues brain size does not cleanly track capacity for suffering.

## Grantmaking Totals and Cost-Effectiveness

Open Philanthropy's farm animal welfare program disbursed roughly $30 million in its first ~2 years (from October 2015), ~$100 million across 160+ grants in its first ~4 years, and approximately $130 million cumulatively by the time of later updates; over its history the program has made 400+ grants ranging from ~$15,000 to ~$13 million. Bollard estimates Open Philanthropy represents about 25% of global farmed-animal-advocacy spending (broadly defined). The program's geographic split runs roughly 37% Europe, 29% US, 24% Asia, 9% elsewhere — a deliberate tilt given that ~80% of farmed animals live in Asia. He pegs total global spending on ending factory farming at roughly $290 million per year, dwarfed by ~$8–12 billion for climate work and by US companion-animal groups (the ASPCA alone exceeds $300 million).

His signature cost-effectiveness claim concerns corporate cage-free campaigns: "at least 150 animals per dollar," with a plausible range of ~100–250 hens moved from cages to cage-free for a year per dollar spent — under the assumption that campaigns brought reforms forward by about five years. Notably, Bollard deliberately avoids publishing detailed cost-effectiveness analyses inside grant writeups, citing the risk of figures being taken out of context by industry actors. For cross-species moral weighting, he has historically used Luke Muehlhauser's 2018 moral-patienthood estimates (which are "steeper"/more hierarchical) rather than Rethink Priorities' welfare ranges, while acknowledging the latter as the most comprehensive interspecies comparison to date.

## Corporate Campaigns: The Cage-Free Win

Bollard's central case study is the corporate cage-free campaign, which he treats as the movement's proof of concept. Beginning in early 2015, advocacy groups secured cage-free pledges from over 200 US companies, including all of the top 25 US grocers and 16 of the top 20 fast-food chains. The USDA estimated these pledges would affect ~225 million hens — ~70% of the US non-organic flock — up from under 5% of hens covered previously. Open Philanthropy seeded this with ~$3 million split among four groups: The Humane League, Mercy for Animals, the HSUS Farm Animal Protection Campaign, and Compassion in World Farming USA. Globally, pledges now span 2,000–3,000+ companies (including McDonald's, Walmart, and Costco).

Implementation numbers tracked upward: ~67 million US hens cage-free by summer 2019 (4× the pre-campaign level), against a target near 320 million by 2025. US cage-free share rose from ~6% a decade ago to ~40% (other sources cite "almost 50%" / 200M+ hens). In Europe's biggest egg markets (France, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, UK), at least two-thirds of hens are now cage-free; Sweden hit 100% in 2025; Brazil sits near 15%. In his "ten big wins" newsletters, Bollard highlighted McDonald's fulfilling its US/Canada cage-free pledge two years early (sparing ~7 million hens) and Japan's Kewpie pledging to quintuple cage-free production.

Bollard's hard-won lesson: "implementation is just as important, if not more important, than securing initial pledges." He documents egg producers scaling back transitions when companies failed to actually purchase certified cage-free eggs — a misalignment between corporate pledges and procurement. His writeup "Why Are US Corporate Cage-Free Campaigns Succeeding?" attributes success to a tractable, narrow, measurable ask backed by escalating pressure tactics.

**Counterarguments / tensions.** Within the movement, abolitionists led by legal scholar Gary Francione reject welfarist corporate campaigns as "blatantly speciesist" — arguing that "happy meat" reforms entrench animal use rather than ending it, and that anything short of veganism and total abolition "sets us up for failure." (Francione has specifically attacked groups like Direct Action Everywhere as covertly welfarist; philosopher Steven Best in turn criticizes Francione.) Bollard sits firmly on the welfarist/incrementalist side, citing the civil-rights and gay-marriage movements as precedents for "small achievable incremental wins" and "disciplined focus on narrow practical goals" over comprehensive systemic demands. He acknowledges "virulent disagreements within the movement" and discourages groups attacking one another while defending respectful debate. The animal-agriculture industry is the named external opponent; egg producers' procurement behavior is his documented internal-market friction.

## Broiler Chickens and the Better Chicken Commitment

After cage-free, Bollard's next corporate priority is broiler welfare via the Better Chicken Commitment (BCC) — a standard built on 150+ scientific studies addressing fast-growth genetics, stocking density (max 6.0 lbs/sq ft), enriched environments, and improved slaughter. He treats fast-growth genetics as the core welfare insult: birds bred to reach slaughter weight in ~42 days suffer lameness, footpad lesions, and organ failure. The Welfare Footprint Project estimates a slower-growing BCC breed prevents at least ~33 hours of disabling pain, ~79 hours of hurtful pain, and ~25 seconds of excruciating pain per bird. Bollard reports incremental wins — e.g., UK retailers pledging ~20% more space for ~350 million chickens (studies suggest this can halve footpad lesions), and German retailers Rewe and Lidl adopting reforms covering ~165 million chickens annually — while noting breed transitions lag pledges, making implementation the bottleneck again.

## Legislation and the Prop 12 Precedent

Bollard has grown "more pessimistic about legislative reform" after industry-backed defeats in the EU and UK, ranking legislative advocacy below corporate campaigns and alternative proteins in tractability. He notes the US has only two federal laws touching farm animals, neither with a private right of action. The major legislative bright spot is California's Proposition 12 (cage-free egg and crate-free pork sales ban), upheld by the US Supreme Court in 2023 — establishing that a state may ban the in-state sale of cruelly produced goods. Bollard frames this as protecting roughly seven other state laws and unlocking future welfare legislation. He also credits California's earlier 2022 sales-ban deadline with driving corporate cage-free action faster than voluntary 2025 timelines.

## Chicken vs. Beef and the "Small Animal Replacement" Problem

Bollard advances a counterintuitive welfare-per-calorie argument: because a cow yields vastly more meat per animal (and per brain) than a chicken, a serving of chicken entails over 10× more days of animal suffering than the equivalent serving of beef. Hence, holding diet constant, substituting beef for chicken reduces total suffering. This underlies his concern about diet shifts that replace large animals with many small ones. He couples this with a pragmatic premise — most people will not give up chicken, eggs, and fish — which pushes his strategy toward making those products less cruel (welfare reform) rather than relying on consumption elimination.

## Alternative Proteins: Optimism Tempered by Setbacks

Bollard ranks alternative proteins second only to corporate campaigns among promising approaches and funds R&D (e.g., the Good Food Institute) rather than betting on the market alone. He recounts the 2020–22 plant-based surge (Beyond Meat's IPO, Impossible, "The Game Changers" documentary) followed by stalled and declining sales. In "The Protein Problem" and "What Happened to Plant-Based Meat?" he attributes the reversal to taste/health shortfalls, price premiums during inflation (plant-based chicken often ~3× conventional), negative media, and especially the "ultra-processed" backlash. He cites a 2024 survey of 10,000 Europeans in which 54% said they avoid plant-based meat because it is ultra-processed — and argues the framing is inconsistent, since conventional chicken is also heavily processed (the extra "processing" merely occurs inside a confined animal).

On cultivated (lab-grown) meat, Bollard is openly skeptical on timelines, judging it "unlikely to significantly displace factory-farmed meat in our lifetimes." He has tracked the political backlash: Florida's SB 1084 (signed May 2024, effective July 2024) became the first US law banning cultivated meat, with six other states (Alabama, Indiana, Mississippi, Montana, Nebraska, Texas) following; a federal appeals court upheld Florida's ban in March 2026. He has also commented on EU moves to restrict "meat" labeling for plant-based products.

**Counterarguments / tensions.** Bollard's own surprise at Beyond Meat's IPO is the stated marker of forecasting humility. The internal tension he names is between technology-optimists who expect markets to solve factory farming and his more cautious view that R&D needs sustained philanthropic funding because near-term market displacement is unlikely. Alt-protein skeptics (and the ultra-processed-food critique) are the external pressure he engages directly.

## Fish, Shrimp, and Invertebrate Welfare

Bollard increasingly emphasizes aquatic animals on sheer numbers. He describes two suffering mechanisms: wild-caught fish asphyxiate slowly (minutes to hours) after capture, and farmed fish endure crowding, low oxygen, deformities, and mortality exceeding 50% in some systems over 1.5+ year cycles (versus 42 days for chickens). He cites evidence of fish pain (cost-benefit pain-avoidance behavior; antidepressants altering zebrafish depression-like symptoms). Wins he highlights include the Aquaculture Stewardship Council's new Farm Standard covering 1+ billion farmed fish, and the Shrimp Welfare Project's pre-slaughter electrical stunning agreements reaching 1+ billion shrimp annually (with installed machines capable of stunning 4+ billion). On strategy, he calls slowing aquaculture "near impossible" (production shifts geographically) and fishing bans politically infeasible and likely counterproductive (demand shifts to farmed fish), favoring instead reducing suffering within current methods.

## Moral Weights, Wild Animals, and Insects

Bollard's grantmaking concentrates on the most numerous farmed vertebrates (chickens and fish), with limited invertebrate funding. Rethink Priorities' 2021–22 Moral Weights / Welfare Ranges Project — covering 11 species (pigs, chickens, carp, salmon, octopuses, shrimp, crayfish, crabs, bees, black soldier flies, silkworms) — is the field's most comprehensive interspecies comparison, but Bollard notes results are highly sensitive to the weights assigned to chickens, shrimp, and fish, and that this sensitivity has "not often been action-guiding" given his portfolio's focus. He has flagged that he cannot publicly share internal point estimates and that cross-cause comparisons (farm animals vs. global health) remain early-stage.

On wild animal welfare, Bollard acknowledges the scale of wild suffering as "huge" and neglected (with the Wild Animal Initiative as essentially the only major group) but prioritizes farm animals on tractability — established, track-recorded interventions versus highly uncertain ecosystem manipulation. Insect farming he treats as an emerging concern warranting monitoring rather than a current major funding line.

## AI and the Future of Animals

In his "7 most promising ways" interview, Bollard frames AI as a double-edged force. Negatively, AI could remove the biological/management constraints that currently cap factory-farm intensification (he cites a Microsoft shrimp-farm example raising yields ~50%), and AI systems trained on current human behavior might "learn that it's OK to tolerate mass cruelty so long as it occurs behind closed doors," risking value lock-in. Positively, AI welfare monitoring (e.g., detecting distress or preventing bird pile-ups in barns) could outperform human oversight.

## Strategic Posture and Stated Internal Tensions (as facts)

- He ranks interventions roughly: corporate campaigns > alternative proteins > welfare technologies > movement-building in lower-income countries > legislative advocacy > movement-building in wealthy countries > institutional meat reduction > litigation > vegan persuasion > holistic food-systems reform > farm transitions.
- He is skeptical of leafleting and online ads (early studies lacked control groups), while citing a more rigorous MTurk study by the Animal Welfare Action Lab showing robust effects from reducetarian articles.
- He argues small individual donations to OP-funded groups likely do not "funge" against OP's fixed FAW budget unless new funding reaches the $50–100M+ scale.
- He warns against analysis paralysis and "solve everything at once" systemic approaches, favoring iteration and measurable goals; OP's goal is reducing suffering per animal and total animals farmed, not destabilizing global factory farming wholesale.
- He notes his grantee relationships have become more professionalized over ~9 years (more recusals, less perceived bias), at the cost of losing informal "gossip"-level intelligence useful for impact assessment.

Named entities recurring in his work: grantees The Humane League, Mercy for Animals, Compassion in World Farming, Anima International, Good Food Institute, Innovate Animal Ag, Shrimp Welfare Project, Wild Animal Initiative; research/infrastructure Rethink Priorities, Farmed Animal Funders, Welfare Footprint Project; funders Good Ventures, Navigation Fund; his personal (non-OP) donation to One Step for Animals. External opponents: the animal-agriculture industry and, within the movement, abolitionist critics (Gary Francione).
