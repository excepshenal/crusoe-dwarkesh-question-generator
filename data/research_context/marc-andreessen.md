# Marc Andreessen — Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference compiled from primary sources (Andreessen's own essays, the a16z blog, the Techno-Optimist Manifesto text) and secondary reporting. Organized by theme.

## Biography and Career

Marc Lowell Andreessen was born in 1971 in Cedar Falls, Iowa, and raised in New Lisbon, Wisconsin. In 1989 he enrolled at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign (UIUC) to study computer science, where he worked at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) and interned at IBM in Austin, Texas.

In 1993, while still an undergraduate, Andreessen co-created the **Mosaic** web browser with Eric Bina at NCSA — the first widely used browser to display inline images alongside text. Mosaic catalyzed the explosive early growth of the web; in its wake the number of websites jumped from roughly 50 to 10,000 and the share of internet users browsing the web rose dramatically.

In 1994 Andreessen moved to California and co-founded **Mosaic Communications Corporation** with Silicon Graphics founder Jim Clark. After a naming dispute with the University of Illinois, the company became **Netscape Communications**, and its flagship browser was renamed Netscape Navigator. Netscape's **August 1995 IPO** — the stock surged on its first trading day despite the company not yet being profitable — is widely treated as the symbolic opening of the dot-com boom. AOL acquired Netscape in 1999 for roughly **$4.2–4.3 billion**.

Andreessen then co-founded **Loudcloud** (1999) with Ben Horowitz, Tim Howes, and In Sik Rhee, an early cloud/managed-hosting company. After selling its hosting business to EDS, the company pivoted to data-center automation software and rebranded as **Opsware** in 2003; HP acquired Opsware in 2007 for about $1.6 billion.

In **2009** Andreessen and Horowitz launched the venture firm **Andreessen Horowitz (a16z)**. Early and notable investments include Facebook, Twitter, GitHub, Pinterest, Coinbase, Skype, Slack, Airbnb, Lyft, Instagram, and Stripe. Andreessen has served on numerous boards, most prominently **Meta/Facebook** (a long-tenured director) and Hewlett-Packard/HP, among others.

**Assets under management:** a16z grew from a $300 million debut fund (2009) to one of the largest venture firms in the world. Reported AUM was roughly **$42 billion in mid-2024**, climbing through the mid-$40-billion range, and surpassed **$90 billion by early 2026** after the firm raised more than $15 billion across several funds in January 2026 — described as the largest single VC fundraising haul in Silicon Valley history. That 2026 raise reportedly included a ~$6.75B scale-up/growth fund, ~$1.7B for AI infrastructure, and ~$1.12B tied to national-interest sectors (defense, housing, supply chain).

## "Why Software Is Eating the World" (2011)

Published in **The Wall Street Journal on August 20, 2011**, this essay is Andreessen's most cited thesis. Its core argument: the global economy was entering a "dramatic and broad technological and economic shift" in which **software companies would disrupt and absorb large swaths of established industries**.

His enabling-conditions argument turned on infrastructure finally reaching critical mass: "Six decades into the computer revolution, four decades since the invention of the microprocessor, and two decades into the rise of the modern Internet, all of the technology required to transform industries through software finally works and can be widely delivered at global scale." Supporting figures: roughly **two billion broadband users** (up from ~50 million a decade earlier), and cloud-computing cost falling from ~$150,000/month (2000) to ~$1,500/month (2011).

Illustrative examples: the world's largest bookstore (Amazon), dominant video service (Netflix), top recruiter (LinkedIn), and the music business shifting to Apple/Spotify/Pandora — all "software companies." He pointed to Google (marketing), Skype (telecom, acquired by Microsoft for $8.5B), Square/PayPal (payments), and named healthcare and education as the **next industries "up"** for software disruption.

**Reasoning:** declining costs plus ubiquitous connectivity make software the dominant means of value creation; incumbents that fail to become software companies will be displaced.

**Counterarguments / tensions:** Critics note the thesis is now so accepted as to be unfalsifiable, and that "software eating the world" did not uniformly raise productivity or wages — gains concentrated heavily among platform owners. A 2026 framing (Morgan Stanley/Fortune) inverts the slogan to "AI is eating software," arguing the same logic now threatens the very software firms the essay celebrated, including via displaced labor — a tension Andreessen's original optimism did not anticipate.

## "It's Time to Build" (April 2020)

Published April 18, 2020 at a16z.com amid the early COVID-19 shock, this essay argues that **Western institutions failed to produce** — masks, tests, swabs, ventilators, ICU capacity — not because the tasks were hard but because of a deeper failure of will. "Making masks and transferring money are not hard," he wrote; the binding constraint is "**the problem is desire**."

His central rhetorical move is "**we chose not to**": the shortages reflect decades of passive non-building masquerading as policy. He indicts **regulatory capture** and "crony capitalism" — incumbents and ossified oligopolies blocking new entrants — and calls on both political parties to enable builders. Domain critiques include housing (we should have "gleaming skyscrapers"; restrictive zoning blocks affordable supply), education (innovation stalled; one-to-one tutoring works but hasn't scaled), healthcare, and highly automated manufacturing. The recurring accountability question: "**What are you building?**"

**The "deployment problem":** while Andreessen does not use that exact phrase, the essay diagnoses the gap between *invention* and *implementation* — pointing to multi-year regulatory lags (e.g., post-Ebola vaccine timelines) and missing manufacturing capacity. The argument anticipates his later "abundance" framing: the bottleneck on progress is institutional will and regulation, not scientific capability.

**Counterarguments / tensions:** Critics including Max Read argued the essay rang hollow given what a16z subsequently funded (crypto, NFTs, "ape" projects) rather than the physical infrastructure the essay extolled. Others noted Andreessen personally opposed denser housing near his own community — a contradiction with his pro-building, pro-density rhetoric that critics repeatedly flagged.

## "Why AI Will Save the World" (June 2023)

This essay is Andreessen's direct rebuttal to AI existential-risk ("x-risk") arguments. He defines AI as "the application of mathematics and software code to teach computers how to understand, synthesize, and generate knowledge" — "a computer program like any other," "owned by people and controlled by people."

**Core claim — AI will not kill us:** Treating AI as a potential killer is a "**profound category error**." Because AI is "math – code – computers," it "doesn't want, it doesn't have goals, it doesn't want to kill you, because it's not alive." He frames apocalyptic scenarios as mythology rather than science, arguing doomer claims are non-falsifiable.

**Moral panic / "Baptists and Bootleggers":** AI fear follows the historical pattern of moral panics around new technology. Borrowing the regulatory-economics frame, he splits AI-safety advocates into sincere "Baptists" and self-interested "Bootleggers," arguing large AI incumbents push regulation to build "a cartel of government-blessed AI vendors protected from new startup and open source competition."

**His five risks and responses:** (1) killer AI — not scientifically grounded; (2) societal harm/"misalignment" — he reframes the real danger as censorship and a "government-corporate-academic authoritarian speech dictatorship"; (3) job loss — the "Lump of Labor Fallacy"; (4) inequality — false, since markets diffuse technology and real inequality comes from sectors *resisting* technology (housing, education, healthcare); (5) bad actors — legitimate but addressable by using AI defensively and applying existing law.

**The real risk — China:** "The single greatest risk of AI is that China wins global AI dominance and we – the United States and the West – do not." His prescription: deploy the full private sector, accelerate Big Tech and startups, keep open source open, and pursue "American and Western AI to absolute global dominance." Conclusion: "**We win, they lose.**"

**Counterarguments / named opponents:** The AI-safety / x-risk camp (e.g., researchers and organizations warning of misalignment and catastrophic risk) reject the "category error" claim, arguing that goal-directed behavior and instrumental risk can emerge from optimization without consciousness. Critics also argue the "Baptists and Bootleggers" frame is itself a rhetorical move that dismisses sincere safety concerns by attributing hidden motives. **Internal tension:** Andreessen invokes the China threat to justify minimal domestic regulation, while simultaneously arguing AI poses no real catastrophic danger — critics note that if AI is genuinely benign "math," the urgency of a great-power race is weaker than he implies.

## The Techno-Optimist Manifesto (October 2023)

A ~**5,200-word** self-published essay structured as roughly **108 statements across ~15 sections** (Lies; Truth; Technology; Markets; The Techno-Capital Machine; Intelligence; Energy; Abundance; Not Utopia, But Close Enough; Becoming Technological Supermen; Technological Values; The Meaning of Life; The Enemy; The Future; and a list of **patron saints**). The phrase "**We believe**" recurs about **113 times** (per Henry Farrell's count). Opening line: "It is time to be Techno-Optimists."

**Central claims:**
- "Societies, like sharks, grow or die." "Economic growth is not a cure-all, but lack of growth is a kill-all."
- Free markets are "a discovery machine, a form of intelligence." Technological innovation is "inherently philanthropic, by a 50:1 ratio" — creators capture ~2% of value while ~98% flows to society.
- "Intelligence is the ultimate engine of progress." AI is "our alchemy, our Philosopher's Stone — we are literally making sand think."
- **Energy:** humanity should raise everyone to current rich-world consumption "and then increase our energy 1,000x," powered by nuclear fission and fusion.
- **Abundance / population:** prices should fall until "all physical goods become as cheap as pencils"; Earth can support "**50 billion people or more, and then far beyond that**." "People are the ultimate resource" (an explicit echo of Julian Simon).

**"The Enemy":** the manifesto names a "mass demoralization campaign" running under labels including "**existential risk, sustainability, ESG, Sustainable Development Goals, social responsibility, stakeholder capitalism, Precautionary Principle, trust and safety, tech ethics, risk management, de-growth, the limits of growth**." It separately lists deceleration, de-growth, depopulation, stagnation, anti-merit/anti-ambition/anti-achievement, and "statism, authoritarianism, collectivism, central planning, socialism." The **Precautionary Principle** is singled out as "deeply immoral," allegedly invented to block civilian nuclear power ("perhaps the most catastrophic mistake in Western society") and capable of having "prevented virtually all progress since man first harnessed fire." The Club of Rome / "limits to growth" tradition is targeted within this enemy list. Andreessen qualifies: "Our enemies are not bad people – but rather bad ideas."

**Patron saints / invoked thinkers (a list of ~56):** the Italian Futurist **Filippo Tommaso Marinetti**; **Friedrich Nietzsche** (invoked against "the Last Man"); economists **Friedrich Hayek, Ludwig von Mises, Milton Friedman, Joseph Schumpeter** (creative destruction), **David Ricardo, Adam Smith, Paul Romer, Deirdre McCloskey, Thomas Sowell**; **Julian Simon** (population as resource); accelerationist philosopher **Nick Land** ("techno-capital machine"); futurists **Ray Kurzweil, David Deutsch, Kevin Kelly, Matt Ridley, Buckminster Fuller, Stewart Brand**; and the fictional **John Galt** (Ayn Rand).

**e/acc alignment:** the manifesto draws directly on **effective accelerationism (e/acc)** language and lineage; commentators (Henry Farrell) note one cited patron saint is associated with founding the e/acc "meta-religion" of a coming "techno-capital singularity." Andreessen explicitly positions the work as a "material philosophy, not a political philosophy."

**Counterarguments / named critics:**
- **Ezra Klein** read it not as a technology argument but as cultural reaction — "It's about how we were once brave and strong and we have become soft and weak" — labeling Andreessen's stance "**reactionary futurism**."
- **Nate Silver** said he agreed with ~84% of the statements but rejected its dismissal of AI existential risk and its accelerationism.
- **Adam Tooze** called it symbolic of "unjustified praise for late-stage capitalism and American nationalism," serving "niche, elite interests."
- **Henry Farrell** described it as a "Nicene creed for the cult of progress," emphasizing its quasi-religious form.
- Physicist **Mark Buchanan** said it "borders on the unreasonable" and is "techno-utopianism, not optimism"; **Elizabeth Spiers** called it "rambling and often contradictory," with "the pathos of the Unabomber manifesto."
- Broader critiques (Max Read and others) frame the manifesto as a justification for unchecked capital deployment that names tech-ethics and safety scholars as "enemies," and faulted it for not citing sources or engaging opposing arguments. **Internal tension:** the document insists technology is apolitical and "material" while simultaneously naming an explicitly ideological enemies list (ESG, sustainability, "tech ethics," "trust and safety"), which critics read as a political program in optimist clothing.

## American Dynamism and the Little Tech Agenda

**American Dynamism:** an a16z investment practice (co-founded by partner **Katherine Boyle**, with David Ulevitch) backing companies that "support the national interest" — aerospace, defense, public safety, manufacturing, energy, logistics, housing, supply chain, and critical infrastructure. These firms treat government as customer, competitor, or key stakeholder. The thesis crystallized around 2020 (heavily shaped by COVID supply-chain failures), with an early flagship investment in **Hadrian** (automated machine shops for aerospace/defense manufacturing) and a dedicated fund reported at ~$600M. The thesis is the institutional embodiment of "It's Time to Build."

**The Little Tech Agenda** (Andreessen and Horowitz, July 5, 2024): a policy manifesto distinguishing "Little Tech" (startups) from "Big Tech" incumbents, arguing American technological supremacy depends on protecting new founders and is now "a first-class political issue." It warns that incumbents pursue **regulatory capture** to entrench themselves into "government-enforced monopolies and cartels." For AI specifically, the agenda argues policy should **regulate harmful use, not model development**, keep open source open, limit a patchwork of state rules, and let startups compete.

**Political shift:** Andreessen has a long Democratic donor history (Clinton 1996, Gore 2000, Kerry 2004, Obama 2008, Hillary Clinton 2016). In 2024 he and Horowitz endorsed and funded **Donald Trump** (Andreessen reportedly donated at least $4.5M to pro-Trump PACs). Stated reasons: a May 2024 meeting with Biden-administration officials he described as "absolutely horrifying," claiming officials told them "don't fund AI startups"; opposition to Biden-era AI and crypto regulation; and allegations the administration "**debanked**" 30+ tech/crypto startups. **Counterargument / named critics:** TechPolicy.Press called the Little Tech Agenda "just self-serving nonsense," arguing it dresses the commercial interests of a mega-fund in populist language; a former a16z partner publicly accused the firm of "political infiltration" around AI; and critics note the tension in a firm with ~$90B AUM branding itself the champion of the "little" guy.

## Recurring Themes and Tensions (as facts)

- **Abundance vs. degrowth:** Andreessen frames the central conflict of the era as growth/abundance versus "deceleration," "de-growth," and limits-to-growth thinking (Club of Rome lineage). His opponents in this frame are degrowth economists and sustainability/ESG advocates.
- **Deregulation as the throughline:** from "It's Time to Build" through the manifesto, the AI essay, and the Little Tech Agenda, the consistent diagnosis is that institutional caution and regulatory capture — not scientific limits — bottleneck progress.
- **The optimism-vs-race tension:** he argues AI is benign "math" yet frames AI policy around an existential U.S.–China race, a juxtaposition critics repeatedly flag.
- **The scale-vs-"little" tension:** he champions startups and "Little Tech" while running one of the largest, most politically connected venture firms in history.
