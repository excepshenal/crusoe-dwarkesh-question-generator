# Ada Palmer — Reference Dossier

## Biography

Ada Palmer (born June 9, 1981, in Washington, D.C.; raised in Annapolis, Maryland) is an American cultural and intellectual historian, science-fiction novelist, and composer. She began college at age 15 at Bard College at Simon's Rock, transferred to Bryn Mawr College for a BA in history (2001), and earned an MA (2003) and PhD (2009) in history from Harvard University. She taught at Texas A&M University (2009–2014) before joining the University of Chicago, where she has been an associate professor of history (with affiliations in Classics and the Stevanos Niarchos / Stevanovich Institute on the Formation of Knowledge) since 2018. She was a Fulbright scholar in Italy and a fellow at Villa I Tatti, the Harvard University Center for Italian Renaissance Studies. In 2021 she received the University of Chicago's Quantrell Award for excellence in undergraduate teaching. Her scholarly focus is the long-durée evolution of ideas — Renaissance science, radical thought, heresy, atheism and freethought, censorship, book history, patronage, and the humanist reception of classical philosophy. She is also a published novelist (the Terra Ignota series), a composer with the a cappella group Sassafrass, and a scholar of Japanese popular culture (especially Osamu Tezuka). She lives with Crohn's disease and chronic pain, about which she writes publicly. She maintains the long-running blog *Ex Urbe* and the *Ex Urbe Ad Astra* podcast.

## Scholarly Work: Lucretius and the Renaissance Reception of Classical Philosophy

Palmer's first monograph, *Reading Lucretius in the Renaissance* (Harvard University Press, I Tatti Studies, 2014), studies how Renaissance readers actually read Lucretius's Epicurean poem *De Rerum Natura* after its 1417 rediscovery by Poggio Bracciolini. The poem threatened to give "radicals and atheists" the one weapon medieval unbelief had lacked: good answers — a sophisticated atomistic physics that explained natural phenomena without divine participation and argued against the immortality of the soul, the afterlife, and a creator God.

Her central methodological move is quantitative analysis of marginalia: she catalogued and statistically tabulated the annotations that real readers left in surviving manuscripts and printed editions, tracking *what* readers marked and how their attention shifted across the sixteenth century. Her core argument is that *the text changed as the methods of reading it changed*: the readers who rescued and disseminated Lucretius (Machiavelli, Pomponio Leto, Pietro Vettori, Marcello Adriani, Montaigne) were chiefly poets and philologists drawn by love of classical Latin literature, not scientists drawn by atomism. Renaissance humanists thus reintroduced atomism into intellectual life almost as a byproduct of literary enthusiasm, and the gradual normalization of these reading practices "expanded Europe's receptivity to radical science," helping set the stage for the Scientific Revolution.

**Reasoning/mechanism.** Palmer treats intellectual history as a study of mediation and slow accumulation rather than sudden rupture. Ideas spread not because they win arguments but because the social practices of reading, copying, annotating, and teaching gradually make them sayable. Her quantitative marginalia method is meant to recover what ordinary educated readers (not just famous thinkers) did with a text.

**Strongest counterposition.** Palmer's work is, in part, a corrective to Stephen Greenblatt's Pulitzer- and National Book Award–winning *The Swerve: How the World Became Modern* (2011), which dramatized Poggio's recovery of Lucretius as the spark that "made the world modern." Palmer's review essay "Lucretius after *The Swerve*" (*Modern Philology*, 2017) and her *Renaissance Quarterly* article "Humanist Lives of Classical Philosophers and the Idea of Renaissance Secularization" push against that triumphalist, secularization-as-modernity narrative. Critics of Greenblatt (e.g., in the *Los Angeles Review of Books* and in reviews pairing him with Gerard Passannante's *The Lucretian Renaissance*) argue he equated modernity too quickly with secular materialism and lost the nuance that many readers — Milton, Thomas Traherne, and others — absorbed Lucretian atomism *into* Christian theological cosmologies rather than against them. Palmer aligns with this nuanced camp: the orthodox, devout sources of unbelief matter, and "secularization" was neither inevitable nor clean.

**Scholarly reception.** The Bryn Mawr Classical Review (Quinn Radziszewski) called the book "a clear and useful work" and "a highly valuable contribution," praising the manuscript analysis and noting Palmer's own candid account of the limits of sample size in her statistics. The book is generally treated as a model of careful reception history.

## The History of Censorship and the Inquisition

Since 2018 Palmer has run a multi-year collaborative project, "Censorship and Information Control During Information Revolutions," with book historian Adrian Johns and author-activist Cory Doctorow. It combined a dialogue/video series, an undergraduate course (35+ students across disciplines), and an exhibit at the University of Chicago's Regenstein Library Special Collections. The project deliberately brought together scholars *and* practitioners — editors, publishers, librarians, activists — who normally never meet. It is the basis for her in-progress book, variously titled *Why We Censor, from the Inquisition to the Internet*. Her widely circulated synthesis is the essay "Tools for Thinking About Censorship" (published on *Ex Urbe* and at Reactor/Tor.com).

**Central theses.**

- **Self-censorship dominates, but is engineered.** Her signature formulation: "the majority of censorship is self-censorship, but the majority of self-censorship is intentionally cultivated by an outside power." Self-censorship and "middleman" censorship (by publishers, editors, librarians, awards committees) are produced by the same deliberate systems of fear and must be analyzed together.

- **Censorship is collaborative, not a single state act.** It works through cascading intermediaries who are pressured into compliance, rather than through one central censor.

- **Censors are always under-resourced.** Contrary to Orwellian fantasy, no regime has had infinite resources; the Inquisition perpetually complained it lacked the manpower to keep up with the printing press. The response was not exhaustive enforcement but *psychological projection*: visible markers of censorship — blackened-out pages, the Comics Code Authority seal, warning screens — remind everyone of authority's reach without requiring comprehensive surveillance.

- **Unpredictable, conspicuous targeting.** Regimes punish individuals visibly and unpredictably rather than enforcing rules uniformly, producing ambient fear so people pre-emptively self-censor.

- **Censorship poisons the historical record.** Once censorship is known to exist, later interpreters can dismiss inconvenient evidence as coerced, and project their own narratives onto documentary gaps.

**Specific examples she gives.**

- *Galileo*: The real success of his condemnation was not silencing him (assassination would have been cheaper) but terrifying Descartes — who suppressed his own work *Le Monde* — and thousands of other scholars into self-revision. Evaluating censorship requires knowing its actual goal.
- *Cardano* and the *Index of Prohibited Books*: an Inquisitor went through a condemned text page by page, manually excising controversial passages following the published expurgation guide — labor-intensive precisely because the visible defacement was itself pedagogical, a reminder of the Inquisition's reach.
- *Gesner's encyclopedia*: censors required crossing out praise words like "learned and excellent" applied to Protestant scholars.
- *Diderot* and the *Encyclopédie*: Palmer treats Diderot as a kind of free-speech hero who self-censored his atheist works to protect the larger educational mission of the *Encyclopédie* — preserving universal knowledge at the cost of his own genuine philosophy being lost to history.
- *Comics Code Authority*: because the First Amendment barred direct government censorship of comics, Congress pressured the industry into a "voluntary" code; later, Black Panther titles were disproportionately delayed through bureaucratic friction rather than outright bans.

**Contemporary application and tension.** Palmer places censorship on a continuum with other information control — copyright, ownership, platform policy, security. She argues a copyright lockout that prevents an Australian reader from accessing a work has "identical" effect to a ban, even with no censorship order. Her present-day worry is platform centralization: electronic infrastructure makes it possible to modify or delete every copy simultaneously, so distributed systems with divergent update policies are more censorship-resilient than centralized ones. A tension critics note: by defining censorship so broadly (to include market and copyright effects) she risks blurring the line between coercive state suppression and ordinary commercial or editorial choice — a line she herself insists still matters morally (censoring a friend's book versus one's own). She also holds, simultaneously, that scholarly detachment is essential *and* that historians have a duty to "show how bad it is and galvanize action" — an explicit pull between the analytic and the activist registers of her work.

## Tools for Thinking About Free Speech, Gradualism, and Progress

Palmer's most-developed argument outside the Renaissance is that *progress is real but not automatic*. Her 10,000-word essay "On Progress and Historical Change" (in *KNOW: A Journal on the Formation of Knowledge*, Fall 2017, also posted on *Ex Urbe*) is the canonical statement.

**Core claims.**

- Progress as a self-aware *project* was invented — she credits Francis Bacon in the early 17th century — meaning systematic reason could improve human life generation by generation. Before that there was change, but not deliberate progress as a goal.
- Her refrain: "Progress is not inevitable, but it is happening… It is not transparent, but it is visible… It is not controllable, but it is us."
- Progress "requires work." Her fish-tank metaphor: adding pH powder to fix one problem releases ammonia and kills other fish; advances create unintended consequences. The same Enlightenment impulse toward education and equality "caused the brutalities of the Belgian Congo AND gave me the vote."
- **Great Forces vs. individual agency.** Both modes of history are "correct, and useful." Economic structures and demographics set what is *possible*; individuals dig the channels that direct the water once it flows. Her annual classroom exercise — students role-playing the 1490s College of Cardinals and European monarchs in a papal-conclave simulation (undergraduates call it "Pope LARP") — shows the pattern: the Great Forces reliably produce a powerful pope and a war, yet the specifics (which cities burn, which alliances form) hinge entirely on individual "kingmaker" choices. "No one controlled what happened, and no one could predict what happened, but those who worked hard… achieved many of their goals."
- Progress is "subjective and local," and how we *present* it is morally charged: the same population data drawn as mountain peaks versus oozing pustules triggers opposite responses. She prefers metrics like infant mortality and "murder with impunity" rates as relatively objective.

**Reasoning/mechanism.** Palmer's synthesis rejects two errors: fatalist despair ("we have no power") and techno-utopian confidence ("progress is automatic"). Real agency operates *within* structural constraints and makes partial, generational differences. She has embraced the label "Hopepunk," distinguishing her optimism from the failure-focused dystopianism common in SF.

**Strongest counterpositions.** Determinist or "great forces" historians (in the Marxist or Annales/Braudelian traditions) would argue individual agency is largely epiphenomenal — outcomes are set by material structures. From the opposite side, declinist and cyclical thinkers question whether "progress" is a coherent category at all, given that her own Belgian-Congo example shows the same forces producing atrocity and emancipation. Palmer's response is precisely to refuse to choose: she insists both frames are simultaneously true, which critics may read as having it both ways. There is also a live tension with her censorship work — she stresses that progress is genuine and accumulating, yet her Renaissance scholarship insists that "golden age" and progress narratives are routinely weaponized as propaganda.

## The Renaissance Was Not a Golden Age; the Black Death Did Not Cause It

Palmer's viral 2020 *Ex Urbe* essay, "Black Death, COVID, and Why We Keep Telling the Myth of a Renaissance Golden Age and Bad Middle Ages," and her 2025 book *Inventing the Renaissance: The Myth of a Golden Age* (University of Chicago Press / Head of Zeus) make a sustained debunking argument. The essay arose from reporters repeatedly asking her whether, since "the Black Death caused the Renaissance," COVID would cause a new golden age.

**Central claims.**

- **The Black Death did not cause the Renaissance.** No single thing did. The plague caused *change*, not automatic prosperity. The post-plague "wage boom" rests on limited English studies; newer work shows wages stagnated or fell in many regions, and elites responded with labor-control laws and workhouses. The deeper drivers — banking innovation (from the 1100s), insurance (1300s), legal reform, urbanization, political centralization — accumulated gradually over centuries before 1348.
- **The Renaissance was culturally "golden" but materially terrible.** Italian city life expectancy fell from roughly 35–40 years in the 1200s to about 18 by 1500. Plague recurred in 7–22% of years in European cities through the 1500s–1600s. Wars grew larger and deadlier; witch-hunting intensified (the *Malleus Maleficarum*, 1486); parasite diversity in remains rose with expanded trade. Her image: Michelangelo's *David* covered in glitter paint, the myth obscuring the damaged original.
- **The "Middle Ages" were largely a Renaissance invention.** Petrarch (1304–1374), grieving and nostalgic for antiquity, projected an "age of ash and shadow" onto post-Roman history; Leonardo Bruni (1370–1444) formalized the ancient/middle/modern tripartite scheme. Her "Ever-So-Much-More-So" principle: the Renaissance is the Middle Ages intensified — the same banking, art, trade, and violence, just more so. Medieval and Renaissance exist on a continuity; historians cannot even agree on a start date (1200? 1500?).
- **The golden-age myth is a reusable political tool.** After Jacob Burckhardt's *The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy* (1860/1869) located the Renaissance's essence in "individualism" and the birth of "modern man," every later movement — capitalist, nationalist, secularist, democratic — could claim *its* X-factor as the true Renaissance spirit and thus claim to embody progress, while smearing rivals as the "bad Middle Ages."

**Specific evidence/anecdotes.** Ercole Bentivoglio's 1506 letter to Machiavelli lamenting Italy's "wretched fortune" and "swift ruin," written as Leonardo painted the *Mona Lisa*; Italian city-states undergoing 4–12 violent regime changes in the 1400s; the Greenland Norse settlements collapsing economically around 1410 *without* plague (showing decline needs no pandemic). Her conclusion on COVID: "Pandemic is a hallway leading to a room where something big is going to be decided — human beings decide." Unlike Renaissance figures, we have epidemiology, effective medicine, and the Enlightenment habit of analyzing and improving our own institutions — so a better outcome is possible "but a thing we have to work for, all of us, and hard."

**Reception of *Inventing the Renaissance*.** Named a *New Yorker* Best Book of 2025 and a finalist for the 2026 Hugo Award for Best Related Work. Reviewers (Chicago Review of Books, Open Letters Review) praised its "chatty," personal, joke-laden register while noting it is demanding, with a large cast of figures. The historiographic thesis — that there is no single "X-Factor" — is its backbone; the book reframes Machiavelli as a man enduring decades of war and plague rather than a "soulless monster." A natural counterargument is that in dissolving the Renaissance into continuity, Palmer risks the opposite error of flattening genuine inflection points; she counters that the *category* itself was drawn by Renaissance people who felt different and claimed to be different.

## The Terra Ignota Science-Fiction Series

Palmer's four-volume series — *Too Like the Lightning* (2016), *Seven Surrenders* (2017), *The Will to Battle* (2017), and *Perhaps the Stars* (2021) — is set in 2454. Geographic nations have been abolished; people belong instead to seven voluntary, non-geographic "Hives": Humanists (Buenos Aires), Cousins (Casablanca), the Masonic Empire (Alexandria), Gordian/Brillists (Ingolstadt), Europe (Brussels), Mitsubishi (Tōgenkyō), and Utopia (Luna City / the Moon). A global network of flying cars (the Mukta/Saneer-Weeksbooth transport system) can circle the globe in under 4.2 hours and made nationless life possible. Public religion is banned after past religious wars; private spiritual counseling is provided one-on-one by "sensayers." "Set-sets" are humans whose nervous systems are rewired to interface with computers, opposed by "Nurturists." The narrator is Mycroft Canner, a brilliant paroled mass-murderer who serves the world's leaders and is protecting Bridger, a child who can make the unreal real.

**Themes and technique.** Palmer writes in a deliberately archaic, 18th-century prose voice, reflecting an in-universe neo-Enlightenment revival; major influences are Voltaire, Diderot, Hobbes, and the Marquis de Sade. The stated themes: "whether humans have the ability to rationally remake their world for the better, whether gender and morality are artificial or innate, whether Providence is a useful way to understand the world." Society publicly suppresses the *category* of gender, so all characters default to they/them — but Mycroft willfully assigns gendered pronouns based on his own reading of personalities. Palmer reports outlining each of the 34 major characters three times (as "he," as "she," and ungendered) to build the effect. She frames the gender-neutral society not as utopian progress but as enforced silence — "two steps forward, one step back" — arguing gender liberation requires ongoing conversation, not a declared victory; gender in her world is performed and constructed, with biological sex existing independently of expression.

**Reception.** *Too Like the Lightning* was a finalist for the 2017 Hugo Award for Best Novel and won the 2017 Compton Crook Award for best first novel; Palmer won the 2017 John W. Campbell Award for Best New Writer; the series was a finalist for the 2022 Hugo Award for Best Series. NPR called the debut "astonishing." Critics consistently flag the density and the unreliable, philosophizing narration as either the chief strength or chief obstacle, depending on the reader. The *Los Angeles Review of Books* ran a dialogue, "Rockets and Voltaire," on the series' Enlightenment ambitions.

## Music, Manga, Teaching, Disability

Palmer composes and performs with **Sassafrass**, an a cappella group (mostly women) writing original close-harmony pieces on mythological and SF themes. Her largest musical project is *Sundown: Whispers of Ragnarök*, a Norse-mythology song cycle (creation to Ragnarök, centered on Baldr's death and the Odin–Loki conflict), funded via Kickstarter. She is a scholar of Japanese popular culture — co-teaching on its history, focused on Osamu Tezuka, postwar manga, and feminist manga — and founded TezukaInEnglish.com, a leading English resource on Tezuka. (Reports of a dedicated Sergei Prokofiev research focus appear conflated with her broader interest in opera and music history; her primary public musical output is Sassafrass and the Norse cycle.)

Her teaching is known for primary-source immersion and the costumed papal-conclave reenactment ("Pope LARP") that doubles as her demonstration of contingency in history. On disability, she has written candidly (the essay "Campbell Award & Invisible Disability" on *Ex Urbe*, and "Writing/Realizing Disability + Power" in *Strange Horizons*) about living with Crohn's disease, PCOS, and chronic pain — an "invisible and intermittent" disability ranging from unnoticeable to cane-assisted to fully incapacitating. She advocates for recognition of invisible disabilities and for academic and creative accommodation, framing it as part of her broader argument that flourishing is not automatic and depends on the structures a society chooses to build.
