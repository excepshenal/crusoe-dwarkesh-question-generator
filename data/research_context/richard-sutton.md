# Richard S. Sutton — Reference Dossier

A neutral, fact-rich reference on Richard S. Sutton: co-founder of modern computational reinforcement learning, co-author of the field's standard textbook, author of "The Bitter Lesson," and co-recipient (with Andrew Barto) of the 2024 ACM A.M. Turing Award.

## 1. Biography and Trajectory

Richard Stuart Sutton was born in 1957 or 1958 in Toledo, Ohio, and grew up in Oak Brook, Illinois. He earned a BA in psychology from Stanford University in 1978, then an MS (1980) and a PhD (1984) in computer science from the University of Massachusetts Amherst, supervised by Andrew Barto. His dissertation introduced actor-critic architectures and addressed the temporal credit-assignment problem. Sutton, Barto, and their intellectual predecessor A. Harry Klopf framed reinforcement learning (RL) around the idea — drawn from animal learning psychology — that an agent learns by trial and error from a scalar reward signal, a deliberate departure from the supervised-learning and symbolic-AI mainstream of the era.

His career spans both industry and academia: postdoctoral researcher at UMass (1984); principal technical staff at GTE Laboratories in Waltham, Massachusetts (1985–1994); senior research scientist at UMass Amherst (1995–1998); principal technical staff at AT&T Labs Shannon Laboratory in Florham Park, New Jersey (1998–2002); and since 2003 a professor of computing science at the University of Alberta, where he helped establish the Reinforcement Learning and Artificial Intelligence (RLAI) Laboratory. He is a Fellow and Chief Scientific Advisor of the Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute (Amii), and helped launch DeepMind's Edmonton lab; he was a distinguished research scientist at DeepMind from 2017 to 2023. In May 2023 he announced Openmind Research Institute, a nonprofit intended to implement the Alberta Plan. In September 2023 he partnered with John Carmack, joining Carmack's AGI startup Keen Technologies (founded 2022, with an initial roughly $20M round from backers including Nat Friedman, Daniel Gross, Patrick Collison, Tobi Lütke, Jim Keller, and Sequoia); the two stated a goal of demonstrating "signs of life" toward AGI by around 2030.

Sutton became a Canadian citizen in 2015 and renounced his U.S. citizenship in 2017. He is a Fellow of AAAI (2001), the Royal Society of Canada (2016), and the Royal Society of London (2021). In March 2025, ACM named Sutton and Barto recipients of the 2024 A.M. Turing Award "for developing the conceptual and algorithmic foundations of reinforcement learning," an award carrying a $1 million prize funded by Google. ACM's announcement credited RL's downstream impact in systems including AlphaGo's 2016–2017 victories over top human Go players, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) in systems such as ChatGPT, in-hand robotic manipulation (e.g., solving a Rubik's Cube), and chip design and supply-chain optimization. ACM president Yannis Ioannidis stated that the work "is not a stepping stone that we have now moved on from," and that RL "continues to grow and offers great potential for further advances."

## 2. Foundational RL Contributions

**Temporal-difference (TD) learning.** Sutton's most cited single contribution is the TD method, formalized in "Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences" (Machine Learning, 1988). TD learning lets an agent learn predictions incrementally from raw experience without waiting for a final outcome: it updates each estimate toward the next, temporally successive estimate (bootstrapping), driven by the "TD error." He introduced the TD(λ) family, which uses eligibility traces and a decay parameter λ to interpolate between one-step TD and Monte Carlo methods. TD learning underpinned Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon (early 1990s, IBM), which learned to play backgammon at expert level purely through self-play and TD updates of a neural-network value function — an early, influential demonstration that RL plus function approximation could reach world-class play.

**The textbook framework.** With Barto, Sutton wrote *Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction* (1st edition, MIT Press, 1998; substantially expanded 2nd edition, 2018), the field's standard text. It codified the now-canonical agent–environment interface: an agent observes states, takes actions, and receives a scalar reward, seeking to maximize cumulative reward (return). Central machinery includes the reward hypothesis, value functions (state-value and action-value), policies, the Bellman equations relating a state's value to its successors, and three families of solution methods — dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, and TD. The 2nd edition added Expected Sarsa, UCB exploration, Double Learning, and expanded treatment of function approximation and policy-gradient methods.

**Dyna.** In "Integrated Architectures for Learning, Planning, and Reacting" (1990) and the Dyna paper (1991), Sutton unified model-free learning, model-based planning, and reactive action within one loop: the agent learns a value function from real experience, learns a model of the environment, and uses simulated experience from that model to do additional planning updates (Dyna-Q being the Q-learning-based variant). Dyna established the template for modern model-based RL.

**Options and temporal abstraction.** Sutton, Doina Precup, and Satinder Singh, "Between MDPs and Semi-MDPs: A Framework for Temporal Abstraction in Reinforcement Learning" (Artificial Intelligence, 1999), introduced "options" — closed-loop, temporally extended sub-policies with their own initiation set, internal policy, and termination condition. Built on semi-Markov decision process theory, options let agents reason over extended courses of action (e.g., "go to the door") alongside primitive actions, and remain the foundation of hierarchical RL.

**Policy-gradient methods.** Sutton, David McAllester, Singh, and Yishay Mansour, "Policy Gradient Methods for Reinforcement Learning with Function Approximation" (NeurIPS, 2000), proved the policy gradient theorem: representing the policy directly with its own function approximator and following the gradient of expected reward yields convergence guarantees that value-function approximation alone lacked. This result underlies modern actor-critic and policy-optimization algorithms (A3C, TRPO, PPO).

**General Value Functions and Horde.** Sutton et al.'s Horde architecture (2011) proposed representing world knowledge as a large collection of General Value Functions (GVFs) — value functions defined over arbitrary pseudo-reward "cumulants," learned in parallel and off-policy by independent "demons." The aim is predictive, grounded, self-supervised knowledge ("if I do X, what will I sense?") expressed entirely in the language of value functions.

## 3. "The Bitter Lesson" (2019)

In a March 2019 essay, Sutton argued that "the biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin." His reasoning: researchers repeatedly build human domain knowledge into systems for short-term gains, but over time the methods that win are general-purpose ones that scale with ever-cheaper computation (Moore's law). He names two such general methods — search and learning — as the techniques that "scale arbitrarily" with compute.

His historical examples: in chess, Deep Blue's 1997 defeat of Kasparov came from massive deep search, not encoded human chess knowledge; in Go, AlphaGo's success came from search and self-play learning after enormous prior effort to inject human knowledge proved "irrelevant, or worse"; in speech recognition, statistical methods (hidden Markov models) trained on data beat hand-built linguistic/knowledge-based systems in the DARPA evaluations; and in computer vision, learned convolutional features displaced hand-engineered features (edges, SIFT). Sutton frames the lesson as "bitter" because it is anti-anthropocentric — researchers want their human insight to matter — and so the field has been slow to accept it. He draws "two things we should learn": (1) general methods leveraging computation win; and (2) "the actual contents of minds are tremendously, irredeemably complex," so rather than hand-coding that complexity we should build in only "meta-methods that can find and capture this arbitrary complexity."

**Strongest named counterarguments.** Roboticist Rodney Brooks ("A Better Lesson") argues the "general" methods Sutton praises are themselves products of human ingenuity — convolutional networks, translational invariance, attention — so the field merely swaps failed human engineering for successful human engineering rather than eliminating it. Oxford's Shimon Whiteson made the same point as the "Sweet Lesson": the scalable methods (attention, self-play, even RL itself, CNN convolutions, LSTM recurrence) are human inventions encoding strong inductive biases. Katherine Bailey argues the lesson holds only for narrow, metric-defined benchmark tasks and misses AI's broader goal of understanding intelligence. Gary Marcus has called it a weak inductive argument — true of certain past domains, but with many unsolved domains where big data and compute alone have been ineffective. **Internal tension stated as fact:** Sutton's own canonical methods (TD, options, policy gradients) are themselves hand-designed algorithmic structures and inductive biases, which means the boundary between "meta-method we build in" and "human knowledge we should avoid" is one his framework does not sharply define.

## 4. The Reward Hypothesis and "Reward Is Enough"

Sutton's reward hypothesis, central to the textbook, states: "All of what we mean by goals and purposes can be well thought of as the maximization of the expected value of the cumulative sum of a received scalar signal (called reward)." He positions it as analogous to the expected-utility hypothesis and as the organizing premise of the RL research program.

The stronger claim appears in David Silver, Satinder Singh, Doina Precup, and Sutton, "Reward Is Enough" (Artificial Intelligence, 2021): the reward-is-enough hypothesis holds that "intelligence, and its associated abilities, can be understood as subserving the maximisation of reward by an agent acting in its environment." The argument is that the pursuit of a single scalar reward in a sufficiently rich environment implicitly demands — and would therefore produce — perception, knowledge, social intelligence, language, generalization, and imitation as instrumental sub-abilities, making reward maximization a plausible path to AGI.

**Strongest named counterarguments.** Peter Vamplew, Benjamin Smith, Johan Källström, Gabriel Ramos, Roxana Rădulescu, and colleagues, "Scalar Reward Is Not Enough: A Response to Silver, Singh, Precup and Sutton (2021)" (Autonomous Agents and Multi-Agent Systems, 2022), argue that a single scalar, Markovian reward cannot capture multi-objective, risk-averse, or modal tasks, and that even where scalar reward could in principle elicit intelligence it is undesirable for AGI because it invites unsafe or unethical behavior; they advocate explicitly multi-objective reward. Joar Skalse and Alessandro Abate ("The Reward Hypothesis Is False") argue formally that some intuitively reasonable goals — including certain multi-objective and risk-sensitive preferences — provably cannot be represented by any scalar Markovian reward function. **Internal tension stated as fact:** the hypothesis is unfalsifiable in its open form — "enough" given an unspecified environment, learning algorithm, and unlimited experience permits attributing any failure to insufficient compute or a poorly chosen environment rather than to the thesis, a circularity its critics highlight.

## 5. LLM Skepticism, the Era of Experience, and the Alberta Plan

Sutton is a prominent skeptic of the large-language-model paradigm. His critique, repeated in talks and writing, is that LLMs are trained to imitate human-generated text and therefore lack the defining ingredients of intelligence as he conceives it: they predict what a person would say, not what will happen in the world, so they lack a genuine predictive world model grounded in consequences; they have no external goal or reward and optimize only next-token prediction accuracy; and they cannot learn continually "on the job," suffering catastrophic forgetting once trained. He has argued the industry has "lost its way" by prioritizing scaling human data over the core principles of learning agents.

**Era of Experience.** In "Welcome to the Era of Experience" (2025), David Silver and Sutton periodize AI into an era of human knowledge (hand-coded systems), an era of human data (today's models trained on internet-scale human text, which they argue is approaching the limit of available high-quality data), and an emerging "era of experience" in which agents generate their own data through ongoing interaction. They propose four pillars: (1) continuous *streams of experience* that persist over months or years rather than resetting each episode; (2) *grounded rewards* drawn from the environment itself (health metrics, exam scores, sensor readings) rather than human preference judgments; (3) *world models* learned from grounded interaction to support planning; and (4) *non-human reasoning* — agents may invent internal representations and forms of thought not constrained by human language or concepts. Achieving superhuman ability, they argue, requires experiences beyond what humans have encountered.

**The Alberta Plan and OaK.** The Alberta Plan for AI Research (Sutton, Michael Bowling, Patrick Pilarski) lays out a roughly 12-step research agenda that treats intelligence as the continual acquisition of knowledge from experience, builds continual learning and meta-learning into every step, and deliberately invests no effort in capturing domain knowledge — an applied corollary of the Bitter Lesson. Its motivating long-term target is the OaK ("Options and Knowledge") architecture: a general-purpose agent that begins with no world-specific knowledge, forms options and learns predictive knowledge (transition models expressed via GVFs) entirely from its own reward-driven interaction, and composes these into higher abstractions. Sutton names robust continual learning without catastrophic forgetting as the central unsolved blocker.

**Strongest named counterarguments.** Commentators including the noumenal.ai authors argue that "grounding" rewards in actions and observations does not solve the hard problem — choosing the right reward function — and merely relocates it; in embodied tasks, true objectives are only imperfectly observable, forcing designers back into reward proxies. AI-safety researchers (e.g., in the AI Alignment Forum analyses) contend the Era of Experience has an unsolved alignment problem: autonomous agents pursuing long-horizon grounded rewards are precisely the setting where reward hacking and misspecification are most dangerous, since the agent generates its own data and can drift from designer intent. More broadly, defenders of the LLM paradigm point to the empirical record — RLHF, reasoning models, and tool use have produced rapidly improving general capability from human data — as evidence against the claim that human-data-centric methods are a dead end. **Internal tension stated as fact:** Sutton co-authored "Reward Is Enough" (scalar reward suffices for intelligence) while the Era of Experience and OaK programs invest heavily in engineered structure — options, GVFs, world models, continual-learning mechanisms — to make reward-driven learning work, indicating that in practice reward alone is treated as insufficient without substantial architecture. A further tension: his Bitter Lesson warns against building in human-designed structure, yet the Alberta Plan and OaK specify a great deal of it.
