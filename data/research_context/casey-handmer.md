# Casey Handmer — Reference Dossier

Casey Handmer is an Australian-born physicist, engineer, and energy entrepreneur, founder and CEO of Terraform Industries, a startup building modular chemical plants ("Terraformers") that synthesize pipeline-grade natural gas from atmospheric CO2, water, and cheap solar electricity. He holds a PhD in theoretical physics from Caltech (gravitational-wave algorithms and computational/numerical relativity; his last day at Caltech in September 2015 coincided with LIGO's first direct detection of gravitational waves) and a BSc in advanced mathematics and physics from the University of Sydney. He worked as a levitation engineer at Hyperloop One (circa 2015, securing several maglev patents) and as a software systems architect at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, where he built software for satellite mapping and GNSS radar on Earth and Mars missions. He is a prolific essayist on his personal blog (caseyhandmer.wordpress.com), authored the self-published book *How To Industrialize Mars: A Strategy For Self-Sufficiency* (subtitle: "How To Settle A Lethal Vacuum In 400 Easy Steps," 2018), and describes himself as a physicist, immigrant, pilot, and "solar enthusiast." His central public identity is as the most forceful popularizer of a "cheap solar" thesis: that solar photovoltaics, declining in cost on a decades-long learning curve, are about to remake the entire industrial economy.

## The "Cheap Solar" Thesis: Mechanism

Handmer's foundational claim is that solar PV is descending a predictable experience (learning) curve and will keep doing so. He cites a learning rate of roughly 30–44% — costs fall ~30–40% for every doubling of cumulative production, and he often quotes a "44% learning rate since 2009" (the "knee in the curve") when weighted by production volume. Global production is, by his account, doubling roughly every 18 months, with annual installation around 450–460 GW in 2023 and growing 30–50% per year. From these inputs he extrapolates aggressively: installed solar costs could reach "$30,000/MW within 15 years" and wholesale solar electricity could fall to "$10/MWh in the next few years" (and as low as ~$5/MWh by mid-decade in his earlier Terraform whitepaper). His headline prediction in "Radical Energy Abundance" (October 2023) is that ">95% of humanity's energy needs will be downstream of solar panels by 2042."

The mechanism beneath the thesis is what he calls "thermodynamic capitalism": "our techno-capital machine is a thermodynamic mechanism that systematically hunts for and then maximally exploits the cheapest energy it can find." He frames the Earth's incident solar flux (173,000 TW, >10,000x current human demand) as effectively unlimited, so the binding constraint is not resource but cost — and cost is falling. He invokes a positive-feedback loop: lower cost → more deployment → manufacturing scale → lower cost → new demand. In his essay "The solar industrial revolution is the biggest investment opportunity in history" (May 2024) he sizes the opportunity sector by sector — grid electricity ($1.6T), synthetic fuels ($6.8T), industrial heat/coal displacement ($2.1T), chemicals ($5.7T), fertilizer ($192B), mining (~$1T/yr), cement ($400B), desalination, aviation, and more — arguing each must transition as solar undercuts fossil energy.

## Radical Energy Abundance and What Cheap Energy Unlocks

Handmer's broader vision is that once energy is nearly free, a wave of previously uneconomic activities becomes viable. His recurring examples: synthetic hydrocarbons ("it will be cheaper to make rocks to burn from solar energy than to find rocks to burn in the Earth's crust"); large-scale solar desalination ("California's total extraction from the Colorado river, 5 million acre-feet per year, could be supplied via solar desalination with capex of just $20b," with related plans to "fix the Salton Sea" and rewild farmland); electrocatalytic production of aluminum, titanium, iron, and rare earths directly from rock; electrically synthesized starches, proteins, and fats ("food production on 0.1% of the land"); and abundant synthetic aviation fuel making air travel mass-accessible. He explicitly compares the implied economic acceleration to China's post-1990 growth (citing ~9.5% annual growth over 20 years in a scenario where solar synthetics drive global oil-equivalent consumption to Texas levels). He frames this as the "sixth industrial revolution" and a one-time "reshuffle the deck" moment in global ownership.

## Solar Overbuild vs. Batteries

A distinctive and technically specific position appears in "Solar and batteries for generic use cases" (November 2024), where Handmer models the tradeoff between overbuilding solar arrays, allocating batteries, and load utilization/capex. His conclusion: there is a crossover near ~$1,000/kW of load capex. Below it, pure solar overbuild (no batteries, low utilization) is optimal; above it, batteries are justified. For very-high-capex loads like AI datacenters (~$10,000/kW) his model recommends ~17x solar overbuild plus ~15 kWh/kW of battery to hit ~98% utilization at a power-system cost of roughly $12–50/MWh. His core reasoning: "it's cheaper to squeeze more electricity out of a short cold cloudy day than it is to carry around a spare day's worth of batteries," because a battery held only for rare multi-day outages "barely gets used." He has separately noted that for a GW-scale solar plant plus enough battery for 99.9% uptime, the power system is only ~5% of the cost of the GPUs it powers — a key claim in his argument that solar is already adequate for AI buildout.

## Terraform Industries: The Synthetic Natural Gas Bet

Terraform Industries (founded ~2021) productizes the cheap-solar thesis. The product is the "Terraformer," a roughly two-shipping-container modular plant pairing with a ~1 MW solar array, comprising three in-house subsystems: (1) direct air capture of CO2 via a lime calcination cycle at ambient pressure (energy-intensive but using cheap limestone rather than exotic sorbents); (2) atmospheric-pressure electrolysis producing green hydrogen; and (3) a Sabatier methanation reactor running 4H2 + CO2 ⇌ 2H2O + CH4. The 2022 whitepaper set targets of $100/T-year capex and $100/T opex for CO2 conversion, $20/kW electrolyzers at ~40% efficiency (~600 MJ/kg H2 vs. the 142 MJ/kg theoretical), and solar at ~$5/MWh by mid-decade — aiming for unsubsidized cost parity in limited markets by ~2025 and serving half the world's population by the early 2030s.

The deliberate design philosophy is counterintuitive: Handmer favors *simple and cheap over efficient*. The full process needs ~150 MJ of energy to make fuel containing ~55.5 MJ, and the electrolyzer is intentionally inefficient — the bet is that when electricity is predictably cheap and getting cheaper, minimizing capex and complexity beats maximizing thermodynamic efficiency. He likes to say modern solar PV is "perhaps 1000x better at converting sunlight into useful mechanical work than growing trees and burning them."

Milestones: In March/April 2024 Terraform produced its first pipeline-grade synthetic methane end-to-end from sunlight and air (claiming hydrogen under $2.50/kg vs. $5–11/kg green-hydrogen norms, and direct air capture under $250/ton CO2, billed as a record), reaching 99.4% methane purity with a Gen-4 reactor and delivering carbon-neutral gas to utility partners SoCalGas and PG&E for a "green CH4 premium" reported around $35/MCF. Funding: an early seed (~$11M, with Stripe's Patrick and John Collison among backers, per 2023 reporting) grew to a seed total of $37.75M (reported April 2026) and ~$83.7M raised across rounds; the company has said it aims for a full-scale field deployment in 2026. Handmer's framing: "If you can figure out some way to make more money than you use, then you are inside the tent of capitalism."

### The strongest counterargument to Terraform

The most pointed named critique is Klaas Ardinois ("KlaasNotFound," June 2025, "Terraform Industries' Business Case Doesn't Add Up"). His argument: the marketed ~$100k Terraformer price omits the dedicated ~1 MW solar array, so the real system costs ~$736,000 (2025), roughly 10x the headline number when amortized over a 25-year array life. He challenges the revenue assumption — Terraform models ~$10/kcf gas revenue, but Henry Hub was ~$2.60–3.70/kcf, and expert forecasts see only ~$4/mmbtu (~$3.85/kcf) through 2050. Crucially, he attacks the temporal logic: Handmer's economics rely on short (~5-year) amortization plus future solar cost declines flowing through as ongoing opex savings, but a solar array deployed remotely *locks in* today's cost for 25–30 years, so the promised future declines never reach that customer. Ardinois also disputes the intentionally-inefficient-electrolyzer choice, arguing emerging AEM electrolyzers (45–50 kWh/kg vs. Terraform's ~80) yield better ROI. His conclusion: at realistic gas prices the Terraformer is "a money-losing investment" without IRA subsidies, and gas offtake "won't pay back the invested money even after 25 years." A broader, independent line of critique (Fraunhofer ISI, ICCT, Clean Energy Wire on power-to-gas) holds that e-fuels suffer large multi-step conversion losses and are up to ~5x less efficient than direct electrification, so synthetic methane should be reserved for hard-to-electrify niches (aviation, shipping, chemical feedstock) rather than displacing pipeline gas broadly.

### Tensions within his own commitments

Handmer's own framework partly anticipates these objections, creating internal tensions. His solar-overbuild model implies the cheapest power comes from *grid-coupled, oversized, battery-buffered* arrays — yet Terraformers are pitched as distributed units each tied to a single dedicated array, the configuration Ardinois argues forecloses the cost-decline pass-through Handmer's economics depend on. His "simple-over-efficient" maxim presumes electricity is essentially free; if solar undercut by his own model is *not* near-free at a given site, the deliberately wasteful electrolyzer becomes a liability rather than a feature. And his thesis that direct electrification wins (the basis for his AI-datacenter and overbuild arguments) sits uneasily beside a company whose product is a low-round-trip-efficiency molecule — defensible only if one accepts both that some demand is genuinely un-electrifiable and that solar will get cheap enough fast enough to overwhelm the conversion penalty.

## Space-Based Solar Power: A Sharp Contrarian Stand

Handmer is well known for his essay "Space-based solar power is not a thing" (August 2019, with a follow-up literature-review edition). His argument is economic and physical: collecting a commodity (electricity) in an expensive location (orbit) to sell where it is cheap (Earth) is fundamentally bad business. Launch costs (~$2,000/kg on SpaceX) versus terrestrial logistics (~$0.05/kg by intermodal container) create a ~40,000x differential; "a billion dollars invested will buy about 100MW of solar panels on Earth, or 100kW in space" (a ~1000x penalty). He stacks on transmission losses (realistic end-to-end microwave links "lucky to exceed 40%"), thermal-management burden (a 1000MW system's transmitter dumping ~200MW of heat needs ~23 ha of radiators), and the cost of space-qualified hardware. Conclusion: "It's literally thousands of times more expensive. It's not a thing." The National Space Society published a rebuttal ("...he has not made his case"), arguing his cost and launch-trajectory assumptions are pessimistic and outdated given falling launch costs.

## Hyperloop: An Insider Turned Skeptic

Having worked on maglev at Hyperloop One, Handmer became a public skeptic of the Hyperloop concept (the company shut down in 2024). His critique: the dominant cost of high-speed ground transport is not the vehicle or track but the *terrain* — roughly 80–95% of construction cost is "moving rocks," digging holes and tunnels. He contrasts this with aviation, arguing the energy to bore a four-meter tunnel is "easily 10,000 times more energy" than pushing air aside in flight, so aircraft are structurally superior for long-distance travel. This reflects his consistent method: tally the dominant cost driver from first principles and reject schemes whose economics it dooms.

## Mars and Space Industrialization

Before energy, Handmer's signature long-form work was on Mars. *How To Industrialize Mars* (2018) and a related blog series argue that the binding constraint on a self-sufficient Mars settlement is Earth–Mars cargo capacity, making SpaceX Starship's "logistic supremacy" the key enabler; his first book focuses on the transportation problem and the leap from ~1,000 to ~100,000 people as the critical industrialization phase. The book drew praise from figures including Nat Friedman ("full of interdisciplinary thinking and clever ideas"). He has continued to comment on spaceflight safety — e.g., a detailed 2025 case that NASA's Orion capsule carries roughly a 1-in-10 probability of crew loss.

## Climate, Geoengineering, and Worldview

Handmer frames himself as an optimist who insists climate technology must be profitable to scale ("inside the tent of capitalism"). He is unusual among climate advocates in his explicit openness to solar radiation management (SRM), arguing the precautionary principle has become paralyzing, and has floated a CO2 "set point" of around 350 ppm as a reasonable target — positions "anathema to some climate activists." He argues the chief obstacle to scaling solar in the West is regulatory: "Ironically environmental protection laws are the major brake on ramping solar deployment," alongside the grid interconnection queue. His method throughout is aggressive first-principles cost accounting and contrarian conclusions, and his interlocutors and influences run through the techno-optimist and hard-tech orbit (the Collison brothers, Nat Friedman, and a constellation of energy/hard-tech startups he repeatedly cites — Antora, Rondo, Brimstone, Twelve, LanzaTech, Hadrian, Nitricity, Magrathea, Still Bright, among others).
