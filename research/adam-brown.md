## Broad research

Adam R. Brown is a theoretical physicist who built his reputation in quantum gravity, holography, quantum computational complexity, and early-universe cosmology, and who since 2018 has worked at Google DeepMind, where he founded and leads the "Blueshift" team and is a core contributor to the Gemini models, focused on advancing the scientific and reasoning capabilities of AI. He studied physics and philosophy at Oxford, completed a PhD at Columbia University, and held faculty/research positions in the physics departments at Princeton and Stanford, where he taught general relativity and continued to publish in high-energy theory. His academic work spans an unusually wide range — the Big Bang, inflation, the multiverse, black holes, quantum computation, "space elevators," "bubbles of nothing," and the long-term fate of the universe — unified by a recurring interest in the deep connections between physics and computer science. His most cited contributions came in the mid-2010s collaborations with Leonard Susskind at Stanford on the relationship between quantum complexity and the interiors of black holes.

### Quantum complexity and the black hole interior

Brown's central body of work concerns a puzzle posed by Susskind: the volume of the wormhole (Einstein-Rosen bridge) behind a two-sided eternal AdS black hole keeps growing for an exponentially long time, long after the black hole has thermalized and its entanglement entropy has saturated. Entanglement entropy stops being a useful bulk diagnostic almost immediately, yet the interior keeps changing. The proposal Brown helped develop is that the quantity tracking this growth is the **quantum computational complexity** of the dual boundary state — roughly, the minimum number of elementary gates needed to prepare the state from a simple reference state. Complexity continues to grow long after entropy maxes out, matching the wormhole's behavior.

Two competing precise statements emerged. The earlier "**Complexity = Volume**" (CV) conjecture tied complexity to the maximal spatial volume of a slice through the wormhole. Brown's own contribution was the sharper "**Complexity = Action**" (CA) conjecture, in:

- **"Complexity Equals Action"** (Brown, Daniel A. Roberts, Susskind, Brian Swingle, Ying Zhao), arXiv:1509.07876, September 2015; and the long companion **"Complexity, action, and black holes"** (same authors), arXiv:1512.04993, December 2015, published as *Phys. Rev. Lett.* **116**, 191301 (2016) under the title "Holographic Complexity Equals Bulk Action?". The conjecture: the complexity of the holographic boundary state equals the classical gravitational action evaluated on the **Wheeler-DeWitt patch** (the bulk region bounded by light sheets emanating from the boundary time slice), divided by π ℏ. They tested it against neutral, charged, and rotating AdS black holes, and against geometries perturbed by static shells and by shock waves.

The reasoning for preferring CA over CV: action is a more natural, less ambiguous bulk quantity than "maximal volume" (which requires choosing a slicing), and the late-time growth rate that CA predicts has a clean physical interpretation. For a neutral black hole the conjecture yields a complexification rate dC/dt = 2M/(π ℏ) — that is, 2E/π ℏ. This connects directly to a known limit from quantum information: **Lloyd's bound** on the maximum rate at which a physical system of energy E can perform orthogonalizing operations (≈ 2E/π ℏ). The headline claim is that black holes **saturate** Lloyd's bound — they are "the fastest computers in nature." Charged and rotating black holes complicate this: subtracting the contribution of the charge (the energy above the ground state at fixed charge) modifies the rate, and the authors found regimes where the naive bound is violated, which they took as both a test and a puzzle for the conjecture.

### Complexity geometry, negative curvature, and the "second law"

A parallel thread reformulates complexity using Riemannian geometry, following Michael Nielsen's idea that the complexity of a unitary is the length of the shortest geodesic to it through the group of unitaries SU(2^K) under a "right-invariant" metric that penalizes hard (many-body) directions.

- **"Quantum Complexity and Negative Curvature"** (Brown, Susskind, Zhao), arXiv:1608.02612, August 2016, *Phys. Rev. D* **95**, 045010 (2017). Core idea: complexity growth looks like geodesic motion on a space of **negative curvature**. On negatively curved manifolds nearby geodesics diverge exponentially (the hallmark of chaos), which gives complexity its long, steady linear growth; the eventual saturation comes from the finite volume/dimension of the space. Negative curvature is thus offered as the geometric *mechanism* behind both the growth and the eventual halt.

- **"The Second Law of Quantum Complexity"** (Brown, Susskind), arXiv:1701.01107, January 2017, *Phys. Rev. D* **97**, 086015 (2018). The argument: complexity behaves like an entropy. Brown and Susskind construct an explicit correspondence mapping the circuit complexity of a system of K qubits to the *positional entropy* of an auxiliary classical system with 2^K degrees of freedom (a particle moving on the complexity manifold), with the Kolmogorov complexity of the Hamiltonian playing the role of a kinetic term. Because the auxiliary classical system's phase space is overwhelmingly dominated by high-complexity configurations, complexity almost always increases — a "Second Law of Complexity," statistical and probabilistic in exactly the way Boltzmann's second law is. They also introduce **"uncomplexity"** — the gap between a state's current complexity and the maximum — as a *resource* for directed computation, which they argue is the holographic dual of the accessible spacetime volume behind the horizon.

- **"Universality in long-distance geometry and quantum complexity"** (Brown, Michael H. Freedman, Henry W. Lin, Susskind), arXiv:2111.12700, *Nature* **622**, 58–62 (2023). This pushes the geometric program toward rigor. The observation: many different homogeneous metrics on a Lie group can have wildly different short-distance structure yet nearly identical *long-distance* distance functions — a "universality class" in the renormalization-group sense applied to geometry rather than to physics. Applied to complexity, this argues that the many superficially different definitions of quantum complexity (different gate sets, different penalty schedules) are all *linearly related* at long distances, so the late-time linear-growth-then-saturation picture is robust and largely definition-independent. This addresses a standing objection: that "complexity" is too ambiguous (gate-set-dependent) to be a fundamental physical quantity dual to a sharp geometric object.

### The Brown-Susskind linear-growth conjecture and its proof

Out of this work came a precise mathematical claim — sometimes called the **Brown-Susskind conjecture** — that the exact circuit complexity of a *random* quantum circuit grows linearly in the number of gates and continues for a time *exponential* in the number of qubits before saturating. Notably, Brown was not an author of the rigorous proof; it was supplied by an independent group — Jonas Haferkamp, Philippe Faist, Naga B. T. Kothakonda, Jens Eisert, and Nicole Yunger Halpern — in **"Linear growth of quantum circuit complexity,"** arXiv:2106.05305 (June 2021), *Nature Physics* **18**, 528 (2022). They proved that for random circuits the exact complexity grows linearly with the gate count, with unit probability, until saturating after exponentially many gates, using tools from algebraic geometry and differential topology plus an inductive Clifford-circuit construction. This is significant because lower-bounding *exact* circuit complexity is notoriously hard; the proof is regarded as confirming the plausibility of the gravity-side picture that motivated it.

### Tensions and open questions within this program

The CA and CV proposals are not fully reconciled — both reproduce qualitative features (late-time linear growth, switchback effects from shock waves) but disagree in detail (coefficients, behavior near horizons, the effect of charge), and which, if either, is the "true" definition remains unsettled in the field. A second internal tension is the definitional ambiguity of complexity itself, which Brown's universality work (2023) was largely an attempt to defuse. A third is conceptual: the entire edifice treats a sharply UV-sensitive, gate-set-dependent information-theoretic quantity as dual to a smooth geometric bulk object, and skeptics in the holography community have questioned whether complexity is observable or even well-defined in continuum field theory — a worry the universality argument addresses but does not fully close.

### Cosmology, vacuum decay, and "bubbles of nothing"

Before and alongside the complexity work, Brown worked on the cosmological landscape and the instability of vacua.

- **"Bubbles of Nothing and the Fastest Decay in the Landscape"** (Brown, Alex Dahlen), arXiv:1010.5240, October 2010, *Phys. Rev. D* **84**, 043518 (2011). A "bubble of nothing" is a violent decay in which a compact extra dimension pinches off and collapses; the resulting "hole in spacetime" expands at the speed of light, leaving literally nothing — no spacetime — behind. Working in 6D Einstein-Maxwell theory in a thin-brane approximation, Brown and Dahlen showed that in a flux landscape built from many *different* fluxes, the fastest decay is often to discharge *all* flux simultaneously, which destabilizes the radion and nucleates a bubble of nothing; they constructed a new instanton mediating simultaneous flux tunneling and decompactification, obtained as the limit of removing ever more flux. (In a landscape of many identical units, by contrast, the fastest decay removes just one unit.)

- **"Vacua Morghulis"** (Brown, Dahlen), arXiv:1610.04564, October 2016. The provocative thesis (the title riffs on "all vacua must die"): every non-supersymmetric vacuum in the string landscape is at most metastable and must eventually decay, with supersymmetric vacua only marginally stable.

- **"Tensile Strength and the Mining of Black Holes"** (Brown, sole author), arXiv:1207.3342, July 2012, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* **111**, 211301 (2013). A characteristically clever result: the **null energy condition** imposes a universal upper bound on the tensile-strength-to-weight ratio of any material. A consequence is that one cannot lower a rigid box near a black hole horizon that is wider than a single wavelength of the Hawking quanta, which means proposed schemes to "mine" black holes by lowering boxes to harvest Hawking radiation — and "space elevators" operated near horizons — cannot work nearly as fast as had been claimed. Brown noted that, ironically, one *can* extract energy quickly by simply threading the horizon with cosmic strings instead.

These cosmology papers share a temperament with the complexity work: take an exotic but physically grounded scenario, push the relevant bound or rate to its sharpest expressible form, and extract a surprising universal limit (Lloyd's bound for computation, the null energy condition for material strength, fastest-decay channels for the landscape).

### Move to AI: Blueshift, Gemini, and "AGI and the future of physics"

Brown joined Google in 2018 and now leads **Blueshift**, a Google DeepMind research team aimed at the mathematical, scientific, and reasoning capabilities of large models, and is a core contributor to **Gemini**. The reported framing of Blueshift is "cracking maths and reasoning" at DeepMind. He has given public talks — e.g. at the Aspen Center for Physics and a Stanford Applied Physics/Physics colloquium — under titles such as "Training Sand to Think: Artificial General Intelligence and the Future of Science" and "Artificial General Intelligence and the Future of Physics," in which his recurring theme is that progress in machine reasoning could change how fundamental science (including physics) is done. This pivot is continuous with his long-stated interest in the connections between physics and computer science.

### Collaborators and influences

His dominant collaborator is **Leonard Susskind** (Stanford), with whom he co-authored most of the complexity work and from whom the original wormhole-volume puzzle came. Other frequent co-authors: **Daniel A. Roberts**, **Brian Swingle**, and **Ying Zhao** on the Complexity = Action papers; **Alex Dahlen** on the cosmology/landscape papers; and **Michael H. Freedman** (Fields Medalist, topologist) and **Henry W. Lin** on the 2023 *Nature* universality paper. The intellectual lineage of his complexity work runs through **Michael Nielsen** (geometric/Riemannian formulation of circuit complexity), the broader AdS/CFT and black-hole-information community, and the holographic-complexity program also pursued by groups around the world that built on the CA/CV conjectures.

### Notable specifics

- Brown's range is unusually broad for a theorist of his cohort — from formal holography to the engineering-flavored "can you build a space elevator" physics — and his paper titles ("Vacua Morghulis"; "Complexity Equals Action") reflect a taste for memorable framing.
- He has written for *Scientific American* and engaged in public-facing science communication.
- A clean throughline across his career is the *fastest-X / sharpest-bound* instinct: fastest computer (black holes saturating Lloyd's bound), fastest decay (bubbles of nothing), the hardest possible material (null-energy-condition limit on tensile strength), and the longest possible monotone growth (linear complexity growth for exponential time).
