## Broad research

### One-paragraph bio

Patrick Collison (born September 9, 1988, in Limerick, Ireland; raised in Dromineer, County Tipperary) is an Irish entrepreneur, the co-founder and CEO of the payments company Stripe, and a prominent advocate for the study of scientific and economic progress. The son of a microbiologist mother and an electronic-engineer father, he won Ireland's 41st Young Scientist and Technology Exhibition in 2005 at age 16 for a Lisp-derived programming language called Croma, briefly attended MIT, and left to pursue startups. With his younger brother John he founded the eBay-seller-tools company Auctomatic (originally "Shuppa"), which sold to Live Current Media in 2008, making both brothers teenage millionaires. In 2010 the brothers co-founded Stripe, which grew into one of the most valuable private technology companies in the world. Beyond Stripe, Collison is known for launching the COVID-era science-funding experiment Fast Grants (2020) with economist Tyler Cowen, co-founding the biomedical research nonprofit Arc Institute (2021), co-authoring the 2019 Atlantic essay that coined "Progress Studies," and maintaining a widely read personal website of essays, reading lists, and open questions about why progress happens and why it may be slowing.

### Major work, with specifics

**Stripe.** Patrick and John Collison founded Stripe in 2010 to make it dramatically easier for developers to accept online payments with a few lines of code, in contrast to the slow, complex merchant-account setup that prevailed at the time. The company drew on the brothers' prior Y Combinator ties (from Auctomatic, a Winter 2007 YC company) and Patrick's relationship with Paul Graham to raise early funding. Early investors included Peter Thiel, Elon Musk, Sequoia's Michael Moritz, and Andreessen Horowitz; a 2011 round of roughly $2 million included Thiel and Musk. Stripe's valuation climbed steeply over the following decade (cited at roughly $9.2 billion in 2016 and around $35 billion in 2019, with later rounds higher), and the Collison brothers were widely described in 2016 as among the world's youngest self-made billionaires. Patrick has served as CEO throughout; he joined the board of Meta Platforms in April 2025.

**"Science Is Getting Less Bang for Its Buck" (The Atlantic, November 18, 2018).** Co-authored with physicist and writer Michael Nielsen, this essay is Collison's most data-driven public argument. Its core claim: humanity has increased spending on science enormously over the past century — far faster than inflation, with the number of researchers and the dollars per discovery rising by large multiples — yet the rate of major breakthroughs has not risen commensurately, implying that research productivity *per researcher, per hour, or per dollar* has fallen sharply. To test this against the intuition that "we just notice old discoveries more," the authors ran a survey of working scientists asking them to compare pairs of Nobel-Prize-winning discoveries within their own fields (physics, chemistry, physiology/medicine) and rank quality across decades; they used these rankings to chart how the perceived quality of Nobel-caliber work changed over time. They found little evidence that the quality of prizewinning discoveries was rising over the decades, and noted the analysis effectively stops in the late 1980s because the Nobel committees have increasingly honored older work (only a small number of post-1990 discoveries had been recognized — too few to estimate). They marshal supporting evidence from the economics literature, most prominently Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, and Webb's "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?" (later published in the *American Economic Review*, 2020), which found, for example, that sustaining Moore's Law required on the order of 18 times more researchers in the 2010s than in the early 1970s, and that research productivity in domains such as agricultural crop yields was declining at roughly 5% per year. The essay's posture is deliberately careful: the authors stop short of asserting that aggregate progress has stalled and instead emphasize the declining per-input return.

**"We Need a New Science of Progress" (The Atlantic, July 30, 2019).** Co-authored with Tyler Cowen, this essay coined the term "Progress Studies" and functions as a manifesto. The authors define progress as "the combination of economic, technological, scientific, cultural, and organizational advancement that has transformed our lives and raised standards of living over the past couple of centuries," and argue that this phenomenon is "understudied": no broad-based intellectual movement is dedicated to understanding what causes it and how to get more of it. They propose Progress Studies as an explicitly applied, interventionist field — in their framing, "closer to medicine than biology: the goal is to treat, not merely to understand." Success, they write, would come from identifying "effective progress-increasing interventions" and getting them adopted by universities, funding agencies, philanthropists, entrepreneurs, and policymakers. The essay catalyzed a movement: Collison maintains a "Progress" page on his site collecting the responses, and the term seeded journals, conferences, and organizations in the following years.

**Fast Grants (2020).** With Tyler Cowen, Collison launched Fast Grants in April 2020 as a rapid-turnaround funding vehicle for COVID-19 science, prompted by frustration that conventional grant cycles were far too slow for a pandemic. In their own retrospective ("What We Learned Doing Fast Grants"), Collison, Cowen, and Patrick Hsu report that the program raised over $50 million and made more than 260 grants in 2020 (grants typically $10,000–$500,000), distributed initial awards within 48 hours of launch and later rounds within about two weeks, used applications that took scientists under 30 minutes to complete, ran at under 3% overhead with roughly four part-time staff plus about 20 reviewing scientists, and drew over 6,000 applications. Donors included John and Patrick Collison, the Crankstart Foundation, Paul Graham, Reid Hoffman, Yuri and Julia Milner, Chris and Crystal Sacca, and others. The retrospective reports striking survey results from grantees that became part of Collison's broader critique of science funding: roughly 64% said the funded work would not otherwise have happened; about 57% reported spending more than a quarter of their time on grant applications; 78% said they would change their research program "a lot" if their existing funding could be spent in an unconstrained way; 81% said their work would become more ambitious; and 62% said they would pursue work outside their standard field. They contrast Fast Grants' speed with NIH decision timelines they put at roughly 200–600 days, concluding that "alternative models of science funding can work."

**Arc Institute (2021).** Collison co-founded the Arc Institute with biochemist Silvana Konermann (whom he later married) and bioengineer Patrick Hsu, launched December 15, 2021, in partnership with Stanford, UC Berkeley, and UCSF. Arc raised more than $650 million from founding donors (Collison among them) to fund scientists on renewable multi-year (reported as eight-year) terms with unconstrained, curiosity-driven support — an institutional embodiment of the funding ideas surfaced by Fast Grants: fund people rather than narrow projects, on long horizons, with minimal grant-writing burden.

**The questions list and the "fast" page.** Collison's site hosts an open list of questions he finds important and underexplored, including: "Why are certain things getting so much more expensive?" (healthcare, education, construction); "Why do there seem to be more examples of rapidly-completed major projects in the past than the present?"; "Why is GDP growth so weirdly constant?"; "Is Bloom's 'Two Sigma' phenomenon real? If so, what do we do about it?"; "How can we better understand the dynamics of progress in science?"; "What's the successor to the scientific paper and journal?"; "Why isn't China producing top-tier research?"; "What does religion cause?"; and "Why don't we build nice neighborhoods anymore?" His companion "fast" page catalogs ambitious projects completed with surprising speed — the Empire State Building (410 days), Disneyland (366 days), the Pentagon (491 days), the Lockheed P-80 jet (143 days), the Alaska Highway (234 days), the Marinship shipyard (197 days), Apollo 8 (134 days), the Boeing 747 (~930 days), and software examples like the first Unix (≈3 weeks), the JavaScript prototype (10 days), and Git (17 days to public release). He observes that the large physical-infrastructure feats cluster before about 1970, and offers hypotheses that something in American governance, regulation, and institutional complexity changed around the 1960s to slow such projects.

### The substance of his views, the reasoning, and the counterarguments

**On scientific progress slowing.** Collison's central empirical claim is not that science has stopped but that its return per unit of input has fallen dramatically. In his own framing (in interviews about the Nielsen essay), he is "somewhat agnostic" on whether *aggregate* progress is slowing, but confident that progress is "declining very rapidly on a per-person, per-hour, or per-dollar basis," given roughly 10–100x increases in scientific inputs without proportionate increases in breakthroughs. The reasoning chain: inputs (researchers, dollars) are measurable and have grown enormously; outputs (transformative discoveries) appear roughly flat by several proxies; therefore productivity per input has collapsed.

The **strongest counterargument**, which Collison explicitly engages, is the "low-hanging fruit" thesis — most directly associated with Tyler Cowen's 2011 book *The Great Stagnation* and economist Robert Gordon (*The Rise and Fall of American Growth*). On this view, the slowdown is a feature of nature, not institutions: the easy, general-purpose discoveries (electrification, the internal combustion engine, antibiotics, indoor plumbing) were genuinely once-off, and finding new ideas necessarily gets harder as the frontier advances — exactly what Bloom et al. document. If true, declining per-researcher productivity is the unavoidable arithmetic of a maturing search, and no amount of institutional reform reverses it. Collison's response is a hedged both/and: he grants that exhausted opportunities are "a nontrivial part of the story" but insists "these sociological, institutional considerations" also matter, and — crucially — argues that the policy conclusion does not require winning the causal debate. As he puts it, "even if it is only 20% institutional factors, the return to science is so enormous that still it's overwhelmingly worth fixing those." This is his characteristic move: convert an unresolved causal question into a high-expected-value bet on reform.

**On science funding mechanisms.** Collison's critique is that the dominant model — exemplified by NIH R01 grants — has become slow, conservative, project-bound, and biased against young and unconventional researchers. He points to the rising average age of first-time R01 recipients as evidence of deteriorating conditions for early-career scientists, and to the increasing formalization of institutions as something that may disincentivize the rule-breaking and risk-taking that breakthroughs require. He argues that prize and grant committees optimize against "the reputation cost of getting it wrong," producing conservative, consensus choices. His constructive alternative is to fund *people, not projects*, on long horizons, with minimal application friction. His favorite supporting datum is the Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI) "Investigator" model: he cites evidence that HHMI-funded scientists were about 98% more likely to produce work in the top 1% by citations than comparable scientists on conventional grants — which he attributes to HHMI funding individuals rather than pre-specified projects. He also argues by analogy from software, contending that much software innovation "would not have occurred" under centralized, consensus-gated grant systems, and that decentralization permits experiments that no committee would have approved.

The **counterarguments here** come from defenders of peer review and from skeptics of billionaire-directed science. Critics quoted in coverage of Fast Grants (e.g., in *The Chronicle of Philanthropy*) worry that fast, founder-selected funding substitutes the judgment of a small wealthy network for broad scientific peer review, risks privileging connected or fashionable researchers, and may not scale or sustain without the accountability structures of public funding. Economist John Cochrane, broadly sympathetic, has nonetheless framed the harder question of whether and how government should subsidize science at all, and whether private speed comes at the cost of the systematic coverage public agencies provide. Collison's rejoinder is empirical and pluralist rather than total: he does not claim NIH should be abolished, but that the system is "close to optimal" is an unjustified assumption and that the sector under-experiments with its own structures — so more and more-varied funding models should be tried and measured.

**On organizations and institutions.** A recurring Collison theme is that organizational form is causal for output, not a neutral container. The "fast" page is the clearest expression: he treats the disappearance of rapidly built megaprojects as evidence that institutional and regulatory accretion (environmental review, procurement complexity, veto points, interest-group influence) — more than engineering limits — now governs the pace of ambitious undertakings. Arc Institute is his attempt to instantiate the alternative: pooled philanthropic capital, long tenures, freedom from grant-cycle churn, and tight coupling between curiosity-driven and goal-directed work. The implicit tension critics note: this is itself a strong institutional claim being tested by a handful of well-funded, elite-affiliated organizations, and the generalizability from a richly resourced boutique institute to the broader research economy is unproven.

**On economic growth.** Collison treats long-run growth as among the most important variables for human welfare, on the compounding logic that small sustained differences in growth rates produce enormous differences in living standards over decades. His "growth" page curates the scholarship he finds persuasive: Bloom et al. on ideas getting harder to find; Cowen and Gordon on stagnation and the exceptional, possibly non-repeatable 1870–1970 surge; Joel Mokyr's "culture of growth" thesis that early-modern Europe's intellectual norms enabled sustained innovation; and Hsieh–Klenow on the large productivity gains (cited at 30–60% in some developing economies) available purely from better resource allocation. In interview, he has defended the welfare relevance of growth against quality-of-life skeptics by pointing to the strong cross-country correlation between GDP per capita and self-reported wellbeing (citing an R² around 0.68 as "astonishing"), while conceding that loneliness, isolation, and other non-monetary factors are real and matter. The tension here is one he openly holds: he is a growth optimist by conviction and a stagnation worrier by evidence — he wants more growth precisely because he fears the data show it slowing.

**Tensions in his views.** Several are worth naming neutrally. (1) He is simultaneously a near-term techno-optimist (a builder who believes ambitious things can still be done fast) and a diagnostician of decline (per-input productivity is falling, megaprojects have slowed). He reconciles these by locating the problem in institutions rather than in human capability. (2) On causation he is genuinely undecided between "nature" (low-hanging fruit) and "institutions," yet his prescriptions presuppose that institutions are a large, fixable share — a move he justifies on expected-value grounds rather than settled evidence. (3) He champions decentralized, pluralistic, "let many models compete" funding, while his own flagship interventions (Fast Grants, Arc) are relatively centralized, founder/donor-curated, and elite-network-dependent — the very concentration critics flag. (4) He is publicly careful and hedged in his written claims (the Nielsen essay repeatedly declines to overclaim aggregate stagnation), even as the movement he seeded is often invoked for stronger declinist conclusions than his texts assert.

### Debates he engages and where he stands

- **The stagnation debate:** He sides with the "something has slowed" camp and with Cowen/Gordon that the post-1870 surge may be exceptional — but he resists fatalism, arguing reform can recover lost return.
- **Nature vs. institutions as the cause of slowdown:** He refuses to fully pick a side, but argues the institutional share is large enough, and the payoff high enough, to act on.
- **How to fund science:** He is a clear advocate of funding people over projects, long horizons, lower friction, more experimentation, and more decentralized/private funding alongside (not replacing) public agencies. He is critical of the conservatism and bureaucratic load of the NIH model.
- **Whether growth still matters:** He affirms it strongly, against degrowth and wellbeing-skeptic positions, while granting non-material factors are real.
- **Whether "Progress Studies" is legitimate scholarship:** He defends it as a needed applied field; critics (including some historians of science, e.g., voices in *The Conversation* and *Lady Science*) argue it risks reinventing or ignoring the existing history and sociology of science and technology, and smuggles in contestable assumptions about what "progress" is.

Tyler Cowen (collaborator and intellectual interlocutor; *The Great Stagnation*), Michael Nielsen (co-author; metascience), the economists behind "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?" (Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen, Webb), Robert Gordon, Joel Mokyr ("culture of growth"), and Paul Graham / Y Combinator (formative early mentorship and funding).
